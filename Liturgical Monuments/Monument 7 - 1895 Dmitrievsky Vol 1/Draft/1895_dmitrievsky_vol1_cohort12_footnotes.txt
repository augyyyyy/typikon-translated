The description of the Typikon of the Turin Library No. 216 made by Prof. Diehl can be called circumstantial only in a relative sense. Strictly speaking, Prof. Diehl does not describe the copy of the Turin Typikon itself, since, in his opinion, it represents a reproduction (*«qu'un reproduction des livres liturgiques grecs déjà connus»*) of Greek liturgical books already known, and would of itself deserve no attention (*«il ne mériterait aucune attention»*), if the monastics of St. Nicholas of Casole had not made in it certain marginal notes relating specifically to the history of the monastery (*Mélanges*, p. 177). In view of this, Prof. Diehl turns his attention entirely to that aspect of the Typikon which principally elucidates the historical fate of the Monastery of Nicholas of Casole, situated on the southern coast of Italy, on the Plain of Hydruntum (Otranto), near the small town of Otranto. With these aims, of course, he occupied himself in his description with a very circumstantial analysis of the *Hypotyposis* (*Ὑποτύπωσις*) given to the said monastery by its third hegumen (1153–1190), the monk Nicholas, in the year 1161 (*Mélanges*, pp. 183–186). Worthy of note, however, is the following circumstance in the aforementioned analysis of Prof. Diehl. Turin manuscript No. 216 has the *Hypotyposis* of Nicholas of Casole unfinished. Owing to the absence of several folios in the manuscript, it breaks off at chapter 23, so that we, in order to supply the missing portion (pp. 822–823), had to apply through the esteemed professor of Novorossiysk University A. A. Pavlovsky to the Barberini manuscript III No. 69. Whereas Prof. Diehl not only fails to note the said defect in the manuscript, but in the exposition of its contents goes even further and indicates such articles as apparently ought not to be in the *Hypotyposis* or *Kanon on Food and Drink* (*«Κανὼν περὶ βρώσεως καὶ πόσεως»*). "One more thing was prescribed (?) to the monastics," we read in Prof. Diehl: "when one goes out from the monastery, he must in all things maintain fitting modesty—not speak, not raise his eyes, 'at the appearance of women and at other useless objects,' but always walk with prayer and with downcast eyes. If he does not observe these rules, he shall be removed from the community for fourteen days. Upon the hegumen lay the duty to encourage the observance of these severe rules and strictly to exact penalty for the slightest deviations. The most minute details of daily life must not escape him: the cook who burns firewood un-economically must receive a reprimand from him. But the compiler (evidently, of the testament?) did not limit himself to these simple prescriptions; for the authoritative hegumen of a rich monastery he traced out also higher duties. The hegumen lives simply, like his monastics; he does not wear luxurious clothing, does not gather gold, does not acquire personal property; when he goes out from the monastery, he does not ride mules or horses without necessity, but, after the example of Christ, walks on foot; he has servants at his disposal neither for himself nor for the monastery, remembering that man, created in the image of God, must not be enslaved. Especially must he care for the dignity of his monastery. 'Thou shalt not honor, beyond what is proper for the community, persons of noble rank and holders of power, and thou shalt not fear to sacrifice life unto the shedding of blood, in order to preserve the laws and commandments of God. With this magnificent and forceful rule conclude the rules of conduct (?) prescribed for the hegumen'" (pp. 185–186). Whence these "rules of conduct of the hegumen" were taken by Prof. Diehl, we do not know. One might assume that the Turin manuscript was complete when it was used by the said professor, but of this we are doubtful, because the following article, for which the title: *Diataxis of the Proskomide*, etc. (*«Διάταξις τῆς προσκομιδῆς» κ. τ. λ.*, p. 823) we borrowed from the above-indicated Barberini manuscript No. 69, has no beginning in the Turin codex and is not indicated by G. Pasini in his description of the manuscripts of the Turin Library (*Codices ms. biblioth. Taurinens.*, part I, p. 309), which has only the one following after it: *Concerning the Presanctified* (*«Περὶ τῶν προηγιασμένων»*). The omission noted in Pasini is possible only under the condition of the absence of the beginning of the article with the indicated title. One must therefore think that these "magnificent and forceful rules of conduct of the hegumen" were borrowed by Prof. Diehl from another codex that was more complete and in good preservation. Thus, for example, similar prescriptions concerning the conduct of monastics within the monastery and outside it can be seen in the MS of the Barberini Library III No. 102, fol. 146.
