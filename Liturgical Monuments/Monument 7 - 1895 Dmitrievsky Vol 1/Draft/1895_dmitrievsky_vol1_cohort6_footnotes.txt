[^76]: A most curious fact is the following. Sava of Serbia built on Athos his Hilandar Monastery in honor of the Mother of God, whom in the Typikon he almost everywhere terms "Directress" [*nastavnitsa*] (pp. 1, 25, 27, 28, etc.), but in Chapter 12 this title is unexpectedly changed, and the Mother of God is named—"Benefactress" [*blagodatel'nitsa*] (According to the remark of the Life of Symeon and Sava, written by Domentijan, — "Evergetis is in Slavic Benefactress" [*Evergetitsa slavenski blagodetel'nitsa*], fol. 11v). The one who would violate the independence of the Hilandar Monastery, according to the remark of the Typikon, "shall be guilty not only before the divine Body and Blood of the Lord God and our Savior Jesus Christ, and of the Mother of God our Lady the Benefactress" (*οὐ μόνον ἔνοχος ἔσται τῷ θείῳ Σώματι καὶ Αἵματι τοῦ Κυρίου καὶ Θεοῦ καὶ Σωτῆρος ἡμῶν Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ καὶ τῇ Θεομήτορι καὶ Δεσποίνῃ ἡμῶν τῇ Εὐεργέτιδι*), etc. (*Typikon of Sava*, p. 26, cf. p. 631). Evidently, this appellation entered the Hilandar Typikon through an oversight of the translator or redactor from its prototype—the Evergetis Typikon. But the Typikon of St. Sava of Serbia in the edition of Mr. Daničić (*Glasnik* XX), which we use, has been considerably renovated in language, and therefore stands further from the Greek original than, for example, the "Typikon of St. Sava for the Studenica Monastery", published by J. Jireček (*Glasnik* Book XL, 1874, pp. 132–181). The latter was, of course, a copy of the Hilandar Typikon, and in the majority of chapters a verbatim one (*ibid.*, p. 137); yet meanwhile it turns out that it betrays its origin more clearly and frankly than its original—the Hilandar Typikon. In the Studenica Typikon of the Venerable Sava of Serbia, Graecisms of the kind of the following: *po anafore* [according to the anaphora], *metanie* [metania], *felagedenu* (*φαγέδαιναν* [gangrenous ulcer]), *skupyne* (*σκνιφότης* [stinginess]), *krasovolem* [with the krasobolion], etc. (*Glasnik* XL, p. 147ff.) are encountered constantly; the translation is closer to the Greek original of the Evergetis Typikon (cf. the eleventh chapters of both monuments); and the epithet of the Mother of God—"Benefactress" [*blagodetelnitse* or *blagodet'nitse*] can already be found repeatedly (pp. 153, 154, 155, 157).

[^86]: Although the Typikon of the Pantokrator Monastery published by us speaks insistently of Emperor John Komnenos as its ktetor, and acknowledges no role in this matter for his pious spouse Empress Irene, we are nonetheless not disinclined to consider the latter as the founder and even initiator in the matter of building the present monastery. Grounds for this we find first of all in the touching narrative of the Synaxarion, which appointed the commemoration of Empress Irene as founder of the Pantokrator Monastery to be celebrated on August 13 (*Μνήμη τῆς ἀοιδίμου καὶ παμμακαρίστου βασιλίσσης καὶ κτητορίσσης τῆς σεβασμίας μονῆς τοῦ Παντοκράτορος Σωτῆρος Χριστοῦ Εἰρήνης, τῆς διὰ τοῦ ἁγίου καὶ ἀγγελικοῦ σχήματος μετονομασθείσης Ξένης μοναχῆς* [Commemoration of the renowned and all-blessed empress and ktetorissa of the venerable monastery of Christ the Savior Pantokrator, Irene, who through the holy and angelic schema was renamed the nun Xene]), and then also in the Pantokrator Typikon of 1136 itself. Describing the rare soul qualities of this fortunate mother-empress, who gave her spouse eight children—four boys and four girls—the Synaxarion notes in the empress a deep, sincere piety and boundless love for charitable works, the fruit of which was her ardent desire to establish a magnificent monastery, which by its churches and charitable institutions attached to it—xenons [*strannopriimnitsami*] and gerokomeions [*bogadel'niami*]—was to surpass all other Byzantine monasteries (*τὴν βασιλικὴν μονήν, τὴν ἐπονομαζομένην τοῦ Παντοκράτορος, αὐτὴ ἔκτισεν ἡ ἀοίδιμος ἐκ θεмеλίων· ὁμοίως καὶ τοὺς νῦν ὁρωμένους περικαλλεῖς ναούς, καὶ τὰ ξενοδοχεῖα καὶ γηροκομεῖα, τὰ ὁποῖα ὑπερέβησαν τούς τε ἀρχαίους ναοὺς καὶ τὰ ἀρχαῖα ξενοδοχεῖα καὶ γηροκομεῖα, καὶ τὰ νέα, κατὰ τὴν τοпоθεσίαν καὶ ὡραιότητα* [the imperial monastery called of the Pantokrator, the renowned lady built from the foundations; likewise both the exceedingly beautiful temples now seen, and the xenodocheia and gerokomeia, which surpassed both the ancient temples and the ancient xenodocheia and gerokomeia, and the new, in location and beauty]). The chief architect under whose supervision these structures were executed was the architect Nikephoros, personally known to the empress, called in the Synaxarion a "new Bezalel" [*novym Veseleilom*]. After the rough completion of the monastery's buildings, when difficult financial circumstances ensued, the empress resolved to draw into the work of completing the planned structures of this monastery the emperor himself. Once, according to the narrative of the Synaxarion, Empress Irene, taking the emperor by the arm, led him inside the church of the Pantokrator, and falling before him to the ground, bowed her head to the very floor and with tears uttered: "Accept, Sovereign, this church erected by God, under thy protection" (*διὰ τὴν χάριν σου* [for thy favor]). The empress would not rise from the floor until she heard from the lips of the emperor consent to her request. Touched by the tears and fervent entreaty of his spouse, the emperor gave a promise, which he also fulfilled brilliantly, placing in all respects the present monastery among the foremost (*ἐπρώτευε μεταξὺ ὅλων τῶν μοναστηρίων τῆς Κωνσταντινουπόλεως*) in Constantinople (*Synaxaristes ton dodeka menon tou eniautou* by Nicodemus the Hagiorite, Zakynthos 1868, Vol. III, pp. 240–242). The Synaxarion adds that the empress, having died in Bithynia, was buried in the monastery founded by her (*ibid.*, p. 242), or, as our Typikon indicates more precisely, in the burial church in honor of the Archangel Michael (pp. 658, 664, 677, 680). — The tomb of Empress Irene in the Pantokrator Monastery, the repeated and insistent admonitions of Emperor John Komnenos to the monks of this monastery to celebrate the memory of his deceased spouse (p. 681, etc.), and the various possessions—*διάφορα κτήματα*—left to the monastery (p. 698), as our Typikon states, by the late empress—all these are facts eloquently testifying, in our opinion, that she was connected with the monastery not by name alone as the wife of the ktetor, but also by more intimate and vivid personal relations, in the capacity of originator of the being of the given monastery and of that flourishing condition into which it was brought, according to her will, by Emperor John Komnenos.
