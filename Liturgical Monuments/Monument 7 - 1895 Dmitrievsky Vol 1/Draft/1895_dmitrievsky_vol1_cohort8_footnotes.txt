[^102]: This carrying of icons and gonfalons in the processions of the cross of ancient Byzantium was by no means an easy matter. A curious picture of such a procession with the icon of the Mother of God, painted by the hand of the Evangelist Luke, taken from life, although not without embellishments and exaggerations characteristic of a "seasoned" traveler, we find in our pilgrim Stephen of Novgorod. "We went on Tuesday to the Holy Mother of God to venerate the *processional icon*," writes our pilgrim, "for that icon the Evangelist Luke painted, having depicted the Lady Virgin Mother of God herself while she was still alive; *for that icon they carry out every Tuesday*. Astounding indeed to behold how the people and folk assemble from other cities! And that icon is very great, richly encased, and chanters sing before it beautifully, and all the people cry: *Kyrie eleison*! with weeping. *Upon one man's shoulders they set a frame, and he spreads out his arms as if crucified, and likewise they roll back his eyes(?); it is terrible to behold, across the pathway it tosses him hither and thither, very violently it wheels him around, and he remembers not whither the icon carries him*. Then another approaches, and he likewise, then a third and a fourth catch hold, and they all do likewise. And they sing great chanting with the clerks, and the people call out: 'Lord, have mercy!' with weeping. Two deacons hold rhipidia, and others a shrine before the icon. A marvelous sight! Three men mount it upon the shoulders of a single man, and he walks as if unburdened by the will of God" (Sakharov, *Skazaniia russkogo naroda* [Tales of the Russian People], Vol. II, Book VIII, pp. 52–53). Evidently, the ordinary physical exhaustion of the icon-bearers was taken by our pious pilgrim for a manifestation of a special miracle from the icon. Conscious of the heavy labor of the icon-bearers and valuing it, Emperor John Komnenos did not forget them in his Typikon. For each above-mentioned procession of the cross, it was appointed to distribute from the monastery 50 hyperpyra, of which *two hyperpyra each* were given to each of the bearers (*βασταγάριοι* [*bastagarioi*]) and ministers (*λοιποὶ δουλευταὶ τῆς ἁγίας εἰκόνος* [remaining servitors of the holy icon]) of the holy icon (pp. 681–682).

[^103]: Describing the sacred relics preserved at the end of the 12th century "in the imperial golden chambers," Archbishop Antony says, among other things, the following: "And many other holy relics *in the golden chambers* did we kiss, *and the icon of the most pure Mother of God Hodegetria, which the holy Apostle Luke painted, which goes into the city*" (P. Savvaitov, *Puteshestvie arkhiep. Antoniia* [Journey of Archbishop Antony], pp. 33, 94–95). The last emphasized words give us reason to think that the icon of the Hodegetria was constantly located in the palace, and from there was solemnly carried out each time litanies with this icon were celebrated throughout the city to this or that monastery, to this or that church. Archbishop Antony knows of the monastery in honor of the Mother of God Hodegetria, but indicates there only the relics of St. Spyridon, which in that "monastery lie beneath the altar" (*ibid.*, p. 112), and mentions not a word about the presence there of the icon that occupies us. The location of this icon in the palace fully corresponds to the historical information that we possess concerning this icon, both in the period described by us and in subsequent times. Emperor John Komnenos directly calls the icon of the Hodegetria his own possession (*τὴν θείαν εἰκόνα τῆς ὑπεράγνου Δεσποίνης καὶ Θεοτόκου μου Ὁδηγητρίας* [the divine icon of my all-pure Lady and Theotokos, the Hodegetria]) (p. 681), and, according to the testimony of Niketas Choniates, harbored toward it a special reverence and respect throughout his entire life. With this icon he went on campaign against the Scythians, and when "the Roman phalanxes were failing, he, standing before the icon of the Mother of God (*τὴν τῆς Θεομήτορος εἰκόνα*) and gazing upon it with an imploring look, shed tears hotter than the sweat of the warriors" (Nicetas Choniates, *Ioannes Comnenus*, Geneva 1593, p. 17), and received help from it. After the victory over the Perso-Armenians, the emperor ordered a magnificent chariot to be constructed for the triumph, "to adorn it in places even with stones, though not the most expensive," and to harness four beautiful horses, with hair whiter than snow. The tsar [emperor] himself did not mount the chariot, but placed upon it the icon of the Mother of God, since she was the cause of his joy and ecstasy, from which he was beside himself, and to her, as the invincible commander (*ὡς συστρατηγέδι ἀμάχῳ* [as invincible fellow-general]), who together with him commanded the army, he attributed the victories. Appointing the foremost grandees in his presence to lead the horses by the bridle and entrusting to close kin the care of the chariot, he himself walked on foot ahead, carrying the cross (*ibid.*, p. 21). But in our later pilgrims of the 14th–15th centuries, the icon of the Mother of God Hodegetria is indicated already in the monastery of her name (N. Sakharov, *Skazaniia russkogo naroda*, Vol. II, Book VIII, pp. 52, 61, 72, 101). We deem it not superfluous to note here that in these pilgrims the monastery of the Hodegetria is either indicated in proximity to the Pantokrator Monastery, or they record some obscure tradition about the presence in the Pantokrator Monastery of even the icon itself. The monk Ignatius of Smolensk, for example, writes the following concerning the icon of the Hodegetria: "On the 6th [day] we went to the most holy Mother of God Hodegetria, and having bowed down we kissed it, and took *pomirizmo* [perfumed oil] and anointed ourselves with rose-water (*ῥοδοστάγματα* [*rhostagmata* / rose-scented drops]). And we went *to the great honorable Monastery of the Pantokrator*" (*Pravoslavnyi Palestinskii Sbornik*, Vol. IV, Issue III, p. 8). "In the Hodegetria," writes a contemporary of Ignatius of Smolensk, the deacon Alexander, "there is an icon of the holy Mother of God; they carry it out every Tuesday, and it works miracles for whoever comes with faith and receives health. And Luke the Evangelist painted this icon. Once there were iconoclasts(?), and they preserved *this icon in the Pantokrator Monastery in a wall, and having lit a lamp before it, for 60 years it was not extinguished before it*" (N. Sakharov, *Skazaniia russkogo naroda*, Vol. II, Book VIII, p. 72). Evidently, in popular tradition even in later times there were preserved echoes of that spiritual bond which existed between the Monastery of the Pantokrator, the icon of the Mother of God Hodegetria, and the monastery of her name, and which Emperor John Komnenos established in the Typikon analyzed by us.
