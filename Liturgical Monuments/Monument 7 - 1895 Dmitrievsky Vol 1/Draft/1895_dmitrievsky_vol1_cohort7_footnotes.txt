[^93]: If the remains of the buildings of the Pantokrator Monastery in the mosque of "Zeyrek Camii" or "Kilisse Camii" are correctly identified by archaeologists, then they relate closest of all to the Church of the Mother of God the Merciful [*Eleousa*], built by Emperor John Komnenos at the Pantokrator Monastery and adorned with rich mosaics. "The magnificent facade of the church," writes Prof. N. P. Kondakov describing the present mosque, "presents a high wall with 7 blind arches. The door in the central arch, which is somewhat larger in size, leads into the first or outer narthex, now extremely dark; five doors leading from this narthex into the second still preserve their doorframes of variegated marble with delicate and complex molding in the form of colonnettes. The second narthex, even darker, is illuminated through the doors and special lunettes made above the doors. It is evident that the illumination of the first narthex was once much stronger, consequently that the arches of the facade were originally open, and walled up only subsequently, perhaps already under the Turks. Meanwhile, both narthexes were richly adorned, and besides the marble floor, there can still be seen in them portions of marble revetment protruding from under the stucco... The colossal half-length mosaic image of the Savior (in Stephen of Novgorod—tall) in the lunette above the door was destroyed under the Turks, when two semicircular windows were punched through here... Three doors of the inner narthex lead into the present mosque. It occupies one right or southern section—likewise a church—of the former temple. The entire temple consists of three similar sections or churches... An extremely original plan of the temple resulted, covered with four domes, namely: the right and left wings—properly churches—covered each with one dome, and a central smaller church covered with two... The small dimensions of the central church and its very shape indicate that this church played the role of the so-called choir in Western churches. In the right church the dome (without a drum) rests by means of four pendentives upon columns, which in their present plastered form represent Romanesque pillars broken into colonnettes... their capitals have the form of trapezoids, on which it is easy to distinguish chalices with branches in the form of Ionic volutes and surrounded by palmettes... Three arches on the western side open a view onto the gynecaeum or galleries situated above the inner narthex; the arches rest upon slender marble colonnettes. Here too, as in the gynecaeum of SS. Sergius and Bacchus, mosaics are hidden beneath the plaster. But of all the decorations there remain only the mosaic floor and the revetment of the apse with variegated marbles according to the design of Hagia Sophia. Three arches resting upon pillars open widely into the middle church. Having a square plan, it is covered with two domes: one oval dome located along the length of the church, and another oval one covering the apse and located transversely. In the sanctuary two open arches leading into the sanctuary of the prothesis and the vestry [diakonikon], covered with half-domes, are curious. The third church or left wing, in its proportions (it is more spacious than the first) and in the arrangement of the dome upon four broad pillars, is curious in its proximity to ancient Russian structures, which evidently also adopted the type of monastic churches of Byzantium. This church has only one entrance (at the present time?) from the northern side, and the dome, as in the first, illuminates and covers its front part; the sanctuary is formed by two large arches and a semi-domed vault of the apse. Beneath the exposed plaster here frescoes are visible (the church suffered recently from water standing in it) and entablatures of red marble with carved palmettes. The eastern walls of the church are quite characteristic in their masonry and architectural decoration: they recall medieval Roman basilicas just as much as Georgian structures: slender colonnettes here too frame arcades along with variegated molding of cornices, string-courses, etc." (*Vizantiiskie tserkvi i pamiatniki Konstantinopolia*, pp. 212–215). Comparing this description of the mosque of "Zeyrek Camii", made by an eyewitness and learned archaeologist, with the brief description of the Church of the Mother of God Eleousa according to our monument, we see a clear resemblance in the facade of both churches, in the arrangement of the narthexes, in the number of entrance doors (we think that the seventh door on the southern side is now walled up), in the division into three naves, and in their decoration with mosaics, concerning which our Typikon, in describing the Church of the Pantokrator, for some reason remains completely silent. Other architectural and decorative details of the present mosque, described with such circumstantiality by N. P. Kondakov, we do not touch upon, since in our Typikon we possess no data for their comparison. We note only that the existing gynecaeum in the mosque of "Zeyrek Camii", arranged on its western side above the inner narthex, likewise speaks in favor of our supposition. In the Church of the Mother of God the Merciful, where every week people gathered in multitude without distinction of sex for agrypnies [vigils], the need for a gynecaeum was natural; whereas in the Church of the Pantokrator, according to the rule of the monastery, women, even noble ones, were not admitted by any means, and in a gynecaeum, consequently, there was no necessity whatever. The monks stood in the church in stasidia.
