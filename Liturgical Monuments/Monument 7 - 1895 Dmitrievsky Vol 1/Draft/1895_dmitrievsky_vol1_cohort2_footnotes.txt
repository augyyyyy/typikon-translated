[^21]: The late professor of the Moscow Theological Academy, I. D. Mansvetov, drew attention to this article on the relaxation of the fast on Great Saturday, but gave it a somewhat peculiar interpretation: "The exceedingly indulgent rule of the Studite Typikon (according to Mai's *Hypotyposis*), utterly incomprehensible from the standpoint of present practice, concerning the complete (?) relaxation of the fast on Great Saturday found a place for itself also in the complete Studite Typika (?!), e.g., in Synodal manuscript No. 330/380. But one of the persons who used this copy," remarks Mr. Mansvetov, "found such an instruction impossible to observe and erased the scandalous words, whence resulted the following unintelligible reading: 'And on the Great Sabbath let there be pure breads, that is, great loaves, and let them be anointed with oil, and let each of the monks take a half; and let them eat also... (here some word has been erased, but it can be deciphered: *and fish*) and both dishes: cooked grain and herbs with oil (then again certain words are scraped away, but they can be deciphered and it is visible that it was written: *cheese and eggs*); for indeed the fathers on that day did not forbid them. But we, because the Liturgy and meal take place toward evening, in order to come to the readiness, do not issue a command, but submit as an opinion that one should refrain from such things; yet if it seems fitting to the hegumen to serve and eat them, we do not forbid it' [fol. 211]. Thus, according to the ordo, it was permissible on Great Saturday after Vespers to eat cheese, eggs, and fish, but in view of the late hour it proved inconvenient and was left to the discretion of the superior. An echo of this custom, which by that time was already abandoned, is heard in the following remark of Synodal Typikon No. 382/905 concerning food on Great Saturday: 'Non-lenten food and fish we have not been accustomed to eat on that Saturday, for the sake of our new Slavic enlightenment' [fol. 315]. This remark belongs to the Slavic redactor (one must bear in mind: a 15th-century manuscript) of the Studite Typikon and was inserted by him in place of the inconvenient rule on food that was read in the original. To retain this strange article seemed scandalous to him, and he struck it out in the name of the religious feeling of newly enlightened Slavic society." *Mitropolit Kiprian v ego liturgicheskoi deiatelnosti* [Metropolitan Cyprian in His Liturgical Activity], Moscow 1882, pp. 163–164.
