To whom, then, does the final redaction of the Typikon of the Evergetis Monastery belong, and to what time can the definitive editing of the copy published by us be assigned? On the basis of the supplementary chapter in the prologue, we can conclude with certainty that the final redaction of the Evergetis Typikon known to us and its indicated additions appeared here under the immediate and nearest successor of the second hegumen, the Venerable Timothy. In setting forth the rule concerning the joint celebration of the memory of both ktetors, its compiler—who is also the final editor of the Typikon (p. 654)—says that Hegumen Timothy, while still alive, asked and bequeathed to him to honor the memory of his demise (*"while still living, he both foretold and enjoined upon us"* [«ἔτι ζῶν καὶ προεῖπε καὶ ἐπέσκεψε ἡμῖν»]). Thus, if the Evergetis Typikon had preserved for us the name of the third hegumen of the monastery and the year of the demise of his predecessor, we would very easily determine the time when the present Typikon received its current form in the copy of the Athens National Library. But since neither the one nor the other is known to us, we must turn to collateral, secondary data that in one way or another aid in a satisfactory resolution of the question posed, as close to the truth as possible. Of such data we have an adequate amount.

In the copy of the Evergetis Typikon published by us we find (p. 614), among other things, a reference to the Typikon of a monastery unknown to us of a certain Manuel ("the Typikon of the most venerable monastery of Manuel forbids" [*«τὸ τῆς εὐαγεστάτης μονῆς τοῦ Μανουὴλ Τυπικὸν ἀπαγορεύει»*]), in whom, as we suppose, one may see the renowned Byzantine Emperor Manuel (1143–1179) Komnenos [^69], son of Emperor John Komnenos. In another Typikon now known to us, namely...

The entire Typikon of the Evergetis Monastery of the Mother of God, as we have said, consists of two parts: (a) the church Typikon in the proper sense of the word, and (b) the prologue, placed by the binder at the end of the manuscript. In a word, in these chapters are set forth all those rules that were necessary for every well-ordered monastery, and therefore it is completely natural that in this part the present Typikon is similar to the analogous prologues of the Typika known to us of Michael Attaleiates of 1077 and Empress Irene of 1114, with the similarity between these monuments being verbatim in places. As regards the exposition, all advantages are on the side of the Evergetis Typikon, since in it we find greater systematicity and consistency than in the others.

The Synaxarion or Typikon, as the above-cited title of this part of the Evergetis Typikon also shows, embraces the complete circle of the annual church divine services, with a detailed exposition of the order of services for every day of the year. In the manuscript of the Athens University Library now published by us, we consequently obtain a new source for our acquaintance with the liturgical practice of the Great Church in Constantinople, a source standing, as it were, on the boundary between patriarchal Typika and ktetorik Typika, thanks to which it acquires in our eyes an even greater significance and a greater scholarly value.

Besides the Typikon of the Great Church, the compiler of the Evergetis Typikon made use at times of the Typika of other monasteries—for example, the Typikon of the monastery built by Emperor Manuel Komnenos (p. 614), and the Typika of Mount Olympus (p. 443), which were held in great esteem in the East for the sanctity of life of their monastic dwellers and the strictness of their church and monastic rules; in certain cases, for the resolution of perplexities concerning church-liturgical practice, the compiler addressed questions to a certain hierarch Antony (p. 442) ("having also learned accurately through inquiry of the most venerable Antony, who served as bishop thereof" [*«ἔτι μὴν καὶ δι’ ἐρωτήσεως τοῦ ταύτης ἀρχιερατεύσαντος Ἀντωνίου τοῦ ὁσιωτάτου ἀκριβῶς ἀναмаθόντες»*]), who was a contemporary of the compiler of the Typikon, and whom the successor of Hegumen Timothy in the supplementary...
