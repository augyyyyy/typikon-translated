In view of disagreements that might arise among the brotherhood concerning a worthy candidate, according to the emperor's will, the living abbot of the monastery selected three candidates whose names, in the presence of all the monks, were written upon slips of paper, which were then sealed and placed in the *skeuophylakion* [sacristy]. The names of the chosen candidates were known to none of the brethren. Upon the death of the abbot, all the brethren assemble in one place and before them the sealed papers with the names of the candidates are opened. If all the brethren settle their choice upon one person from among these three candidates, then that person is appointed as abbot; but if disagreement arises concerning the identity of the most worthy for the orphaned throne, then all the brethren gather in the main monastery for earnest and tearful prayer to God that He might indicate the most worthy to govern the monastery. In this case the names of the candidates are written upon three slips of paper, which are placed in the sanctuary upon the holy table [altar] beneath its sacred coverings. All the brethren over the course of three days celebrate pannychides or vigils, Vespers, and Liturgies. On the third day, after the Liturgy, an illiterate [^106] monk (*γράμματα μὴ εἰδώς* [knowing no letters]) is sent into the sanctuary, who takes from the holy table one of the three slips of paper, and the name of the candidate read aloud to the entire brotherhood from the paper resolves the brotherhood's dispute. The abbot was the head of the monastery, sovereign master in its administration, and at the same time the spiritual father to whom were accessible all the inner thoughts of his subordinate brethren. Every monk was obliged to reveal all his thoughts and inner movements of the heart to the abbot and to receive from him necessary spiritual healing. It was not forbidden, however, for the brethren to have a spiritual father besides the abbot, but from the midst of their own brotherhood and by all means a venerable elder, experienced in spiritual life and having received the right to confess the brethren through a hierarchal charter (*διὰ τῆς ἀρχιερατικῆς σφραγῖδος* [by hierarchal seal]) (pp. 672–673). But notwithstanding the extensive powers with which the abbot of the Pantokrator Monastery was endowed by the emperor, in matters concerning the monastery he, however, undertook nothing without counsel with the representative elders (p. 696). In their turn, these representative elders likewise had no right to do anything without the will and counsel of the abbot of the monastery (*ibid.*). In exceptional cases that demanded special attention, according to the emperor's rule, the Grand Droungarios Nikephoros and the Protonotarios of Nicomedia Constantine were admitted to this council, and it was brought to the notice of the patriarch through a special notification (p. 700).
