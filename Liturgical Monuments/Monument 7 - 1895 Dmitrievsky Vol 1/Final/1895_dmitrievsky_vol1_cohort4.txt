If we now recall the above-indicated examples of similarity between the *Hypotyposis* published by us and the Typikon of Patriarch Alexius according to the 12th-century manuscript of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 330/380, which very sharply distinguish both recensions of the *Hypotyposis* known to us from one another, both in completeness of composition and even in exposition; if, finally, we direct our attention to the fact that certain articles of the *Hypotyposis* printed by Cardinal Mai are completely omitted in the recension published by us (as, for example, chapters 14 and 15), and in their peculiarities and exposition stand in a rather close relation to the *Diatyposis* of the Venerable Athanasius of Athos (cf. p. 231, note 7 and pp. 249–250), then the connection of this latter with the *Hypotyposis* of Cardinal Mai becomes beyond all doubt for us. And since the *Diatyposis* of the Venerable Athanasius of Athos appeared not long before the demise of this father, which, as Meyer supposes, ensued between the years 997 and 1011 [^54], it is natural to conclude from this that the copy of the *Hypotyposis* that was laid at its foundation appeared much earlier than this time. In other words, we consequently consider the *Hypotyposis* in the recension of Cardinal Mai to be older than the *Hypotyposis* printed by us, which, as we think, in its turn came into the world not later than *the first half of the 11th century*, because all...

Having thus indicated the scholarly significance of both recensions of the *Hypotyposis*, attributed by tradition to the Venerable Theodore the Studite, which are encountered very frequently in manuscripts, it remains for us merely to express regret that during our stay on Sinai we did not make use of the excellent manuscript of the year 1086, No. 401, for the correction or even for a new edition of the *Hypotyposis* in the recension of Cardinal Mai, but limited ourselves only to noting from it some of the most important and most curious variants [^55]. VI. From what has been stated above concerning the relation of the *Hypotyposis* of Theodore the Studite to the *Diatyposis* [^56] of the Venerable Athanasius of Athos, it becomes obvious of itself what important significance this monument possesses in scholarly terms. The necessity of publishing it...

*(Orig. p. XXXII)*

...to the world is, consequently, beyond all doubt. But one can also point out other reasonable motives for the same. The *Diatyposis* of Athanasius of Athos until the very latest time was known to scholarship only in the *Russian translation* [^57] of the late father Archimandrite Antonin, and that with large *omissions* in the text, which depended on the defectiveness of the original from which it had been made. We noted several omissions in the translation at the beginning of the *Diatyposis*, but there are far more of them in that part which describes the divine services of the lavra of the Venerable Athanasius of Athos. For example, here the service on the Saturday before Pentecost is not finished; the service of Vespers on the feast of Pentecost is completely omitted, as are the daily cycle services during the fast of the holy Apostles (pp. 248–249), and so forth. It is not surprising, therefore, that in our time we could not agree [^58] with many of those differences between the *Diatyposis* of Athanasius of Athos and the *Hypotyposis* of the Venerable Theodore the Studite that were pointed out by Prof. Mansvetov in his dissertation: *The Church Ordo*, pp. 66–68.

Having printed in the present *Description* the *Diatyposis* of the Venerable Athanasius of Athos according to a 16th-century Iveron manuscript, as the only one accessible to us, with the addition of variants for certain parts of it taken from manuscripts of the Cairo Patriarchal Library and the library of the Russian Panteleimon Monastery on Mount Athos, when there appeared recently Meyer's book under the title: *Die Haupturkunden für die Geschichte der Athosklöster*, Leipzig 1894, in which its author succeeded in publishing entirely the testament of the Venerable Athanasius of Athos according to codices of the skeuophylakion of the Athos Lavra hitherto inaccessible [^59], we collated our copy with this latter and found very substantial gaps in our own. In view of the serious critical methods that were applied by Mr. Meyer in publishing these most important monuments of 10th-century liturgical literature, and the authority...

VII. The division of "ktetorik Typika" in the proper sense begins with manuscript No. 788 of the Athens National Library with the title: "Synaxarion, with God, that is, the Typikon of the ecclesiastical service of the venerable monastery of the Most Holy Theotokos the Benefactress [Evergetis], beginning from the month of September, containing the entire service until the completion of August; likewise...

=== LEAF p37 ===

*(Orig. p. XXXIV)*

...and of the Holy Great Lent, from the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee until All Saints." As the cited title shows, this manuscript contains within itself a complete Church Ordo with a detailed exposition of the services for every day of the year, that is, according to the Synaxarion and the Lenten and Flowery [Festal] Triodia [Pentecostarion]. In scholarship, such a ktetorik Typikon was known hitherto only in Church Slavonic translation according to the 12th-century manuscript of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 330/380, given by Patriarch Alexius of Constantinople (around 1034) to the monastery founded by him in Constantinople; but in the Orthodox East, the Evergetis Typikon, so far as we know, is for the time being the sole representative of this class of monuments. In Eastern manuscript repositories, to be sure, not a few copies and originals of ktetorik Typika are encountered, but all of them consist of a single part, the so-called prologue, treating of the time of foundation of this or that monastery, of the ktetors and benefactors, of property holdings, of the internal monastic organization of life in them, and so forth. In the Evergetis Typikon being described, this part of the Typikon bears the title: "The Prologue of the Typikon" (*«Ὁ πρόλογος τοῦ Τυπικοῦ»*), or more specifically, "Exposition and rule of life of the monks in the monastery of the Most Holy Theotokos the Benefactress" (*«Ἔκθεσις καὶ ὑποτύπωσις τοῦ βίου τῶν ἐν τῇ μονῇ τῆς ὑπεραγías Θεοτόκου τῆς Εὐεργέτιδος μοναχῶν»*), and occupies a position at its end, and not at the beginning as the inscription says—evidently through a misunderstanding of the binder of the manuscript, who placed this part of the Typikon not in its proper place. As regards the exposition of the peculiarities of church services, and that for an entire year, in the majority of monuments of this class we do not find it; the whole matter in them is for the most part limited to a brief indication to be guided in liturgical practice either by the generally accepted Typika—the Studite and the Jerusalem—or else by the Typika of some well-known monasteries or churches. Thus, for example, in the Typikon of the year 1162 of the Monastery of the Mother of God *tōn Hēliou Bōmōn*, there is indicated "the Studite Synaxarion currently sung" (*«τὸ νῦν ψαλλόμενον Στουδιτικὸν Συναξάριον»*) (p. 728 et al.); in the Typikon of the year 1210 of Nilus, hegumen of the Monastery of the Mother of God of Machairas—to the Typikon of the monastery of the Venerable Sabbas the Sanctified according to Palestine (*κατὰ Παλαιστίνην*) [^60]; in the Typikon of the Auxentios Monastery—to the Jerusalem...

=== LEAF p38 ===

*(Orig. p. XXXV)*

...Typikon (*τὸ ἱεροσολυμικὸν Τυπικόν*) (p. 785); in the Typikon of the Monastery of St. Mamas—to the Typikon of the Evergetis Monastery (p. 728, note 1; p. 758, note 5; p. 760, note 3); in the Typikon of the Pantokrator Monastery for the Church of the Mother of God the Merciful [Eleousa]—to the Typikon of the Great Palace Church or the *Ekklesiastes* (p. 678), and so forth. But the Evergetis and Alexian Typika were not exceptional phenomena in the series of monuments of this kind. Among the ktetorik Typika of ancient Byzantium there are known at present also such in which we find clear indications of the existence of separate, independent expositions of church-liturgical orders practiced in a specific monastery, written by its ktetor specially for it alone. "And all doxologies, both nocturnal and diurnal," writes the dignitary Michael Attaleiates in his Typikon, "I will to take place according to my Typikon, which I composed privately" (*«Τὰς δὲ δοξολογίας πάσας, τάς τε νυκτερινὰς καὶ ἡμερινάς, θέλω γινέσθαι κατὰ τὸ Τυπικόν μου, ὅπερ ἰδίως συνεγραψάμην»*) [^61]. "We will, therefore, that the service with the divine hymns of this venerable monastery proceed," says Emperor John Komnenos in the Typikon of the Pantokrator Monastery, "according to the ecclesiastical Typikon enacted by us" (*«κατὰ τὸ γεγονὸς παρ’ ἡμῶν ἐκκλησιαστικὸν Τυπικόν»*) (p. 657). There is reason to believe that similar Typika or Synaxaria existed in the monastery of Empress Irene [^62], in the Hilandar Athonite Monastery [^63], and elsewhere. Confirmation that there were many such ktetorik liturgical Typika in ancient times in Byzantium is found in the *Taktikon* of Nikon of the Black Mountain, an 11th-century writer, who, when compiling his comparative table of the peculiarities of various Typika, undoubtedly made use of such Typika. Unfortunately, they have not been preserved down to our time and are not encountered in the known manuscript repositories of the Orthodox East. It is completely natural, therefore, that the Evergetis Typikon in the Greek original and the Alexian Typikon in the Slavonic translation, as representatives of this class of monuments of an entire epoch, acquire in our eyes a special value and exceptional interest.

*(Orig. p. XXXVI)*

In our literature concerning the time of foundation of the Monastery of the Mother of God Evergetis and the personality of its ktetor, there exist two opposing opinions. Some (Prof. N. P. Kondakov) call its ktetor Basil the Protasekretis [^64] and assign the time of the monastery's construction to the 10th or the beginning of the 11th century, while others (P. Savvaitov) more correctly consider it to be the hegumen of this monastery, Paul [^65], but do not know either the place where the monastery was situated, nor the time when it was built. Both of these opinions are based on a single source—on the book: *Constantinopolitana Christiana* by Du Cange [^66], who himself does not specify precisely either the time of construction of the Evergetis Monastery, nor the name of its ktetor, and cites extracts from manuscripts confirming both the one and the other opinion. In the complete copy of the Evergetis Typikon now being published to the world according to the manuscript of the Athens University Library, we draw the most authentic information concerning everything that relates to the initial fate of the given monastery. The second chapter of its prologue directly and categorically names as ktetor of the monastery the ever-memorable and most blessed Paul of Evergetis, who, having left fatherland, parents, relatives, and friends, arrived in Constantinople (*«εἰς βασιλίδα ταύτην μεγαλόπολιν»*) and here, in a suburban (*προάστειον*), deserted (*τὸ ἀγρίδιον*) little place that had fallen to him by inheritance (*«ἐκ γονικῆς αὐτῷ κληρονομίας»*), founded a monastery at his own expense in the month of June 6556 = 1048, fourth indiction (pp. 615–616). An indication of the suburban location of the Evergetis Monastery is also found in the second journey of St. Sabbas, Archbishop of Serbia, to Jerusalem (between 1233 and 1237). Upon returning from this journey to Constantinople, the Venerable Sabbas, as is related here, "first having come, bowed before the most bright Benefactress, the Most Pure Theotokos Evergetis, and thence went into the midst of the city to the holy Apostle Andrew" [^67]. Our 12th-century pilgrim,...

=== LEAF p40 ===

*(Orig. p. XXXVII)*

...Archbishop Antony of Novgorod, likewise places this monastery outside the city, not far from the renowned Monastery of the Peribleptos, which was located in a district called "Triantaphyllitsa" (*Τριακονταφύλλου*), in the neighborhood of the Monastery of St. Mokios, situated in the 12th Region of Constantinople, near the Golden Gate: "And thence (that is, from the monastery where lay the relics of St. Stephen the New) the metochion of the Holy Theotokos of Evergetis... and in this monastery lived Sabbas, the Serbian prince, when he came forth from the Holy Mountain" [^68].

The Venerable Paul governed the small brotherhood (*ὀλίγους τινάς*) of his monastery for five years, dying on April 16 in the year 6562 = 1054. Dying, the Venerable Paul transferred his little (*μικρόν*) monastery, by testament (*ἐνδιαθήκως*), to his disciple Timothy, who had come to the monastery founded by him only two months after its opening, that is, in the month of September in 6557 = 1048. To the lot of this second hegumen of the present monastery fell the task of building in it the main catholicon church (*κυριακὸς ναός, μεγάλη ἐκκλησία*) in the name of the Mother of God Evergetis (the Benefactress), the renewal [consecration] of which was celebrated in the monastery on December 29 (p. 369); of expanding and improving the monastic cells; of supplying this church with ecclesiastical appointments; of painting it with frescoes; of seeing to everything that concerned both its internal and external arrangement and prosperity (p. 616). Besides the catholicon church, under the same hegumen there were built a small chapel (*τὸ εὐκτήριον τοῦ Σωτῆρος*) in honor of the Savior (pp. 481–571), a similar chapel in honor of the Apostles (*τὸ εὐκτήριον* or *ναὸς τῶν ἁγίων ἀποστόλων*) (pp. 366, 533), and a church in the brethren's sepulchre (p. 447). It is not in the least surprising, therefore, that Hegumen Timothy, the compiler of the present Typikon, directly declares concerning himself that this little (*μικρόν*) monastery, which had come to him from his renowned predecessor, he brought from obscurity and incompleteness (*«ἀπὸ τοῦ ἀφανοῦς καὶ ἀτελοῦς»*) into a magnificent appearance and made it famous (*«ἐπὶ τὸ μεγαλοπρεπέστατον καὶ διαφανές»*) (p. 616).
