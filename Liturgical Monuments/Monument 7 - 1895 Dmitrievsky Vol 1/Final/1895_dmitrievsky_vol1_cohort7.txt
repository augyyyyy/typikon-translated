In the monastery, according to the Typikon, there are to be no more than 80 monks, of whom 50 constantly (*ἀδιαπάστως*) abide in the church at prayer, while the remainder, distinguished by high qualities of mind and character (*τὴν φρόνησιν πολιοί, σεμνοὶ τὸ ἦθος* [venerable in wisdom, grave in manner]), adorned with virtues, zealous in the fulfillment of their duties, are appointed to various monastic offices. From these persons are chosen the oikonomoi, the ekklisiarches, the skeuophylakes, the chartophylakes, those in charge of the hospital (*νοσοκόμος*) and the hostel (*ξενοδόχος* [xenon-master]), as well as the docheiarioi and granary-keepers (*δοχειάριοι καὶ ὡρειάριοι*). The number of church servitors was comparatively small: two domestikoi, 6 priests, 6 deacons, two canonarchs, two paradomestikoi, and one timekeeper (*ὁ ὡроλόγος*). Moreover, the number of deacons and priests, according to the abbot's wish, could also be increased. Laymen were by no means admitted into the staff of monastic servants. According to the Typikon, "monks alone had the right to inhabit their own paradise (*αὐτοὶ μόνοι τὸ οἰκεῖον παράδεισον κατοικήσουσι*), to cultivate and till it." Counted among the staff of servants were assistants to the ekklisiarchai (*παραεκκλησιάρχαι*), under-oikonomoi (*παροικονόμοι*), bakers, gardeners, cooks, etc. Four persons from the servant staff (*ἀπὸ τῆς τῶν δουλευτῶν μοίρας*) were appointed to the most menial tasks (*πρὸς τὰς ἐσχάτας διακονίας*), or, as the compiler of the Typikon corrects himself, "to the first and godlike" (*τὰς πρώτας καὶ ἰσοθέους*), namely: to wash the linen and outer garments for the brethren, to prepare baths (*τὰ σώματα ῥύψουσι τῷ λουτρῷ*), to minister unto the sick, and to wash rolling pins, dishes, and jars (*ξέστας καὶ πίνακας καὶ χύτρας*) (p. 671; cf. p. 696).

The two distinct churches of one and the same monastery not only had separate rolls of clerics, but also completely different church typika (*ἐκκλησιαστικὸν Τυπικόν*). For the monastery, according to the custom of other ktetors, the emperor composed (*κατὰ τὸ γεγονὸς παρ’ ἡμῶν ἐκκλησιαστικὸν Τυπικόν* [according to the church Typikon produced by us]) his own Typikon (pp. 657, 664), the detailed exposition of which remains unknown to us, and enacted that it be followed in liturgical practice; whereas upon the clergy of the Church of the Mother of God the Merciful he enjoins the obligation, when celebrating divine services, to adhere, after the model of the great church in the palace, to the Order of the Hagiopolite. "The Order of the Hagiopolite ought to be chanted," writes the emperor in the Typikon, "according to the model of the great church in the palace" (*«Ὀφείλει ψάλλεσθαι ἡ ἀκολουθία τοῦ Ἁγιοπολίτου, κατὰ τὸν τύπον τοῦ ἐν τῷ παλατίῳ μεγάλου ναοῦ»*) (p. 678). On the other hand, from this we now obtain a clear and distinct notion of what the *"Akolouthia tou Hagiopolitou"* [^100] (*Ἀκολουθία τοῦ Ἁγιοπολίτου* [Order of the Hagiopolite]), or *"Akolouthia kata ten paradosin tou Hagiopolitou"* (*Ἀκολουθία κατὰ τὴν παράδοσιν τοῦ Ἁγιοπολίτου* [Order According to the Tradition of the Hagiopolite]), or, finally, simply *"Ho Hagiopolites"* (*Ὁ Ἁγιοπολίτης* [The Hagiopolite]) is, excerpts from which we frequently encounter in various liturgical books of ancient Byzantium. Several such excerpts for an example we cited in the footnotes to the Typikon of the Great Church, published by us from the Patmos manuscript (p. 58, note 1; p. 59, note 1, etc.). Now it is beyond doubt that by this name was designated the court Church Typikon, practiced also in certain imperial monasteries, as for example in the Pantokrator and, in all probability, in others.

The liturgical details of the Pantokrator Typikon are not devoid of interest, but, unfortunately, they chiefly concern the daily cycle of divine services. Remarks concerning the annual festal cycle in it are quite meager.

The monastic day in ordinary times began at midnight, when the brethren awakened from sleep for the morning service [Matins], but on feast days the service began even earlier, in the fifth hour of the night. The awakener (*ὁ ἀφυπνιστής*) with the exclamation: **"Bless and pray for me, father,"** asked the blessing of the abbot and then struck the great assembly semantron (*μέγα συνακτήριον*). The brethren at the sound gathered into the narthex of the church for the Midnight Office, during which the **"Blameless"** [Ps. 118] were sung, divided into three staseis, wherein, after each stasis, they pronounced the Trisagion and two midnight troparia with a theotokion (p. 658).

Upon the conclusion of the Midnight Office, from the narthex the brethren proceeded into the church, where each, having prayed before the Royal Doors, took his appointed place assigned to him by the ekklisiarches. To converse in church, or to begin chanting or reading...

=== LEAF p69 ===

*(Orig. p. LXVI)*

...before the leader on the kliros, or to instruct him, even if one knew the order better, was strictly forbidden. In extreme cases questions were permitted only during the kathismata, but with observance of due silence (p. 660).

The priest on duty, stopping before the sanctuary, receives the censer from the ekklisiarches and, having censed thrice before the chancel barrier (*ἔμπροσθεν τῶν κιγκλίδων*), withdraws into the sanctuary and censes thrice each side of the holy table [altar]. Upon exiting from the sanctuary, the priest, preceded by a reader with bared head and with a lamp in hand, proceeded into the Church of the Archistrategos Michael unto the imperial tombs, then censed the icon of the Pantokrator, the other icons and holy places in the church, and all the brethren standing in the stasidia. During this censing, during the lifetime of the emperor, the monks were obliged to chant for him the Trisagion and Psalms 19 and 100, as also now, and the troparia: **"Save, O Lord"** [Troparion of the Cross], **"Thou Who wast lifted up upon the Cross"**, and the theotokion: **"O speedy protection, anticipate us before we are enslaved"**, and 15 times **"Lord, have mercy"**. After the death of the emperor, the reading of the 19th Psalm alone and the indicated troparia was retained for the living emperor, while for the deceased the monks were obliged to chant another Trisagion, Psalm 6, the troparion: **"With the saints give rest"** with the theotokion: **"Thee, a wall and a haven"**, and the prayer: **"Remember, O Lord, our deceased Orthodox sovereigns and ktetors"**. After the conclusion of the censing of the church, the priest took his stand before the Royal Doors, censed them, pronouncing the exclamation: **"Glory to the holy [and consubstantial, and life-creating, and undivided Trinity]"**, and the Six Psalms [Hexapsalmos] were read in the midst of the church. The brethren without noise quietly repeated the words of the psalms being read. After the Six Psalms they chanted either **"God is the Lord"** or **"Alleluia"**, and then the kathismata were read (pp. 658–659). The hypakoe, as well as the other hymns, the chanters sang always before the Royal Doors (p. 660). The First Hour was joined to Matins.

The Third and Sixth Hours preceded the Liturgy, while the Ninth was celebrated after it (p. 664).

For the Liturgy [^101] prosphoras of pure flour (*σεμιδάλεως* [fine flour / semidalis]) of 4 exagia weight were prepared for each day: two larger ones—in...

*(Orig. p. LXVII)*

...honor of the Lord and the Mother of God, a third in honor of the saint of the day, and four smaller ones for the commemoration of the emperor's father, grandmother, mother, and spouse. From this same flour bread was baked also for the abbot. The prosphoras, cut into portions [antidoron], were distributed to the monks. On Saturdays, in addition to this, there were prepared from wheat flour (*ἐξ ἀλεύρου*) also other prosphoras for the commemoration of the chief members of the imperial house, both living and deceased, and a separate prosphora for the grandson Alexios, son of the Sebastokrator Isaac (pp. 662–663). For the same purpose on Saturdays three dishes of kolyvo were prepared. The prosphoras and kolyvo for the commemoration of the persons of the imperial family constituted an indispensable accompaniment of the liturgies also on the days of the annual commemorations: on Meatfare Saturday, on Cheesefare Saturday, and on Saturday of Pentecost (p. 663). The day of monastic life concluded with Vespers and Compline, between which, according to the Typikon of the monastery, supper was appointed (pp. 667–668).

It goes without saying that for us of far greater interest in scholarly terms must be those remarks of the present Typikon which in one way or another concern the annual festal cycle of divine services, and in particular the divine services celebrated in the churches of the Mother of God and of the Holy Archangel Michael according to the typikon of the main church in the imperial palace. Such remarks, to our deep regret, are very few, and in their exposition they are distinguished by brevity and even fragmentariness. But this prompts us to value all the higher the worth and significance of these data for the history of Byzantine church ritual. And so, what then does the Pantokrator Typikon provide us in this regard?
