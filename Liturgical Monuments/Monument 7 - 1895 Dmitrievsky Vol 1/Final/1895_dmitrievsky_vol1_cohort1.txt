## Author's Preface (*Предисловіе*)

*(Orig. p. I)*

The Church Ordo or Typikon, which in ancient times among us was termed *Tserkovne Oko* (lit. 'Eye of the Church', *«Око церковное»*), occupies the preeminent place within the cycle of liturgical books of the Orthodox Church. Its historical destiny is bound up in a close, indissoluble connection with the history of Orthodox liturgical worship in general, and for this reason it is not in the least surprising that the history of the Church Ordo has from antiquity served as a subject of scholarly research for Western investigators and for our Russian scholars alike. Apart from the well-known dissertation: *De libris et rebus ecclesiasticis Graecorum dissertationes*, Paris 1646, pp. 3–16, by the renowned 16th-century scholar Leo Allatius, who devoted a brief sketch to this book, we also possess in the West a specialized monograph on this subject, belonging to the learned librarian and hieromonk of the Grottaferrata Monastery Theodore Toscani under the title: *Ad Typica Graecorum ac praesertim ad typicum cryptoferratense S. Bartholomaei abbatis animadversiones*, Rome 1864. In our Russian literature, there appeared in the most recent time on the very same question the extensive work of the late talented professor of the Moscow Theological Academy I. D. Mansvetov: *The Church Ordo (Typikon), its Formation and Destiny in the Greek and Russian Church* (*«Церковный уставъ (Типикъ) его образованіе и судьба въ греческой и русской церкви»*), Moscow 1885. In addition, we possess no small number of brief journal articles, notes, and sketches that in one way or another touch upon the historical destiny of our Church Ordo, proceeding from the pen of prominent representatives of our scholarly thought, such as, for instance, Bishop Porphyrius Uspensky, Archimandrite Antonin, Archbishop Sergius of Vladimir (author of *The Menologion of the East* [*«Мѣсяцесловъ востока»*], Moscow 1876), and others; yet of a complete, systematic, and scientifically elaborated history of this liturgical book based on a comprehensive study of primary sources—we do not even now possess.

Leo Allatius intended in his book, which embraces the history of all the liturgical books of the Greek Church and certain other ecclesiastical subjects, to provide only a general sketch, without entering...

=== LEAF p5 ===

*(Orig. p. II)*

...into detailed examinations of all the questions touching upon the destiny of the Greek Church Ordo, for which, the truth must be told, he was scarcely fully prepared. The subsequent Western scholar, Father Toscani, was in comparatively better circumstances; however, as the very title of his work demonstrates, he set as his primary objective to familiarize readers with the Typikon of St. Bartholomew, the founder of the Grottaferrata Monastery, and speaks of the Greek Typika only to the extent that it was necessary for him in order to indicate the connection of the Typikon of St. Bartholomew with them. One cannot in any way demand a detailed history of the Church Ordo from Bishop Porphyrius, Archimandrite Antonin, Archbishop Sergius, and others, who touched upon the given question only incidentally and in passing. Finally, Professor I. D. Mansvetov also failed to provide such a history of the Church Ordo, from whom, judging by the title of his book, one might most of all have expected it. The reason, as the author candidly confesses in his "preface," lies in the fact that he began to write his history with merely "a dozen Greek copies" of the Typikon from the Moscow book depositories, and moreover, it must be added, all these copies belonged to one and the same recension. Professor Mansvetov "remained unaware of the materials for the history of the Greek Ordo in its most ancient epoch—in the transitional period from brief notes to the formation of full written expositions thereof." But in reality, much also remained unknown to the late professor about which he did not even suspect in his time.

This is why we, when departing on a foreign research mission authorized by the supreme authorities in the 1887–88 academic year, and subsequently, during repeated excursions through the East and West on our own initiative, set for ourselves two tasks with respect to our Church Ordo: 1) to describe and study as fully and thoroughly as possible those Greek manuscripts that in one way or another relate to the Church Ordo and elucidate for us the historical past and current composition of this book, with primary attention to those manuscripts that concern its history in the most ancient epoch of the Christian Church, when our Church Ordo was only just being forged into a code of rules regulating the order...

=== LEAF p6 ===

*(Orig. p. III)*

...of Orthodox worship, and when written records of Ordines or Typika in the present-day sense of this word did not yet exist; 2) to verify by personal inspection all those manuscripts and data for the history of the Church Ordo that had been published and brought into God's light from the book depositories of the East and West by previous travelers and scholars, in order to be able to judge fully independently and without prejudice both of the monuments themselves and of their scholarly value for the history of this liturgical book. The results in all respects, we state without self-delusion, surpassed our expectations. There accumulated in our hands, in this manner, much new, highly valuable, and curious material for the history of our Church Ordo, which it is at present utterly impossible to enclose within the bounds of a single book, even an extensive one. In view of this, we resolved to postpone the publication of the extensive materials relating to the historical destiny of the Jerusalem Typikon (linked in its origin with the lavra of St. Sabbas the Sanctified) and the Holy Mountain Athonite Typikon to the second part of this volume, and to dedicate the present first part exclusively to the materials of two divisions from the most ancient history of the Church Typikon: *the monuments of patriarchal rubrics and ktetorik monastic Typika*. The following reasons, in our view entirely valid, served to move us to grant an independent and prominent place in the edition we have undertaken to the materials of these two divisions.

Above we noted that to our predecessors "the materials for the history of the Greek Ordo in its most ancient epoch remained unknown—in the transitional period from brief notes to the formation of full written expositions thereof." The monuments that we now publish in the present first part of our *Description* were sought out and studied by us primarily with the aim of filling this essential deficiency. Certain of these monuments, such as, for instance, the Patmos manuscript of the Typikon of the Great Church, the Athens manuscript of the Evergetis Typikon, and others, contain an integral systematic exposition of the divine services of the Byzantine Church for the entire year, and precisely in its most ancient and most important...

=== LEAF p7 ===

*(Orig. p. IV)*

...epoch of formation. Consequently, the scientific liturgical importance of the given materials is beyond all doubt. This is on the one hand. On the other hand, both the monuments of patriarchal rubrics—or the monuments of the ordo of the Great Church of Constantinople [^1] in particular, whether these be complete manuscripts (such as, for example, the Patmos manuscript No. 266 published by us), or brief extracts from the Gospels, Apostolos, Prophetologia, and other liturgical books of the most ancient epoch (such as, for example, the notes and additions to the Patmos manuscript published by us)—and likewise the ktetorik Byzantine Typika up to the 13th century inclusive, possess, beyond their specialized liturgical significance, an immense scientific interest in general. In monuments of this kind we discover extensive historical material elucidating for us the fortunes of very many of the most ancient and renowned monasteries, temples, and other monumental structures, chiefly of Constantinople and its environs, concerning which either no information whatever existed in historical literature, or else this information was fragmentary and inaccurate. Here, furthermore, we draw diverse data on hagiology, topography, archaeology, and the history of culture and religious life in ancient Byzantium. This is the reason why the majority of the monuments are printed by us almost in their entirety, with very insignificant omissions, which will be noted in their proper place below. The important scientific significance of the present monuments in this respect was recognized long ago, and their publication was undertaken by such scholars of the West, the East, and our own Russian scholars, as *Montfaucon* (the Typikon of Empress Irene of 1114), *Sathas* (the Ordo of 1077 of the Byzantine grandee Michael Attaleiates), Professors *Miklosich and Müller* (*Acta et diplomata monasteriorum et ecclesiarum orientis*, vol. The liturgical monuments now published by us, appearing in print for the first time, not only stand no lower in interest than those already known to scholarship, but in the majority significantly surpass the latter (such as, for instance, the Pantokrator and Evergetis Typika, and others) and, consequently, fully justify our approach to them in the present case.

Let us now communicate concerning each of the monuments that constitute the content of the present first part of our *Description* the bibliographical and other necessary information, in order to present more vividly to our readers the scientific significance of each of them individually.

I. The first division of our *Description* opens with the publication of the *Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople*, extracted by us from a parchment manuscript of the 10th century that has been preserved in the Patmos Library from a very ancient period [^2], but which became known to us only after the appearance of the book of the late I. Sakkelion, *Patmiake Bibliotheke* (*Πατμιακὴ βιβλιοθήκη*, Athens 1890), who described it very briefly (p. 136). To the present monument we have assigned the first place because hitherto there was no complete Ordo of the Great Church known to scholarship, and all information concerning the peculiarities of this practice we drew partly from the book *De ceremoniis aulae Byzanti-...

=== LEAF p9 ===

*(Orig. p. VI)*

...nae*, attributed to Emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus, partly from the writings of the 14th-century liturgist Symeon of Thessalonica, and partly, finally, from the *Taktikon* of Nikon of the Black Mountain, an 11th-century writer. Only in the most recent time have our scholars turned for material of this kind to the synaxaria attached to Apostolos and Gospel lectionaries [^3], written in Byzantium in the most ancient era. It is self-evident that the manuscript of the Patmos Library, as the firstborn of its kind, deserves the full attention of those who are interested for whatever reason in the state of liturgical worship in the ancient Byzantine Church. In it we possess a sequential—though brief and in places fragmentary—exposition of the services of the entire church year, that is, according to the Menaia and the Lenten and Flowery Triodia. The scientific interest of this highly precious and curious monument in other respects is presented with sufficient fullness and thoroughness by Professor N. Th. Krasnoseltsev of Novorossiysk University, to whom the contents of the present manuscript became known chiefly through a copy prepared for the present edition, in a solid specialized monograph under the title: *The Typikon of St. Sophia in Constantinople of the 9th Century* (*«Типикъ св. Софіи въ Константинополѣ IX в.»*), Odessa 1892.

The brevity of exposition of the present Typikon accounts for the circumstance that, in editing it, we resolved (we regret that this thought did not occur to us from the very beginning) to supplement its content in the footnotes, and subsequently in the "supplementary" notes, with that information from the practice of the Great Church of Constantinople which we extracted from Eastern manuscripts—namely, Apostolos, Gospels, Prophetologia, and others. Thanks to these "supplementary" data, the practice of the Great Church can be studied with greater thoroughness, and the data themselves are presented to the readers in an unscattered form, given their occurrence in manuscripts of the most diverse...

=== LEAF p10 ===

*(Orig. p. VII)*

...content, yet arranged according to the order of the church year. Certain of such additions have been inserted with an explanatory note directly into the text of the monument, since they successfully supply the extant defects of the given manuscript. As such an addition we regard the insertion in the description of the rite of the Elevation of the Precious Cross in the service of September 14, borrowed by us from a parchment Apostolos of the 10th–11th century, No. 12 of the Lavra of St. Athanasius on Mount Athos (p. 5).

The Patmos manuscript No. 266, containing within itself the Ordo of the Great Church of Constantinople, is written in the very careless hand of an illiterate scribe (*ὑπ’ ἀмаθοῦς γραφέως*), as the late I. Sakkelion expressed it, and is therefore overfilled with a multitude of orthographical errors that severely impede the reading of its text. Hence the methods that we were forced to adopt when copying and publishing the present manuscript become understandable. Wishing to lighten our labor during transcription, we abbreviated the texts of Holy Scripture that are well known at present and stand in the manuscript as prokeimena or alleluia verses, and we did not transcribe the opening words of the apostolic and gospel readings, nor the verses of the communion hymn, if these readings and communion hymns proved identical to those indicated at present in the Typikon currently in force. Since, by reason of the scribe's illiteracy, proper names were distorted most of all—that is, the names of saints, emperors, governors, and the names of cities, localities, rivers, and the like—therefore, in order to make the text of the published manuscript fully intelligible to readers, we resolved to adhere to the spelling of these names found in a monument contemporary with the given manuscript, in the well-known *Menologium Graecorum Basilii Porphyrogeniti imperatoris* (Migne, *Patrologia Graeca*, vol. 117). In certain cases we had recourse for the same purpose to the *Μηноλόγιον τῶν εὐαγγελίων ἑορταστικόν, sive Kalendarium ecclesiae Constantinopolitanae Steph. Anton. Morcelli*, Rome 1788, in two volumes, and sometimes even to the book of Nicodemus the Hagiorite: *Συναξαριστὴς τῶν δώδεκα μηνῶν τοῦ ἐνιαυτοῦ*, publ. in Zakynthos 1868, in three volumes. But even with all these aids, it must be candidly confessed, by no means all the orthographical and paleographical difficulties of the manuscript being described were overcome by us.
