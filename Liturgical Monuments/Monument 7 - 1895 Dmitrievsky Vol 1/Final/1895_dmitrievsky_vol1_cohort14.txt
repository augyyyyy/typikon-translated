The Grottaferrata or Cryptaferrata Monastery of the Mother of God is located in Tusculanum near Frascati, at no great distance from Rome (not more than 30 versts). Its founder is considered to be the monk Nilus, descended from the noble and wealthy ancient Byzantine family of Melenas (*Malenarum seu Malai-...*)

*(Orig. p. CXXXI)*

...*norum*), which had migrated together with other emigrants from Byzantium to Rossano, the wealthiest city of Calabria. He was born around the year 910 and lived until the age of 30 in his native city, dedicating his time to learning, when he finally resolved to enter the Monastery of St. Mercurius not far from Rossano. Loving solitude, however, he departed this monastery and founded his own monastery in honor of the Archangel Michael in the neighboring mountains, wherein he lived for a full 17 years. Upon its destruction by the Saracens, St. Nilus wandered for a long time among other foreign monasteries, until he finally resolved, together with two of his disciples and future—as is supposed—successors in the hegumenate at the Grottaferrata Monastery, Paul and Bartholomew the Younger, to set out for Tusculanum. Here he first settled in the Greek Monastery of St. Agatha, but later, having requested as a gift from Gregory, the lord of Tusculanum, who had grown very fond of St. Nilus, a plot of land near the hamlet of Cryptaferrata (which gave its name also to the founded monastery), he founded his own monastery, which, however, he did not succeed in completing, as he reposed on September 25, 1004 (= 6513). This monastery was built up and endowed with property holdings providing means for the maintenance of the monks living in it by the disciples of St. Nilus, Hegumens Paul and Bartholomew the Younger. Especially much labor was expended in this regard by the latter, likewise a native of the city of Rossano, a member also of a noble Greek family. He constructed in the monastery in the year 1025 (p. 905) a splendid church in honor of the Mother of God, which exists with certain alterations even unto the present time [^189].

Although in the Life of this father, written by his disciples, there is, in truth, not even a faint hint of this fact, yet we have no sufficient grounds to doubt that the original Typikon of the Theotokos Monastery of Grottaferrata came from the hands of the third hegumen of this monastery. On the contrary, as it seems to us, it was entirely natural for a caring hegumen, who completed the work of his renowned predecessor, Hegumen Nilus, by the creation of an excellent church in the monastery in honor of the Mother of God, to take care also for a more solid internal organization of this monastery and the regulation of liturgical orders within it by compiling, according to the custom of ancient Byzantine ktetors, a special ktetorik Typikon. Th. Toscani inclines toward the very same thought and in his study on Western typika presents other considerations in its favor [^190]. Here only that fact noted in the inscription might seem somewhat incomprehensible and even strange: that the ktetorik Bartholomew Typikon, by decree of Blasius II, hegumen of the same monastery, in the year 1300 was *renewed* (*ἀνακαινίσθη*), and not *transcribed* (*ἀπεγράφη*) word for word, which would have been more natural; but even this fact, strange at first glance, becomes completely understandable upon closer acquaintance with the historical circumstances of the life of this monastery.

It is beyond all doubt that the first ktetor of the Grottaferrata Monastery, St. Nilus, as a native of Southern Italy and a pupil of the local Greek monasteries, introduced into the monastery founded by him, both in monastic life and in church liturgical practice, the very same orders to which he had been accustomed from childhood in his homeland, and strictly observed them in his monastery. In favor of this speaks the eloquent fact from the life of St. Nilus, that having settled in Tusculanum and categorically declared: **"This is my rest unto ages of ages: neither shall anyone lead me away hence"** (*«Haec requies mea est in saeculum saeculi: neque hinc futurum est, ut quisquam me deducat»*), notwithstanding the urgent persuasions of his friends and noble Roman patricians, who were very favorably disposed...

For this submissive servant of the Roman pontiff, very naturally, nothing else remained to do than to reform the internal monastic life and liturgical structure in the monastery governed by him according to the customs of the Western Church.

Regarding the food of the monks, we find in this Typikon not a few directions that run completely contrary to the Eastern and the remaining Western Typika. Thus, for example, on the feast of the Elevation of the Cross of the Lord the table appointed is the same as for the feast of the Nativity of the Theotokos, and this means that the monks, besides two dishes of vegetables, ate *fresh cheese, eggs, and fish* (*τυρὸν χλωρὸν καὶ ὠὰ καὶ ἰχθύας*) and drank *good wine* (*ὑποκεраσίαν καλήν*) (p. 904); during the Fast of the Holy Apostle Philip in the morning the monks ate greens with cheese (*δύο λάχανα μετὰ τυρίου*) and well-mashed beans, and in the evening—eggs with pepper, fresh cheese, and fried eggs (*ὠὰ μετὰ πιπερίου καὶ τυρὸν χλωрὸν καὶ ὠὰ τυγανισμένα*) (p. 905); on Great Thursday they were dispensed for fish (p. 910), etc.

On the contrary, we personally arrive at a conclusion completely the reverse, namely, that the Typikon of the Cryptaferrata Monastery that issued from under the pen of Blasius II completely altered its character (*substantiam*) and received a different form. The Blasian Typikon of the year 1300 is *Western* in the narrow, special sense of this word, or, more correctly, *Greek-Uniate*. The submissive "vassal" of the Roman pontiff knows no other model and no other traditions than the Roman Church, and only on the basis of its practice and customs strives to reorganize both the liturgical order and the monastic constitution of life in the monastery governed by him. Of the traditions that guided the predecessors of Blasius II and the Greek monks of other Italian monasteries—traditions whose origin was connected with the most renowned Byzantine typika: of the Great Church, the Studite, the Jerusalem, etc.—he seems not even to suspect, passing them over in complete silence in his Typikon. If he leaves in the Typikon certain services without substantial alterations, as, for example, for the Twelve Great Feasts and the Triodion cycle, this he does out of necessity, since in the Latin Church different services had been composed for these days, completely distinct from the Greek ones, and to introduce them into the practice of the Grottaferrata Monastery would have meant breaking definitively the connection with the orders that had been practiced in the monastery for almost two hundred years.
