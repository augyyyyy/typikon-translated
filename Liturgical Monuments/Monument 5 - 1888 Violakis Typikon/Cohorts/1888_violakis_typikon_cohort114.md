The classical Greco-Roman tunic was originally sleeveless, as sleeves were regarded as barbarian apparel typical of northern climates. Tunics did not incorporate sleeves until the third century, when imperial portraits depict Roman emperors returning from eastern campaigns clad in sleeved tunics (*tunica manicata*).

Because of its loose draping, the mantle impeded vigorous physical movement and was routinely removed during physical exertion. The himation never entered ecclesiastical vesture, as its loose folds rendered it impractical for the active manual duties of the liturgical ministers. The Holy Gospels record that Jesus was led to His Crucifixion clad in a himation over an inner tunic. The Roman soldiers partitioned His himation into four parts, but because His tunic was woven in one continuous piece from top to bottom, they cast lots for it (John 19:23–24). The seamless tunic of Christ was sleeveless and reached to the ankles. While patristic authors reference seamless weaving techniques, the classical methods for producing seamless garments were largely lost following the fall of the Western Roman Empire. ## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Theos Kyrios Concluded – Theotokarion – Theotokion – Theotokos – Theia Akolouthia – Throne Part 1) [Demos, p. 1125 / TD-146]

**"God is the Lord"** (*Θεὸς Κύριος* / Slav. *Bog Gospod’* / Psalm 117:27, 26 [MT 118:27, 26]); the solemn proclamation and verses chanted at Matins (**Orthros**) immediately following the Great Litany and preceding the Dismissal Troparia.

**THEOTOKARION**, pl. **THEOTOKARIA** (*Θεοτοκάριον, τό*, pl. *Θεοτοκάρια, τά*; Slav. *Bohorodychnyk* / *Богородичникъ*): A liturgical hymnographic collection containing canons and suites of troparia composed in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos, organized according to the eight Tones and appointed for devotion throughout the ecclesiastical year (such as the *Theotokarion* compiled by Saint Nicodemus the Hagiorite).

**THEOTOKION**, pl. **THEOTOKIA** (*Θεοτοκίον, τό*, pl. *Θεοτοκία, τά*; Slav. *bohorodychnyj* / *богородиченъ*): A hymn (troparion) composed in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos, the Mother of God (lit. "Birth-giver of God"). The Theotokion customarily concludes a larger liturgical cycle of hymnography, such as the troparia at the conclusion of Vespers, the stichira at **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*), or the Aposticha. A series of stichira or troparia concludes with a Theotokion following the Little Doxology: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen"** (*Δόξα Πατρί, καὶ Υἱῷ, καὶ Ἁγίῳ Πνεύματι· καὶ νῦν, καὶ ἀεί, καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων. Ἀμήν*). The Theotokion is chanted in the same Tone as the preceding Doxastikon. At Saturday Great Vespers, however, the Theotokion of the Entrance is the solemn **Dogmatikon** (*Δογματικόν* / Slav. *Dogmatik* / *Догматикъ*) of the Tone of the week, regardless of the Tone of the Doxastikon. The same Theotokion is repeated at Vespers on the following Friday. In Slavic usage, this entrance Theotokion is designated the Dogmatikon, whereas in Byzantine practice the term *Dogmaticon* is likewise applied to the corresponding Theotokion at Small Vespers.

**THEOTOKOS** (*Θεοτόκος, ἡ*; Slav. *Bohorodytsya* / *Богородиця*; lit. "Birth-giver of God"): The supreme dogmatic title of the Virgin Mary, solemnly affirmed by the Universal Church at the Third Ecumenical Council of Ephesus in 431 A.D. The Council defined that Mary's Son, Jesus Christ, is simultaneously true God and true Man, and that His divine and human natures are united hypostatically in the one Divine Person of the Son, the Second Person of the Holy Trinity.

**THEIA AKOLOUTHIA** (*θεία ἀκολουθία, ἡ*; Slav. *bozhestvennoye posliduvanie* / *божественное послѣдованіе*): A sacred liturgical office or divine service.

**THRONE** (*θρόνος, ὁ*; Slav. *prestol* / *престолъ* or *kafedra* / *каѳедра*): In Eastern Orthodox and Byzantine liturgical tradition, the episcopal cathedra or seat of the presiding hierarch, and by extension the Holy Table (**Altar** / *῾Αγία Τράπεζα*) whereon the Holy Sacrifice of the Eucharist is offered.[^690]
