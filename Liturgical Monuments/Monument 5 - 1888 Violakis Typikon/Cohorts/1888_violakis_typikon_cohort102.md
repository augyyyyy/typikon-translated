The Mandylion disappeared again after the Persians conquered Edessa in 609. An Arab legend, related to historian Andrew Palmer when he visited Urfa (Edessa) in 1999, relates that the towel (*mendil*) of Jesus was thrown into a well in what is today the city's Great Mosque. The Christian tradition is at variance with this, recounting that, in 944, it was exchanged for a group of Muslim prisoners—at that time the Image of Edessa was taken to Constantinople where it was received amidst great celebration by Emperor Romanos I, who deposited it in the Palatine Chapel. It remained there until the Crusaders sacked the city in 1204 and carried off many of its treasures to western Europe—though the "Image of Edessa" is not mentioned in this context in any contemporary document.

Other documents from the 6th century—it is said—in the Vatican Library and the University of Leiden, Netherlands, seem to suggest another image at Edessa. A 10th-century codex, *Codex Vossianus Latinus* Q 69 found by Gino Zaninotto in the Vatican Library, contains an 8th-century account saying that an imprint of Christ's whole body was left on a canvas kept in a church in Edessa: it quotes a man called Smera in Constantinople saying “King Abgar received a cloth on which one can see not only a face but the whole body” (in Latin: *“Faciei figuram sed totius corporis figuram cernere poteris.”*). This image is apparently not the same as the Mandylion whose widely-disseminated and familiar iconic image is of a face alone.

Some modern scholars have suggested that by folding the Shroud of Turin in a certain way, it is possible to display a rectangle of fabric showing only the face, suggesting that the Shroud and the Mandylion may in fact be one and the same relic. This interpretation is also a means of explaining the provenance of the Shroud—of which no record exists before the thirteenth century.[^566]

**AIR / AER** (Ἀήρ, ὁ or Ἀέρας, ὁ) *Slav.* Vozdukh, vozdux 'the air'; often the term has been spelled **Aer**. In the Byzantine rite, this is the largest and outermost covering placed over both the chalice and **PATEN** (Diskos). It is larger than the individual veils each used to cover the chalice and the paten. It symbolizes Joseph’s shroud on Christ’s body as well as the stone sealing Christ’s tomb. All of these veils are beautifully embroidered and are often in the same style and color as the vestments of the celebrating priest. The Aer, which takes its name either from the lightness of the material of which it was formerly made or from the fact that in the Divine Liturgy, during the time of the recitation of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed, the priest holds it high in the air and waves it slowly over the chalice and paten. The use of the Aer, like that of the smaller veils, was intended to prevent anything from falling into the chalice or paten and contaminating them before the sacred vessels were brought to the altar prior to the consecration. The Aer is first mentioned by name in an explanation of the Liturgy by a writer of the sixth century, and is also alluded to as “the so-called *aër*” in the Acts of the Council of Constantinople. In the Byzantine rite the Aer is placed on the shoulders of the **DEACON** or **PRIEST** who brings the **PATEN** to the altar during the procession of the **GREAT ENTRANCE**.[^567]

The text of the Akathist Hymn is a beautiful poem, most likely authored by Romanos the Melodist and dedicated to the **THEOTOKOS**. It is offered on behalf of the people who flee to the Virgin Mary and Christ seeking deliverance from both adversity and sorrow.

The hymn is comprised of 24 stanzas arranged in four groups of six stanzas each. These stanzas are distributed through the evening service of each of the first four Fridays of Lent. Each **STANZA** begins with a successive letter of the Greek alphabet, from Alpha to Omega. The work is, therefore, an **ACROSTIC** poem.

In the first six stanzas chanted on the first Friday of Lent, the annunciation to the Virgin Mary by the Angel is described. It describes Mary’s surprise at the news, her visit to Elizabeth, and Joseph’s doubts as to her innocence.

The stanzas of the second Friday tell of the experience of the Shepherds and the adoration of the Wise Men at the Nativity, the flight into Egypt, and the Presentation of the Christ Child in the Temple.

The stanzas of the third Friday educate us as to the new “Creation” through the coming of Christ, the uplifting of our minds to heaven, the Lord’s presence on earth, and of the confounding of pagan philosophers who could not understand the mystery of the Incarnation.

The stanzas of the fourth Friday are once more a lyric and rhetorical appraisal of the Theotokos as the Protectress of the devout. The poet adorns the Virgin with the most beautiful epithets asking her to accept his poetical offering and to intercede for the salvation of the human race from earthly sin.

**AKOLUTHIA** (ἀκολουθία, ἡ) *Lit.* a following, attendance, or train. From the Greek *akolutho* “I follow”; *Slav.: posledovanie*). In the Eastern churches, the term signifies the arrangement of the Divine Services (Canonical Hours or Divine Office), perhaps because these various parts are closely connected and follow in order. In a more restricted sense, the term “akoluthia” refers to the fixed portion of the Office (that which does not change from day to day).

While the structure and history of the various forms of the Divine Office in the numerous ancient Christian rites is quite rich, the following article will restrict itself to the Office as it evolved in the Byzantine Empire.

The Office is composed of both musical and textual elements, the first is usually identified by the musical mode or tone or, in Greek, Tone (*Ichos* / ἦχος), according to which the compositions are to be chanted (see **TONE**). In Byzantine Music there are eight musical modes (Tones 1 to 8): four authentic and four plagal Tones. The textual elements may be read quietly in silent prayer, read in a normal speaking voice, or may also be chanted in a simple recitative style or sung with a fully constructed melody.

**ALLELUIA** (Ἀλληλούϊα): This word is derived from Hebrew and is interpreted in Greek as meaning **"Praise the Lord"** (*Αἰνεῖτε τὸν Κύριον*). The word is sung after the reading of the **APOSTOLOS**. The faithful use it as a sign of joy and an expression of gladness as they praise God and thank Him for His wonders. This is also evident from the ending phrase of many Psalms to which the word is attached and from what the Book of Tobit (a deuterocanonical book of Scripture in the Septuagint Greek Old Testament) speaks of Jerusalem being rebuilt again after its fall, that all its streets will say ‘Alleluia’ (Tobit 13:18 [LXX 13:18]). It is also evident from the Apocalypse of John, in which, after the fall and disappearance of spiritual Babylon, John heard a loud voice from heaven saying continuously ‘Alleluia’ (Revelation 18:2, 20–21; 19:1–6). **AMOMOS** (Ἄμωμος, ὁ): See also **PSALM 118 [MT 119]**. A common abbreviation for the entire 17th **KATHISMA** of the Greek Old Testament Psalter (Ψαλτήριον, τό). This Kathisma contains only Psalm 118 [MT 119]. The abbreviation is so named because of a form of the key word in Greek in the first verse of the Psalm: **"Blessed are the undefiled in the way"** (*Μακάριοι οἱ ἄμωμοι ἐν ὁδῷ*). This Psalm, like the others, has 3 parts called Stases or Sections (Στάσεις).

**AMVON** (ἄμβων, ὁ) *Slav.* Amvon: See also **PULPIT**. Also spelled *Ambon* in some translations.

As Christians began to build churches, a special place was designated from which the homily could be preached and the Gospel could be read. This place was called the amvon, today’s pulpit. It was a place erected in the middle of the church to which the deacon or priest would ascend via a stairway. Later in the church’s development the pulpit was attached to a column on the north side of the **NAVE**. As seen today, the pulpit is usually constructed on the soleas, to the north side of the **BEAUTIFUL GATE** (Royal Doors) of the **HOLY SANCTUARY** (the central opening in the iconostasis).
