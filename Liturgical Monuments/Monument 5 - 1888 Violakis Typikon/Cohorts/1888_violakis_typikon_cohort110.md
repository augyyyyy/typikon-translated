## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Matins Part 1) [Demos, p. 1076 / TD-97]

**MATINS** (*ὄρθρος, ὁ*; Slav. *Utrenya* / *Утреня*): Matins is a Western term which, in Greek tradition, is designated by the Greek term **Orthros** (*Ὄρθρος*), signifying "early dawn" or "daybreak." As an integral part of the daily cycle of services, it constitutes the final office among the four nocturnal offices, which encompass Vespers, Compline, the Midnight Office, and Matins. In traditional monasteries it is celebrated daily in the early morning so as to conclude at sunrise.

Matins is the longest and most complex service of the daily cycle. It is normally celebrated in the early morning, on occasion preceded by the Midnight Office, and regularly followed immediately by the First Hour. On Great Feasts it is celebrated as the principal component of an All-Night Vigil on the preceding evening, conjoined with Great Vespers and the First Hour. In the Greek parish tradition, Matins is normally celebrated in the morning immediately preceding the Divine Liturgy. In the Slavic tradition, an All-Night Vigil is celebrated on every Saturday evening preceding Sunday.

The fixed psalmody of the service comprises primarily the Six Psalms, fixed morning psalms, and litanies. The variable hymnography of Matins is drawn principally from the canons and hymns of the **Paraklitiki** (**Oktoechos** / *᾿Οκτώηχος*), the liturgical volume containing the hymnographic cycle for each of the eight Tones across each day of the week, cycling over eight weeks, and from the **Menaion** (*Μηναῖον*, pl. **Menaia** / *Μηναῖα*), the twelve monthly volumes comprising the propers for each calendar day of the ecclesiastical year. During the Great Fast (Lent), portions of the Oktoechos and Menaion are superseded by the proper hymnography of the **Triodion** (*Τριῴδιον*), and during the Paschal Season by the joyful hymnography of the **Pentecostarion** (*Πεντηκοστάριον*). On Sundays, Matins also features Gospel lections and proper hymns from the eleven-part cycle of Resurrectional Gospels (*῾Εωθινὰ Εὐαγγέλια*).

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Mode / Tone Part 1: Irmologic, Sticheraric, Papadic Styles; Octoichos Origins) [Demos, p. 1081 / TD-102]

**MODE** (*ἦχος, ὁ*; Slav. *Glas*, pl. *glasy* / *Гласъ*): The musical and liturgical **Tone**. A musical Mode in the Byzantine system is defined by: (a) a specific scale of melodic intervals, and (b) a characteristic corpus of melodic formulae and cadential figures (Slav. *popevki*). A mode is likewise governed by its distinct melodic range (*ἔκτασις*), opening intonation formula (*ἀπήχημα*), foundational base tone or ison (*βάσις, ἶσον*), dominant tones (*δεσπόζοντες φθόγγοι*), modal and cadential signature marks (*μαρτυρίαι*), and modal modulation signs (*φθοραί*). The liturgical melodies within each mode are composed in three distinct melodic genres or styles:

1. **Heirmologic** (*εἱρμολογικόν* / lit. 'irmologic'): The melodies are strictly syllabic in character, wherein each syllable of the sacred text corresponds to a single musical interval (or at most two beats). They are chanted in a brisk, rapid tempo (*allegretto* to *vivace*). A prime exemplar of the Heirmologic style is the musical setting appointed for the eight Resurrection Dismissal Troparia (lit. 'Apolytikia') chanted at the Small Entrance of the Divine Liturgy.

2. **Sticheraric** (*στιχηραρικόν*): The melodies are moderately ornate and neumatic compared to the Heirmologic genre, with each syllable sustained across two to three beats. The tempo ranges generally from *allegretto* to *andante*. The Sticheraric style is employed for the Lucernarium stichera (**"Lord, I have cried"** / *Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*), the Praises (*Ainoi* / *Pasapnoaria*) at Matins, festal stichera, and select festal Megalynaria at the Divine Liturgy.

3. **Papadic** (*παπαδικόν*): The melodies are highly melismatic, expansive, and elaborate. The tempo is stately and slow, though certain ornate passages are executed more rapidly. The Cherubic Hymn (*Χερουβικόν*) and the Communion Hymns (*Κοινωνικά*) serve as classic models of the Papadic genre. The particular melodic style chosen alters and expands the modal characteristics outlined above.

This eightfold modal classification system in Byzantine chant and hymnology gave rise to the weekly cycle of the Eight Tones. As early as the sixth century, these liturgical hymns were compiled into the hymnographic collection known as the **Oktoechos** (*᾿Οκτώηχος*), which was systematically edited and perfected in the eighth century by our venerable Father, Saint John of Damascus.[^646]

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (The Cycle of Modes Part 2: Weekly Oktoechos Cycle, Paschal Sequence) [Demos, p. 1082 / TD-103]

### The Cycle of the Eight Tones (Modes)

In Byzantine liturgical music, eight canonical Tones are recognized: Tone 1 (Mode I), Tone 2 (Mode II), Tone 3 (Mode III), Tone 4 (Mode IV), Tone 5 (Mode I Plagal), Tone 6 (Mode II Plagal), Tone 7 (Varys / Grave Tone), and Tone 8 (Mode IV Plagal). All hymnography of the Byzantine liturgical tradition is set within one of these eight Tones. The Eight Tones constitute the foundational architectural framework around which the Church organizes the daily offices and their hymnographic cycles throughout the ecclesiastical year. This structural logic is codified within the principal liturgical book of the daily cycle, the **Paraklitiki** (**Megali Oktoichos** / *Μεγάλη ᾿Οκτώηχος*).

The Paraklitiki contains the appointed services and propers for every day of the week across the year. It is divided into eight modal sections or tone periods (*περίοδοι τῶν ἤχων*), each comprising the complete services of a full week from Sunday through Saturday. Each of the eight modal sections is sung in one of the eight Tones, advancing sequentially from Tone 1 through Tone 8. When the entire eight-week sequence has concluded, the cycle recommences with Tone 1, repeating continuously throughout the church year.

This annual cycle of Tones is solemnly inaugurated on Holy Pascha with Tone 1, as evidenced by the predominant use of Tone 1 in the Praises (**Ainoi** / *Αἶνοι*) of the Paschal Matins preceding the Great Doxology. During Bright Week, the daily services draw upon the Paraklitiki in a unique daily progression:
- Bright Monday: Tone 2 (Mode II);
- Bright Tuesday: Tone 3 (Mode III);
- Bright Wednesday: Tone 4 (Mode IV);
- Bright Thursday: Tone 5 (Mode I Plagal);
- Bright Friday: Tone 6 (Mode II Plagal);
- Tone 7 (Varys / Grave Tone) is omitted during Bright Week;
- Bright Saturday: Tone 8 (Mode IV Plagal).

On the Second Sunday of Pascha (Sunday of Saint Thomas), the weekly cycle returns to Tone 1. From that day forward, the modal pattern shifts weekly, changing at Great Vespers on Saturday evening before each Sunday. Thus, the Third Sunday of Pascha (Sunday of the Myrrh-Bearing Women) is appointed in Tone 2, and so forth in unbroken progression through the subsequent Sundays until Tone 8 is completed. Upon reaching Tone 1 again, a new modal cycle begins, repeating cyclically until the Fifth Sunday of the Great Fast of the following year. Palm Sunday,[^647]
