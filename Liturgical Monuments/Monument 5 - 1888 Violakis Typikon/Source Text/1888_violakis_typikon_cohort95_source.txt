The relationship between immovable and movable feasts in the Gregorian Calendar.

    In accordance with the Gregorian Calendar, where the Triodion period begins on 24 January (13 days later than the beginning date in the Julian Calendar) the first four immovable feasts listed in the Chart — 11, 17, 18 and 20 January — will no longer fall within the Triodion period. Peter and Paul the Apostles; and 30 June, the 12 Apostles.

Interpretive Rules

    It was deemed important and necessary for the Priests and Chanters to know, if not also to find it easier to solve, the perplexities and difficulties that arise when the so-called immovable feasts, that is, those of the Minologion, coincide with the movable feasts of the Triodion and Pentikostarion periods. However, while the Typikon has written a great deal about each of these coincidences, they were never presented in a Chart; therefore, the following was developed, to help the curious find their answers. Since the movable feasts of the Triodion and Pentikostarion periods do not begin earlier than January 11 on the Julian Calendar-JC (the earliest date for the beginning of the Triodion period) [January 24 on the Gregorian Calendar-GC] nor do they extend beyond May 25-JC [July 3-GC,] the latest date that the Pentikostarion period may end, that is, the Feast of All Saints, for this reason the Chart has these beginning and ending boundaries, and only refers to those feasts of the Minologion that occur within this time span in order to facilitate the use of the Typikon for the Priest and Chanters. In order to make the use of the Chart easier, we deemed it important to make the following observations:

1. Holy Pascha [Easter] may occur between March 22-JC and April 25-JC [between April 4-GC and May 8-GC] a thirty-four day period. Therefore, the beginning of the Triodion period may occur between January 11-JC and February 13-JC [between January 24-GC and February 28-GC]. The first column of the Chart indicates the beginning date of the Triodion period; the second column the date of Pascha [Easter]. The third column shows the date and name of each of the 18 immovable feasts and, below it, the days of the movable feasts with which each immovable feast may coincide; and the fourth column displays the paragraphs and pages in the present work, where the details of the order of service of each coincidence are described. We present the following example: if we desire to know in the year 1889 on which day and which week of the Triodion period the Feast of the Presentation of our Lord occurs, we find the date of Easter for that year — which in this case is April 9 — then we look at the first two columns under this feast, and we find in the first column the number 29 [i.e. January 29], which is the date for the beginning of the Triodion period in that year; in the second column, we find April 9, which is the date of Easter for that year. In the third column, across from those dates, is indicated which week and which day of the week of the Triodion period this immovable Feast of the Presentation occurs, which in this case is the Thursday of the Week of the Publican and the Pharisee.

2. The number of immovable feasts that occur during the periods of the Triodion and Pentikostarion are 18, from the Feast of Saint Theodosios (January 11)—which is the earliest date on which the Triodion period can begin—until the Feast of the Third Finding of the Venerable Head of the Forerunner [John the Baptist] (May 25)—which could possibly occur within the Pentikostarion period. Of these 18 immovable feasts, twelve occur within the Triodion period and six within the Pentikostarion period, as the Chart indicates.

3. The immovable feasts that occur between January 11-JC and February 13-JC [between January 24-GC and February 26-GC] could possibly occur either within or without the Triodion period. The two last immovable feasts of May of St. Constantine and of the Forerunner — could possibly occur within or without the Pentikostarion period, depending on the date of Pascha [Easter]. Consequently, when the columns next to these immovable feasts do not indicate their occurence within the time frame of the movable feasts, then it is clearly understood that these immovable feasts occur outside the Triodion and the Pentikostarion periods.

[It should be noted, however, that the relationship between immovable and movable feasts in the Gregorian Calendar differs from that in the Julian Calendar (see the Comparative Outline of Movable Feasts, (pg. 926). In accordance with the Gregorian Calendar, where the Triodion period begins on 24 January (13 days later than the date of beginning in the Julian Calendar) the first four immovable feasts listed in the Chart — 11, 17, 18 and 20 January — will no longer fall within the Triodion period. This Chart does not address the anomaly that may possibly occur in Leap Years between the dates of the beginning of the Triodion period and the dates on which Pascha [Easter] falls, because it would be impossible to describe this anomaly clearly in a chart. For this reason, we must clarify here the following situations.

    When we have in mind two consecutive regular (i.e. non-Leap) years that have Pascha [Easter] on the same date, it is certain that the date of the beginning of the Triodion period, as well as the dates of the immovable feasts of the Minologion with the coinciding movable feasts of the Triodion and Pentikostarion periods, occur in these two years on the same dates without any change. When, on the other hand, we have in mind two years, one of which is a Leap Year and the other is not, both of these years having the same date for Pascha [Easter], we confront a difficulty in the coinciding patterns between the immovable feasts that occur before February 29 and the movable feasts within that time frame. This difficulty happens because these two years have different dates for the beginning of the Triodion period and, consequently, different dates for the coincidence between all the immovable feasts occuring before February 29 and the movable feasts. For example, we have the non-Leap Year 1889 and the Leap Year 1900. They both have Pascha [Easter] on April 9. However, in the year 1889, the beginning of the Triodion period occurs on January 29, whereas in the year 1900, the beginning of the Triodion period occurs one day later, i.e. on January 30.
