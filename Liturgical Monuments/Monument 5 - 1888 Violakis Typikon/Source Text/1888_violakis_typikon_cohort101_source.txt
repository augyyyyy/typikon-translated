EXTENDED DICTIONARY OF TYPIKON
RELATED TERMS.

ABSTINENCE (ἐγκράτια, ἡ). See also FAST: A penitential practice in Christianity consisting of voluntary deprivation of certain foods for religious reasons. In the Orthodox Church, days of abstinence are observed on Wednesdays and Fridays, or other specific periods, such as Great Lent. (FL)

*ACOLYTE (ἀκόλουθος, ὁ) 'follower': One who follows or assists the priest during services, ceremonies or processions. In today's church, an acolyte's duties may be performed by male children (altar boys). In the early Church, however, the acolytes were adults.

ACROSTIC (ἀκροστιχίς, ἡ): The term is derived from two Greek words: Akros, meaning the end or tip [of a verse], and STICHOS meaning a line [of verse]. A stichos is similar to a STANZA, although not as long. An acrostic poem, then, is a poem or verse of several lines in which the initial letters of all the lines read in exact sequence spell a word which may be the name of the author or other appellation/dedication. An acrostic may also begin with a successive letter of the Greek alphabet beginning with alpha. The earliest example of an acrostic was a Hebrew poem in which the consecutive lines or verses began with the successive letters of the Hebrew alphabet. This kind of poem is also known as an 'abecedarian poem'; the a-b-c-d can be perceived in this title. The most famous Scriptural acrostic is PSALM 119, where, each of the 22 stanzas of 8 verses begins with a successive Hebrew letter.

AERAS: See AIR.

AETOS (Ἀετός, ὁ) Slav. Orlets: Lit. 'eagle'. A small, usually woven circular rug, upon which an Orthodox hierarch stands at certain times in services in which he officiates. The eagle depicted on the rug represents an eagle overlooking and hovering over a city

A hymn which is based on an aftomelon is called a PROSOMION (Lit. in Greek 'toward the same', or 'similar.') Cf. IDIOMELON.

AGIA TRAPEZA (Ἁγία Τράπεζα, ἡ): Lit. the HOLY TABLE or ALTAR TABLE. It is the large central table found in the center of the SANCTUARY which has been sanctified in the Service of Consecration and contains the relics of three Saints or Martyrs.

It is upon this table that the Word of God rests in the text of the book called the Holy Gospel and it is upon this table that our Lord is sacrificed offering His gifts "on behalf of all and for all." Many Orthodox theologians consider the Holy Altar Table to be the seat or place of Christ in the Church.

The practice of the ancient church was simply to celebrate the "breaking of bread" in homes, on a simple wooden table not unlike that used in the Last Supper. In time though, the place on which the bread was broken changed from common wooden tables to the tombs of those men and women of the faith who were persecuted and martyred in the name of the Lord. This practice evolved into the use of marble altar tables (reminiscent of tombs) in which the sacred relics of saints and martyrs of the Church are placed at the time of its Consecration.

Although the appearance and function of the altar table has evolved over time, the church has remained steadfast in the practice of one priest celebrating only one Eucharistic celebration, the Divine Liturgy, on only one altar table on any particular day. A large community with more than one priest may in fact celebrate two liturgies; however, in the spirit of the above tradition, each liturgy would have a different chief celebrant who would serve at a different ALTAR TABLE or on a different ANTIMINSION.

Today, the Altar Table is covered with two cloths. The first cover on the bare surface of the table, the KATASARKION, is reminiscent of the shroud placed on our Lord in the tomb at His burial. It is placed on the table by the bishop during the service of the consecration of the church and remains there permanently as the very Body of our Lord has sanctified it. The second and often richly ornate cloth, placed over the Katasarkion, stems from a gesture of respect and love by the builders of the Great Church (St. Sophia) in Constantinople. This beautiful cloth "gold-threaded and adorned with precious stones" covered the table which housed the relics of the martyrs and upon which were placed the precious gifts of our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Today, the faithful as members of the body of Christ, may offer beautiful altar covers in like manner.

AGION MANDYLION (Ἅγιον Μανδύλιον, τό): The Image of Edessa, (known to Orthodox Christians as the Mandylion), was a holy relic consisting of a square or rectangle of cloth upon which an image of the face of Jesus was imprinted.

In a legend recorded by Eusebius of Caesarea, King Abgarus of Edessa had written to Jesus, asking Him to come and cure him of an illness. Instead, the apostle Thaddaeus is said to have come, bearing a cloth featuring Jesus' facial features, by which the king was miraculously healed. Since Jesus was still living at the time, this image would not seem to have a connection with His alleged burial shroud, today known as the shroud of Turin. The vicissitudes of the Edessa image between the first century and his own time are not reported by Eusebius.

The image is next said to have surfaced in 525 A.D. during a flood of the Daisan, a tributary stream of the Euphrates. This flood is mentioned in the writings of Procopius of Caesarea. In the course of the reconstruction work, a cloth bearing the facial features of a man was discovered hidden in a wall above one of the gates of Edessa.

There is no record explaining why or when the image was hidden in this manner, although some have suggested that it was to extend the assumed protection provided by the cloth to the whole city, or to hide it from persecutors of the Christian faith, persecutions being recorded in the area from the late first century until the time of the Emperor Constantine the Great. If the image was truly brought to Edessa in the first century, it might well have been hidden during the reign of Abgarus' son Ma'nu VI, who is thought to have reverted to paganism.

Evagrius Scholasticus in his Ecclesiastical History mentions the image of Edessa as being discovered in 544, that it was "created by God, and not produced by the hands of man." This idea of an icon that was Acheiropoietos (Ἀχειροποίητος) Lit. "not made by human hand" is a separate enrichment of the original legend. Similar legends of supernatural origins have also accrued to other Orthodox icons.

St. John of Damascus († 749) mentions the image in his anti-iconoclastic work On Holy Images, quoting an instance when King Abgarus had requested an image of Jesus and Jesus himself put a cloth to his face to produce the image. In St. John's work, the cloth is described as being a "strip", or oblong cloth, rather than a square, as other accounts have held.

Still another tradition maintained in the Vatican library, holds that the image on the mandylion or kerchief was transferred when a woman (St. Veronica) applied the cloth to the face of Christ as he was scourged while walking along the way of the cross to Golgotha and His crucifixion — this subsequently became known as "The Veronica."
