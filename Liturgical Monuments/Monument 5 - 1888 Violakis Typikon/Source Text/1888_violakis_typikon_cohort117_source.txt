TD-167

NON-LITURGICAL ATTIRE OF THE CLERGY:

    The MANDYAS (Μανδύας, ὁ) or mantle of the Bishop is also a monastic garment, which, like a cloak, covers the whole body with the exception of the head. All monks, when present at divine services, must be robed in their mantles. The Mantle is not technically speaking a liturgical vestment but in Vespers and Matins (Orthros) it is worn when a bishop first arrives for the service and is presiding from the episcopal throne; it is later removed with assistance by the priests or deacons before he is vested for the liturgy. In Greek Orthodox practice, the bishop descends from the Throne after the chanting of the KATAVASIE, enters the sanctuary through the BEAUTIFUL GATE where, after reverencing the book of the gospels lying on the altar table, he proceeds to the DIAKONIKON where the mantle is removed, and if he is to be the celebrant of the liturgy, he begins vesting with full episcopal vestments for the liturgy. In Slav. practice, after the bishop descends from the Throne as described above, he proceeds to the center of the soleas where the mantle is removed and, if he is to be the celebrant, he is publicly vested for the liturgy with the assistance of the deacon or priest.

    One peculiarity of a Bishop’s mantle is that it is not black, like the monastic mantle, but usually of purple, or some other color. Upon it are sewn the so-called “Tables of the Law” (Slav. Skrizhali), and, in particular, the “Fountains” (Slav. Istotchniki). The Tables (square patches at the neck and feet) typify the Old and the New Covenants, from which the ministers of God should draw their doctrine. The Fountains are ribbons, usually red and white in hue, sewn horizontally round the Mantle which represent the streams of teaching which flow from the mouth of the Bishop. Small bells are attached to the Mantle of a Bishop, and to his Dalmatic, as they were on the upper robe of the High Priest of the Jews.

    VIGIL (Ἀγρυπνία, ἡ) Slav. In Byzantine Orthodox practice, the All-Night Vigil follows Great Vespers including Liti and Artoklasia, Orthros, the First Hour, Third Hour, and Sixth Hour, and culminating with the Divine Liturgy. The All-Night Vigil, because of its length, is not usually celebrated in the parishes; however, on major feast days, it is sometimes done. In monasteries, the service is commonly celebrated on Great Feasts and the patronal feast of the monastic community.

As the NARTHEX was historically the place of the penitent and the catechumens and the NAVE was the place of the faithful, the SANCTUARY is the place of the clergy. Although the penitent and the catechumens today find themselves integrated with the faithful in the nave, the sanctuary continues to be the place of the presbyters of the CHURCH.

    The Vima is separated from the NAVE by the IKONOSTASION or icon screen. Up until the 14th century a simple, waist-high railing, the TEMPLON, separated these places. Although it divides space in the house of worship, it serves a greater purpose of uniting humanity with the Divine. The people of God are united with the saints, the Theotokos, and the angels to offer up all glory to Christ.

    Regardless of the direction the local church faces, the sacred space remains an awesome place. No one should enter the Vima unworthily or without the blessing of the priest for a specific task. Clergy alone are meant to enter into the altar, and then only after the clergy have spiritually prepared to glorify God and serve among the angels who are ever present in this the ‘Holy of Holies’. As clergy, as laity, as catechumens, and as the penitent, we find our respective places within the structure of the Church. As we celebrate the Divine Liturgy from our place we are distinguished according to our roles within the priesthood of all believers and It is in these places that we both realize our stewardship to Christ, and “glorify God in every place of His dominion.”

Today, the Ypakoi is the title of a hymn sung during Resurrectional Matins. It varies according to the Mode of the week from the Oktoichos and comes after the Resurrectional hymns which are sung together with the refrain from Ps. 119: “Blessed are You, O Lord, teach me Your statutes” («Εὐλογητὸς εἶ, Κύριε, δίδαξόν με τὰ δικαιώματά Σου.») The Ypakoi of Pascha is the one most commonly known. It is sung after the third ode of the Paschal Canon, during the Paschal Hours, and again after the Small Entrance in the Paschal Divine Liturgy.

    In Greek the word ypakoi means “obedient hearing” The term suggests a response, which takes the form of a TROPARION. The Ypakoi is performed: (1) at Sunday Compline; (2) at the Sunday Midnight Service; (3) at Sunday Matins before the gradual antiphons and the reading of the Gospel; and (4) sometimes after the 3rd Ode of the Canon, in place of the KATHISMA HYMN. (modified from APL.)

    Furthermore, the Ypakoi is a short hymn that takes the place of the third poetic Kathisma at Matins on Sundays and some great Feasts. It is normally read and not sung. In Greek use it is also read at the Hours on Sundays in place of the Kontakion. In strict Athonite use it is sung at the Liturgy after the Apolytikia and before the Kontakia. (AE)
