PROSTRATION (κατάπτωσις, ἡ): A term indicating that the worshipping individual falls down to a completely prone position.

*PROTHEORIA (προθεωρία, ἡ): Lit. Pre-theory. In his Typikon, George Violakis devotes the introduction—which he calls the Protheoria—to defining the general principles that he uses to order the services of the church. In the main body of his Typikon, he applies these principles in a detailed fashion to the order of service of each specific feast day in the church calendar for both immovable and movable feasts.

*PROTHESIS (Πρόθεσις, ἡ): The small alcove or the table where the service of PROSKOMIDI takes place. This area is located inside the SANCTUARY on its north side.

    If one were to peer through the central BEAUTIFUL GATE of the IKONOSTASION, one would likely see small alcoves, marble shelves or tables on either side of the back wall of the Sanctuary. This area on the left is known as the PROTHESIS while the area on the right is known as the DIAKONIKON. The unique purpose of each in the Sanctuary is realized by the various items that rest upon them and the various icons that are found on the wall immediately behind them.

    Located in the SANCTUARY, the Prothesis is the place where the Eucharistic Gifts are prepared during the Morning MATINS Service (ORTHROS), prior to the celebration of the Divine Liturgy. Additionally, the Prothesis is the place where the Eucharistic items i.e., the Holy Chalice, the Holy Paten, the communion cloths, the spoon used to distribute the Holy Gifts, etc., are stored. On the back wall of the Prothesis is traditionally found the icon of the Nativity.

PSALM (ψαλμός, ὁ): Lit. A Psalm. According to ancient practice, monastics would recite all 150 Psalms on a recurring basis. The custom of reciting all 150 Psalms each week during the course of the servies developed over time.

    The Kathismata are divided between Vespers and Matins, so that all 150 Psalms are read during the course of the week. Normally, one Kathisma is read at Vespers and either two or three at Matins, depending on the day of the week and the time of the year, according to the Church’s liturgical calendar.

    On Sunday nights, and the nights following an All-Night Vigil there will be no Kathisma read at Vespers. During Great Lent, Kathismata are read during the Hours also, so that the entire Psalter is completed twice in a week.

    Besides the 150 Psalms, the Psalter also contains the nine biblical Canticles. These are chanted at Matins during Great Lent.

    Kathisma 17, which is composed entirely of Psalm 118, “The Psalm of the Law,” is also an important component of Matins.

SCALE: Generally, in music, a scale is a sequence of musical tones in ascending or descending order. Scales are ordered by the pitch of the tone which is the position of a tone in the scale with the sound being determined precisely by the frequency of vibration of the source of the tone. Such ordering of tones provides a measure of musical distance. The distance between two successive notes in a scale is called an interval or scale step.

    In the science of acoustics, tones are measured by the frequency of their vibration numbers. Such frequencies are measured in cycles per second (CPS) or, in German, Hertz.

    In music, a tone is distinguished from noise by having a definite frequency or pitch, caused by the regularity of the vibrations which produce it. Further, in addition to frequency, any tone will also possess the physical attributes of intensity (loudness), and quality (timbre or color); the latter helps identify the source of the tone (such as the voice or an instrument.)

    A great variety of scales have been used in the past and in different cultures; no single interval relationship is common to all of them. In the 6th cent. B.C., Pythagoras defined the mathematical relationship of the perfect intervals (the octave, fourth, and fifth) and of the intervals between them (an interval being the difference in pitch between two successive tones). Later, the Ancient Greek modal system was adapted and modified in the Middle Ages by the Eastern and Western Christian Churches.
