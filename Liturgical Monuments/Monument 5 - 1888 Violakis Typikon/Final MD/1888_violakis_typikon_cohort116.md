Amen."** (*«Πάντοτε, νῦν καὶ ἀεὶ καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων. Ἀμήν»*).

The liturgical head-covering of the bishop is the Episcopal Crown or **MITER** (**Mitra** / *μίτρα, ἡ*; Slav. *mitra* / *митра*).

The **MITER** (*μίτρα*), also spelled *mitre*, is a richly embroidered and adorned domed crown or diadem representing the crown of thorns worn by Christ during His holy Passion, and serving as an insignia of the pastoral authority bestowed upon the bishop as an apostle and shepherd of the Church. It is worn by all hierarchs in episcopal orders and is patterned architecturally upon the imperial crown (*kamelaukion*) of the Byzantine emperors.

It is historically improbable that the Christian episcopal miter was derived from the Old Testament high-priestly headdress (*miznephet*), since crown-like miters were not adopted by Eastern bishops until after the fall of Constantinople in the fifteenth century. While in Byzantine Greek usage the miter is reserved exclusively to hierarchs (bishops, metropolitans, and patriarchs), in Slavic praxis the privilege of wearing a miter has in recent centuries been granted to certain distinguished archimandrites and protopresbyters as an ecclesiastical decoration.

As the Bishop puts on the Miter, the First Deacon proclaims: **"Let us pray to the Lord. Lord, have mercy. Thou hast set upon his head a crown of precious stones; he asked life of Thee, and Thou gavest him length of days"** (Psalm 20:4–5 [MT 21:3–4]) (*«Τοῦ Κυρίου δεηθῶμεν. Κύριε, ἐλέησον. Ἔθηκας ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλήν αὐτοῦ στέφανον ἐκ λίθων τιμίων· ζωὴν ᾐτήσατό Σε καὶ ἔδωκας αὐτῷ μακρότητα ἡμερῶν»*). The Second Deacon then completes the prayer: **"Always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen."** (*«Πάντοτε, νῦν καὶ ἀεὶ καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων. Ἀμήν»*).

The **PASTORAL STAFF** (**Crozier** / *ποιμαντορικὴ ῥάβδος, ἡ*; Slav. *posokh* / *посохъ* or *zhezl* / *жезлъ*) is bestowed upon bishops, and in certain Slavic traditions upon Archimandrites and Hegumens, as a visible symbol of their pastoral and spiritual authority over the monasteries[^709]

The **EAGLE** (**Aetos** / *ἀετός, ὁ*; Slav. *orlets* / *орлецъ*): A small circular or oval liturgical rug depicting an eagle with outspread wings soaring over a fortified city. The bishop stands upon this rug during divine services, and its use is reserved strictly and exclusively unto him. A larger Eagle-rug is placed beneath the bishop-elect during the rite of episcopal consecration, as prescribed in the Pontifical Archieratikon. The representation of the city denotes the bishop's pastoral oversight and canonical governance over his diocese, while the soaring eagle signifies the celestial loftiness and purity of orthodox theological doctrine. Thus the eagle-rug spread beneath the bishop symbolizes that in his life, virtue, and sacred teaching, he must emulate the eagle, which with keen vision soareth above all earthly and corruptible things, fixing its gaze upon heavenly realities.[^710]
