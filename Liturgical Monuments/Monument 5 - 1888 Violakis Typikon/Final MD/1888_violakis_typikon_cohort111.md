**ODE** (*ᾠδή, ἡ*): See under **CANTICLE**.

**OKTOECHOS** (*᾿Οκτώηχος, ἡ*) or **GREAT OKTOECHOS** (**OCHTOICHOS I MEGALI** / *᾿Οκτώηχος ἡ Μεγάλη*; Slav. *Oktoikh* / *Октоихъ*, *Osmoglasnik* / *Осмогласникъ*): See **PARAKLITIKI**.

**OLONYKTIA** (*ὁλονυκτία, ἡ*): All-night prayer and vigil without sleep. See **VIGIL**.

**OMOPHORION** (**OMOFORION** / *ὠμοφόριον, τό*; Slav. *Omofor* / *Омофоръ*, *Omoforij* / *Омофорій*, *Nadramenik* / *Надраменникъ*): See also **VESTMENTS**.

The distinguishing liturgical vestment of a bishop, worn over the shoulders, and the supreme emblem of his spiritual and pastoral authority. Originally fashioned from unbleached white sheep's wool, in modern usage it is tailored from rich liturgical brocade embroidered with crosses, worn encircling the neck and draped across the shoulders. Symbolizing the lost sheep that the Good Shepherd sought out and carried home upon His sacred shoulders, it signifies the bishop's pastoral vocation as a living icon of Christ.

Clergy and ecclesiastical institutions subject to a bishop's canonical governance and episcopal protection are formally designated as being "under his Omophorion."

While every Eastern bishop vests in the Omophorion, a corresponding vestment in Western Christianity is the archiepiscopal *pallium*, whose liturgical employment is governed by distinct canonical rubrics and limitations.

The Omophorion exists in two distinct forms: the ancient **Great Omophorion** (*μέγα ὠμοφόριον*), which passes around the neck, folds across the breast, and descends past the knees in both front and back; and the **Small Omophorion** (*μικρὸν ὠμοφόριον*), which is simpler in form, passing around the neck and descending in front similar to an **Epitrachilion** (stole), yet broader and shorter, reaching just past the waist. Owing to the intricate arrangement of the Great Omophorion and the liturgical dignity of the episcopal rank, whenever the bishop vests in this Omophorion or removes it, he is assisted by two subdeacons.[^651]

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Omophorion Concluded Part 2) [Demos, p. 1087 / TD-108]

Whenever he presides at any divine service, the bishop vests in the Omophorion. If he celebrates the Divine Liturgy, he wears both the Great and the Small Omophorion at different appointed times over his sacred vestments. At any divine office other than the Divine Liturgy, he customarily wears the Small Omophorion.

During the Divine Liturgy, the rubrics prescribe that the bishop vest in and remove the Omophorion repeatedly. When first vested before the Liturgy, the subdeacons place the Great Omophorion upon him; subsequently, when the rubric calls for him to resume the Omophorion, it is replaced, for practical convenience, with the Small Omophorion. In local traditions where multiple hierarchs concelebrate, customary usage assigns the Great Omophorion exclusively to the principal presiding celebrant at appointed rubrical moments, while the other concelebrating bishops wear the Small Omophorion throughout the celebration.

**Historical Development:**
In the early Church, the Omophorion consisted of a broad band of white wool embroidered with crosses and draped loosely around the neck, over the shoulders, and across the breast. The modern Roman pallium developed from this primitive Omophorion; however, in the West it evolved over the centuries into a narrow circular woolen band resting upon the shoulders, with short, weighted pendants hanging before and behind. In contemporary times, the papal pallium adopted by Pope Benedict XVI returned closer to the original form of the ancient Omophorion.

In the Christian East, the only structural alteration in the Omophorion has been an increase in its width and the adoption of embroidered silk and gold brocade in place of wool. Distinct historical testimony confirming the Omophorion as the liturgical vestment of the bishop is preserved in the epistles of Saint Isidore of Pelusium (c. 400). At that early date, it was already woven of wool and viewed as an explicit symbol of the pastoral duty of bishops shepherding their spiritual flocks. In the miniature illuminations of an Alexandrian World Chronicle, written probably during the fifth century, pictorial representations of the Omophorion already appear. In later centuries, the same depiction is found upon the celebrated ivory relief of Trier, depicting the solemn translation of holy relics. Among the sacred images dating from the seventh and eighth centuries in which the Omophorion appears are the frescoes uncovered in the church of Santa Maria Antiqua in the Roman Forum. The representation in these frescoes is essentially identical to its modern form.[^652]

**PROSKYNISIS** (**PROSKYNESIS** / *προσκύνησις, ἡ*; Slav. *Poklonenie* / *Поклоненіе*): A reverent bowing, prostration, or veneration offered before holy icons, relics, or the Cross, distinguished from *latreia* (the worship and adoration offered unto God alone).

**PROS KYRION, TA** (*Τὰ Πρὸς Κύριον*): Lit. "Unto the Lord." A standard abbreviation commonly used in the **Typikon** for the entire Eighteenth Kathisma of the Septuagint (LXX) Psalter. This Kathisma encompasses a total of fifteen Psalms, from Psalm 119 to Psalm 133 inclusive (the Songs of Ascents). See also **PSALM 119**.

**PROSKOMIDI** (**PROSKOMIDE** / *Προσκομιδή, ἡ*; Slav. *Proskomidiya* / *Проскомидія*): Often rendered *Proskomide* or *Office of Oblation* (lit. 'the offering or bringing of gifts to God'). This is the Service of Preparation performed before the Divine Liturgy. In this service, the priest prepares the bread and the wine for the Holy Eucharist at the Table of **Prothesis** (**Table of Oblation**). This table or alcove is situated on the north side of the Sanctuary. The service standardly takes place while **Orthros** (Matins) is being chanted.

**PROSOMION** (*προσόμοιον, τό*; pl. **PROSOMIA** / *προσόμοια, τά*; Slav. *Podoben* / *Подобенъ*): A proper liturgical hymn whose poetic meter, rhythm, and melody are modeled upon and copied from an **Automelon** (**Aftomelon** / *αὐτόμελον*), an original model hymn.[^660]
