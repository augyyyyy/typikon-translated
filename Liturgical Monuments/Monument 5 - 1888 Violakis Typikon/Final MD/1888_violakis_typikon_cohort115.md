The term *Troparion* indicates that the hymn is composed in a defined rhythmic style or melodic pattern (Greek *tropos* / *τρόπος*, mode, manner, or turn). A Troparion provides a concise theological distillation of the sacred event or holy saint celebrated in the services. In early hymnography, it served as an antiphonal response and refrain to the psalm verses chanted by the cantor. The name Troparion was thus originally applied to short prayers composed in rhythmic prose and inserted after each verse of a psalm.

In the fifth century, when troparia expanded into strophic forms of greater length, these poetic prayers were chanted only after the final three to six verses of a psalm. Hymns of this character formed part of Matins and Vespers in ecclesiastical and monastic centers of the fifth century. In this era, the divine office consisted of the Psalter, the nine Biblical Odes or Canticles, established liturgical formulas deriving from apostolic antiquity, and troparia composed by contemporary hymnographers. This practice, however, did not obtain in strictly secluded desert monastic communities: anchorites and hermits of the strict desert tradition, according to surviving accounts, rejected all forms of florid melodic chanting.

Κύριε, ἐλέησον»*). He then recites the vesting prayer: **"My soul shall rejoice in the Lord, for He hath clothed me with the garment of salvation, and with the tunic of gladness hath He encompassed me; as a bridegroom He hath set a crown upon me, and as a bride hath He adorned me with ornament; always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen."** (*«Ἀγαλλιάσεται ἡ ψυχή μου ἐπὶ τῷ Κυρίῳ· ἐνέδυσε γάρ με ἱμάτιον σωτηρίου, καὶ χιτῶνα εὐφροσύνης περιέβαλέ με· ὡς νυμφίῳ περιέθηκέ μοι μίτραν, καὶ ὡς νύμφην κατεκόσμησέ με κόσμῳ [Isaiah 61:10]. Πάντοτε· νῦν καὶ ἀεὶ καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων. Ἀμήν»*).

(2) The **Epitrachelion** (**Priestly Stole** / *ἐπιτραχήλιον, τό*; Slav. *epitrakhil* / *єпитрахиль*; from *epi*, upon, and *trachelos*, neck): The epitrachelion consists of a broad band of brocade worn about the neck and hanging straight down in front, permanently joined along its entire length. It signifies the outpouring of sacerdotal consecrating grace upon the priesthood (Psalm 132:2 [MT 133:2]). The priest, like the deacon, cannot celebrate any divine office without his stole. Vested in the Epitrachelion alone, without the **Phelonion** (*φαιλόνιον*), he celebrates the less solemn offices: Small Vespers, ordinary Compline, the Midnight Office (Greek *Mesonyktikon* / Slav. *Polunoshchnitsa*), and the Hours (when no Gospel is appointed to be read). At diverse prayer services (*treby*) in private dwellings, such as the prayer at the birth of a child or the blessing of homes, the priest likewise wears the Epitrachelion.[^700]
