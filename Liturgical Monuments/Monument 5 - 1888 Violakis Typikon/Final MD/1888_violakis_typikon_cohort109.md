The term *kontakion* originally derived from the Greek diminutive *kontakion* (*κοντάκιον*, from *κόνταξ*, a short rod or wand), referring to the wooden stick or cylinder around which the hymnographic parchment roll or scroll was wrapped on account of its length.

Originally, the Kontakion was a poetic "verse sermon" consisting of an introductory preamble or Proimion (*Προοίμιον*) followed by a series of longer stanzas, the Ikoi (*Οἶκοι*), all terminating with an identical recurring phrase or refrain (*ephymnion*). Kontakia were gradually supplanted in the liturgical offices by a subsequent poetic hymnographic cycle, the **Canon** (*Κανών*). In the present ecclesiastical office, only the Proimion (now itself customarily designated simply as the Kontakion) and the first Ikos survive in liturgical recitation. In contemporary Byzantine practice, they are regularly read at Matins (*Orthros*), except on great feasts, and the final phrase is repeated by the choir—a living survival of the ancient practice. The Kontakion is likewise read at the Little Hours and chanted at the Divine Liturgy, where in standard usage the proper kontakia are seasonal, coordinating with the prescribed Katavasiai.

No direct documentary evidence survives to establish the exact date at which the Kontakion was formally incorporated into the Byzantine liturgical system. No reference to this poetic genre is to be found in imperial Byzantine documents or writings during the initial era of its flourishing. Even the technical name *Kontakion* appears for the first time only in ninth-century sources; the monastic hymnographers who originally composed works of this genre designated such a composition variously as a Hymn (*ὕμνος*), Psalm (*ψαλμός*), Poem (*ποίημα* or *ἔπος*), Canticle or Song (*ᾠδή* or *ᾆσμα*), Praise or Laud (*αἶνος*), Prayer (*προσευχή*), or Supplication (*δέησις*).

A complete historical Kontakion comprised from eighteen to thirty, or even more, structurally and metrically identical stanzas. A single stanza is designated a Troparion (*Τροπάριον*), varying in length from three to thirteen poetic lines. All subsequent Troparia in the work are composed upon the exact metric and rhythmic model of an initial stanza, the **Heirmos** (*Εἱρμός*). A Kontakion is either constructed upon the pattern of an Heirmos composed expressly for that poem, or adopts the meter of a pre-existing Heirmos previously composed for another Kontakion. At the period when the classic Kontakia were composed, the Greek language had already surrendered the classical distinction between long and short vowels, which constituted the foundation of the quantitative meter of ancient classical poetry. The vowels and diphthongs *η*, *ει*, and *οι* had become phonetically equivalent to *ι* (iota), and *αι* equivalent to *ε* (epsilon). Consequently, a new tonic-rhythmic system of versification arose, founded upon the strict rule that all stanzas must possess the exact same number of syllables as the model **Heirmos** (*Εἱρμός*) upon which they were modeled, and that the word stress accents must occupy identical positions across all stanzas as in the corresponding verses of the Heirmos. The composition of a Kontakion—or, in later centuries, of a **Canon** (*Κανών*)—was a sophisticated undertaking that could be accomplished only by a poet who was simultaneously a master composer and musician. Melody and verse formed a single entity. In adapting the Troparia to the melody of the Heirmos, it was not merely necessary to equate the syllable count of each line with the model strophe; the dynamic melodic stress accents had to coincide precisely with the accented syllables of the hymnographic text.

The Kontakion appeared in Byzantine worship with striking suddenness, without direct literary antecedents in classical Greek forms. Byzantine hagiographical tradition attributes its introduction into Eastern Christian worship to Saint Romanos the Melodist, celebrated by Saint Germanos, who composed a hymn in his honor providing an account of his miraculous divine inspiration. The historical association of Saint Romanos with the rise of the Kontakion allows us to date its formal entry into the Constantinopolitan liturgical tradition to the early decades of the sixth century. Saint Romanos was of Jewish lineage, born in Emesa upon the Orontes River (modern Homs, Syria). He was ordained deacon at Berytus (Beirut) in Phoenicia, and subsequently journeyed to Constantinople during the reign of Emperor Anastasios I (491–518), joining the clergy of the Church of the Most Holy Theotokos in the Kyros district. There, according to sacred tradition, the Mother of God appeared to him on the Feast of the Nativity of Christ, bestowing upon him the miraculous charism of hymnography, whereupon he composed more than one thousand Kontakia. Notable antecedents to the literary form of the Kontakion existed in Syriac Christian poetry, with which Saint Romanos, being of Syrian provenance, was intimately familiar. Originally, the Kontakion was an extended liturgical poem comprising eighteen to twenty-four stanzas, whose substantial length necessitated the use of a continuous parchment scroll. **LITURGICAL YEAR** (*Λειτουργικὸν ἔτος, τό*): The annual cycle of ecclesiastical seasons, festal periods, fasts, and commemorations in the Eastern Church governing the celebration of feasts, memorials, daily scriptural lections, and proper hymnography. The liturgical calendars of the Christian East and West share a common historical foundation and structural logic, though divergent calendar reckonings (Julian and Gregorian) and computational methods for calculating the date of Pascha create variations in festal dates. **The Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts**: Celebrated on Wednesdays and Fridays of the Great Fast, on Thursday of the Great Canon, and on the first three days of Great and Holy Week (Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday). This rite is structurally an Office of Solemn Vespers conjoined to a communion rite, wherein the faithful receive the Holy Mysteries previously consecrated at the Divine Liturgy on the preceding Sunday and reserved upon the Holy Table. Sacerdotal tradition attributes the compilation and ordering of this service to Saint Gregory the Dialogist (Pope of Rome).

The Great Fast is fundamentally an "aliturgical" season on weekdays, meaning that the full celebration of the Eucharistic sacrifice with its festal, resurrectal character is precluded on ordinary Lenten days and reserved for Saturdays and Sundays. The celebration of the Presanctified Liturgy was instituted to strengthen the faithful through frequent reception of Holy Communion during their ascetic struggle without interrupting the penitential character of the fast.
