The diatonic minor scale is based upon the interval sequence: tone–semitone–tone–tone–semitone–tone–tone (t–s–t–t–s–t–t). This arrangement produces the lower third, sixth, and seventh degrees characteristic of the minor mode; the higher seventh degree, situated a semitone rather than a whole tone below the primary note or "tonic," is frequently borrowed from the major mode for use at cadential resolutions. Until the close of the nineteenth century, the use and influence of the diatonic scale in Western music remained predominant.

Related to musical scales is the fundamental concept of key. In musical theory, the term "key" is employed to designate the scale from which the tonal material of a given composition is derived. To state, for example, that a composition is set in the key of C major signifies that it utilizes as its primary tonal material the intervals proper to that scale.

The conception of key developed wherein a home tone, or tonic, serves as the principal focus of a musical composition, while the remaining tones assume structural importance according to their harmonic relationship to the tonic. The increasing complexity of musical instruments necessitated more refined tuning systems for assigning specific pitches to the twelve Western pitch classes that constitute the octave. In standard usage, this procedure designates the method employed in the tuning of keyboard instruments.

By the era of J. S. Bach, equal temperament—the calibrated adjustment of specific intervals from their mathematically pure acoustical ratios in order to establish a tuning system wherein music may modulate freely from key to key without unacceptable dissonance—had become firmly established. The resulting chromatic scale comprises twelve semitone intervals within the octave (represented by the white and black keys of the keyboard). Although the diatonic scale is fundamentally heptatonic (seven-toned), compositions set in standard major or minor tonality employ the remaining five tones of the chromatic scale as auxiliary or ornamental pitches. Music that employs them freely is characterized as highly chromatic, whereas music that employs them sparingly is designated diatonic.

*Byzantine Modal Scales* (*βυζαντιναὶ μουσικαὶ κλίμακες*) (See also **MODE**) (T. Bogdanos):

The musical scales of Byzantine sacred hymnography (Byzantine Chant) are fundamentally modal in character. They comprise eight ecclesiastical modes: Tone 1, Tone 2, Tone 3, Tone 4, Tone 5 (Tone 1 Plagal), Tone 6 (Tone 2 Plagal), Tone 7 (Grave Mode / Tone 3 Plagal / *Varys*), and Tone 8 (Tone 4 Plagal).

Although derived historically from the Ancient Greek modal system, both Byzantine and Western church modes in the Middle Ages diverged from their classical predecessors in nomenclature as well as in intervallic structure. Having exerted a formative influence upon Western plainchant (Gregorian Chant), Byzantine modal scales were structurally analogous to those of the medieval West and, like them, essentially diatonic. With the introduction of chromaticism, the scales of Post-Byzantine chant became increasingly nuanced, utilizing intervals narrower than the semitone and broader than the whole tone of the Western tempered system. This was accomplished through the addition or subtraction of microtones (*moria*) to or from standard intervals. These microtonally calibrated scales cannot be executed upon fixed-pitch tempered instruments such as the piano.

While modern Western music relies primarily upon two scale types—major and minor—Post-Byzantine sacred music employs four comprehensive tonal classifications designated genera (*γένη*, sing. *γένος*): the Diatonic Genus, the Enharmonic Genus, the Hard Chromatic Genus, and the Soft Chromatic Genus.

(1) The **Diatonic Genus** (*Διατονικὸν γένος*), in its central octave, spans from C to C′, with the pitches E and B pitched microtonally flatter than the corresponding E and B of the Western tempered scale. To this genus belong the scales of Tone 1, Tone 5 (Tone 1 Plagal), Tone 8 (Tone 4 Plagal, with an occasional minor seventh: C, D, E, F, G, A, B-flat, C′), and Tone 4 (the papadic melodies of Tone 4 employ a minor seventh and occasionally a minor third: G, A, B-flat, C, D, E, F-natural, G′).

An ecclesiastical mode consists not merely of an abstract scale, but of a defined intervallic scale united to traditionally established melodic formulae and cadential figures (*theseis*) characteristic of that tone (see **MODE**).

**SKEVOPHYLAKION** (*Σκευοφυλάκιον, τό*; Slav. *Sosudokhranilishche* / *Сосудохранилище*; lit. "vessel-treasury"): The sacred treasury and sacristy. In contemporary ecclesiastical architecture, this area functions as the **Diakonikon** (*Διακονικόν*) or vestry, situated on the southern side of the Sanctuary, where liturgical vestments, sacred vessels, holy relics, and crosses are preserved and where the clergy vest prior to divine services. In early Christian antiquity, the Skevophylakion was erected as a distinct, freestanding structure near the eastern facade of the church basilica. From this repository, the Book of the Gospels and the Eucharistic Offerings were solemnly carried into the church in procession, a liturgical movement commemorated in the Small and Great Entrances of the Divine Liturgy.

The modern Patriarchal Treasury of the Ecumenical Patriarchate contains a select collection of embroidered vestments, dating primarily from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, worn by Patriarchs, synodal hierarchs, priests, and deacons. No vestments have survived in situ from the Byzantine era, owing to catastrophic fires, urban conflagrations, and the presentation of diplomatic gifts to foreign Orthodox sovereigns and prelates—particularly in Russia—both prior to and following the Fall of Constantinople (1453).

Extant Byzantine vestments are preserved in international museums and monastic treasuries. The historic **Sakkos** attributed to Emperor Nikephoros II Phokas (963–969) is preserved at the Great Lavra on Mount Athos, while that of Emperor John I Tzimiskes (969–976) is conserved at Iviron Monastery. The Synodical Sacristy of the Patriarchate of Moscow preserves two gold-embroidered *sakkoi* bearing imperial portraits presented to Saint Photios, Metropolitan of Moscow (1408–1431), by Emperor John VIII Palaiologos (1425–1448). *Patriarchal Sakkoi*: The **Sakkos** (*σάκκος, ὁ*), a tunic featuring wide half-sleeves, originated as an imperial garment bestowed exceptionally by Byzantine emperors upon the Ecumenical Patriarch and selected autocephalous hierarchs.
