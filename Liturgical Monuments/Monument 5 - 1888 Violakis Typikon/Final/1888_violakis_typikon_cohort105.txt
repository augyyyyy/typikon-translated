**DEACON(S), SECOND, THIRD, etc.** (*Διάκονος, Δευτερεύων, Τριτεύων, κλπ.*; Slav. *Diakon*):

The Deacon is the third in order of the major sacred orders of clergy in the Orthodox Church, following the **BISHOP** and the **PRIEST**. The word deacon signifies a minister or server, originally designating one who waited upon tables (cf. Acts 6:1–6).

In the contemporary Church, the deacon ministers to the bishop and priest in the divine services and assists in the celebration of the Holy Mysteries (Sacraments) of the Church. A deacon may not, however, celebrate the Holy Mysteries by himself.

With the blessing of the celebrating bishop or priest, the deacon leads the faithful in the collective prayers (the litanies or ectenies) and reads from the Holy Scriptures (the Holy Gospel) during the divine services. He is likewise responsible for the ceremonial decorum of public worship, calling the assembly to attention at the appointed liturgical moments.

In addition, the deacon may perform other duties pertaining to Church life according to pastoral circumstance, with the blessing and at the direction of his bishop or priest: for example, the distribution of alms to the needy, or the distribution of the Holy Eucharist to the faithful from a second chalice at the Divine Liturgy. The deacon, however, may never consecrate the Holy Gifts.

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Order of Deacons Concluded – Despotikon / Despotika Part 1) [Demos, p. 1027 / TD-48]

In the Orthodox Church, the diaconate is not merely a transitional step toward the priesthood; numerous deacons embrace it as a permanent vocation, serving the ministry of the Church either full-time or in conjunction with secular occupations.

While early deacons of the Church primarily assisted bishops in charitable administration and works of mercy, in later centuries the diaconate evolved into a predominantly liturgical office wherein deacons assist at the celebration of liturgical services. Today, deacons frequently direct educational initiatives and youth ministries, visit hospitals, engage in missionary outreach, and oversee charitable programs.

Sacramentally, all deacons share equal clerical grace. Nevertheless, they are ranked and serve by precedence according to the date of their ordination.

Just as with bishops and presbyters, administrative distinctions of precedence exist among deacons. A senior deacon of a cathedral or principal church may be awarded the dignity of **PROTODEACON** (*πρωτοδιάκονος*, after the prototype of the Holy Protomartyr and Archdeacon Stephen), obtaining precedence when serving with other deacons. The chief deacon who attends specifically upon a bishop is designated an **ARCHDEACON** (*ἀρχιδιάκονος*). A deacon who has received monastic tonsure is called a **hierodeacon** (*ἱεροδιάκονος*).

In formal correspondence and ecclesiastical protocol (for instance, in epistolary salutations or formal introductions), a deacon is addressed as “The Reverend Deacon [Name].” The title is customarily abbreviated Dcn. or Dn. (though Dn. is likewise employed as an abbreviation for dean).

In informal address and ordinary conversation, it is customary to address a deacon as “Deacon [Name],” although in certain local traditions he is addressed simply as “Father”—for example, “Father Deacon [Name]” or “Father [Name].”

For an architectural and liturgical description of the deacon’s sacred vestments, see **VESTMENTS**.[^592]

**DEUTEROCANON** (*ἀναγινωσκώμενα, τά* / *Anaginoskomena*, "books read"): The ecclesiastical designation for the deuterocanonical books of the Sacred Scriptures. These comprise the holy books recognized by the Eastern Orthodox and Catholic Churches as inspired, canonical components of the Christian Old Testament, which are absent from the rabbinic Hebrew Masoretic canon. The term *deuterocanonical* derives from the Greek meaning “pertaining to the second canon.” Although the etymology might suggest a secondary status, reflecting the historical deliberations before their universal conciliar ratification, the term in Catholic and Orthodox theology does not imply inferior authority or non-canonicity, despite occasional conflation by outside commentators with the Apocrypha.

Protestant communions do not classify these sacred books as canonical Scripture; they generally omit them from published editions or segregate them under the heading of Apocrypha. The divergence in nomenclature often generates confusion between Catholic and Orthodox canonical corpora and non-canonical pseudepigrapha.

**DIAKONIKON** (*διακονικόν, τό*; Slav. *Diakonik*): The Diakonikon is the sacred vestry and liturgical repository situated on the south side within the **SANCTUARY**, where sacred vessels, vestments, and liturgical appointments are stored. Notwithstanding its mystical transcendence, the Sanctuary functions with precise liturgical efficiency. Like the **NARTHEX** and the **NAVE**, the Sanctuary is architecturally disposed to serve the ritual actions of the clergy who officiate at the Divine Services.

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Diakonikon Concluded – Skevophylakion – Diataxis Part 1) [Demos, p. 1029 / TD-50]

Looking through the central **BEAUTIFUL GATE** (*Ὡραία Πύλη* / Royal Doors) into the Sanctuary, one observes a recessed marble table or niche on either side of the eastern wall. The table situated on the north (liturgical left) is the **PROTHESIS** (Table of Oblation / *Proskomide*), while that on the south (liturgical right) is the **DIAKONIKON**. The distinctive functions of these two sanctuary chambers are defined by the sacred vessels resting upon them and the sacred iconography adorning their respective walls.

The **PROTHESIS** is the sacred table upon which the Eucharistic Gifts of bread and wine are prepared during the Proskomide office, performed during the morning Matins (*Ὄρθρος* / Orthros) prior to the celebration of the Divine Liturgy. In addition, the holy vessels and implements—the Holy Chalice, the Holy Paten (*Diskos*), the liturgical veils and Aer, the Asterisk, the Spear, and the Communion Spoon—are vested and preserved upon it. Upon the wall behind the Prothesis is customarily depicted the icon of the Nativity of Christ, commemorating that Christ is the sacrificial Lamb offered “on behalf of all and for all.”

In the early centuries of the Church, these subsidiary tables were not located within the sanctuary proper. Instead, the Eucharistic Gifts were prepared and the liturgical treasures preserved in an adjacent, independent structure designated the **SKEVOPHYLAKION** (*σκευοφυλάκιον*, vessel-guardhouse). From this external treasury the Holy Gospel and the Eucharistic Offerings were solemnly carried into the temple at the appointed liturgical moments by the clergy—a ceremonial movement preserved today in the processions of the Small Entrance with the Gospel and the Great Entrance with the Gifts. Over time, as liturgical architecture evolved, the separate *skevophylakion* fell into disuse, replaced by the integrated Prothesis and Diakonikon niches within the sanctuary itself.
