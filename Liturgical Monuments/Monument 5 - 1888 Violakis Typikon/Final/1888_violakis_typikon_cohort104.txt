**CANON** (*κανών, ὁ*; *pl.* *κανόνες, οἱ*; See also **KANON**, **KANONES**) *Slav.* *Kanon*: A principal structural component in the Matins (Orthros) service, although it also occurs at Compline, Midnight Office, and in various trebniki (services of supplication or *parakleseis*). A Canon is a lengthy poetic hymnographic composition structured into nine Odes, with each Ode consisting of multiple troparia, the exact number and appointment of which are regulated by the *Typikon*. The Canonarch holds precedence higher than an ordinary Reader and lower than a Subdeacon or Deacon. The Canonarch is tonsured by the Bishop through the cheirothesia for minor orders. In early Byzantine practice, this tonsure was linked with that of the subdiaconate or reader. The Slavonic *Typikon* prescribes that a monastic canonarch fulfills his liturgical office with his head covered (that is, wearing the monastic klobuk).

The earliest Christians assembled for the Eucharist in private homes (*domus ecclesiae*); during periods of imperial persecution, they gathered covertly in catacombs, caves, and secluded dwellings. **CHYMA** (*χῦμα*): *Lit.* 'in a poured, flowing stream.' A liturgical rubric specifying that a text is to be read in a simple, continuous speaking tone or steady recitative rather than chanted with elaborate melismatic melodies. On occasion, the term also denotes a continuous, flowing recitative chant, such as the priest's chanted petitions. The *Typikon* explicitly distinguishes between these modes of execution, employing the directives **“it is said chyma”** (*λέγεται χῦμα*, simple reading) and **“it is chanted chyma”** (*ψάλλεται χῦμα*, straight recitative chanting).

**CODEX** (*κῶδιξ, ὁ*; *pl.* **CODICES** / *κώδικες, οἱ*): Originally designating a bound collection of wooden tablets coated with wax for writing, the term *codex* came to denote a manuscript book composed of bound sheets of papyrus or parchment. The widespread adoption of the codex format was fundamentally spurred by early Christianity. While pagan and classical texts long continued to be written on scrolls (*volumina*), Christians embraced the parchment codex in the first and second centuries for liturgical and scriptural use, because a codex permitted writing on both sides of the folio (recto and verso), facilitated rapid cross-referencing between Old and New Testament passages, and was far more portable. By the fourth century, the codex had completely superseded the scroll for lengthy ecclesiastical documents and biblical manuscripts. In Christian iconography, Old Testament prophets are traditionally depicted holding scrolls, whereas the holy Evangelists hold bound codices.[^587]

**CREED** (*Σύμβολον τῆς Πίστεως, τό* / The Symbol of Faith) *Slav.* *Simvol Very* (*Сѵмволъ Вѣры*): Derived from the Latin *credo* (“I believe”). In Greek Christian tradition, the Creed is termed the **Symbol of Faith** (*Σύμβολον τῆς Πίστεως*), denoting the authoritative gathering and harmonization of the apostolic teachings by which Christians profess their common faith in the Triune God.

The official Creed of the Eastern Church is the **Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed**, formulated at the First Ecumenical Council in Nicaea (325) and completed and ratified at the Second Ecumenical Council in Constantinople (381).

In the early Church, diverse baptismal confessions of faith existed across various apostolic sees. These confessions were rooted in the baptismal liturgy, wherein catechumens were required to solemnly profess their personal faith prior to descending into the baptismal font. The earliest apostolic confession was the concise profession that Jesus is the Christ and the Lord. Through this public confession, the catechumen was baptized into Christ, dying and rising with Him into the new life of the Kingdom of God in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit (cf. Matthew 28:19; Romans 6:3–4).

As the Church expanded, local churches developed more detailed baptismal creeds to articulate the apostolic faith against emergent heresies. In the fourth century, the Arian crisis convulsed the Christian world regarding the eternal divinity of the Son of God (the divine Logos). The heresiarch Arius maintained that the Son was a created being, saying “there was a time when He was not.” Orthodox fathers, led by Saint Athanasius the Great, confessed that the Son is eternal, uncreated, and consubstantial (*homoousios*) with the Father.

This doctrinal conflict was resolved by the 318 Holy Fathers convened by Emperor Constantine at the First Ecumenical Council in Nicaea in 325, who promulgated the authoritative Symbol of Faith.[^589]
