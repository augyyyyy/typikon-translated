## IN LOVING MEMORY OF OUR PARENTS AND GRANDPARENTS

**Christ Economou and Sophia Economou**  
**Reverend Antonios Kalogeropoulos and Presvytera Katina Kalogeropoulos**[^5]

Christ Economou was born in Rendina, Greece in 1890. His grandfather, the village priest, placed him at the psaltiri (chanter’s stand) around the age of nine. Left legally blind by a high fever, Christ memorized from hearing what other chanters had learned from books. Arriving in America at age 21, he served the church in every city in which he lived, always without remuneration for himself and without reimbursement for the materials he purchased for the choir or the chanters. In 1913, Christ became the First Chanter in the “new” church of Sheboygan, Wisconsin. He went on to serve in churches in Washington State, California, Texas, and finally Tulsa, Oklahoma. In Tulsa, Christ organized a women’s choir, possibly the first women’s Greek Orthodox choir in America. Later, men joined and it became a typical four-part choir. He recruited and trained 15 other chanters during his tenure and continued as First Chanter until his death in 1973. During his 74 years of service to the church, Christ missed only two Sundays, and that was because of illness. His devotion and stewardship were recognized by Bishops and Archbishops, and he received the title of Tonsured Anagnostis.

*(Printed Page v)*

=== LEAF p8 ===

Although Christ Economou [the cantor] had no formal music training in his youth, he studied Byzantine music most of his life, and received vocal and instrumental music lessons throughout his time in America. He constantly transcribed Byzantine music into Western notation. In 1945, Christ wrote a concise version of the Typikon. The small book spelled out the important points of the services and pointed out mistakes in the original Violakis Typikon. If there were questions or differences in a section, he suggested how they could be resolved.

The study of the Typikon was a passion for my father, Christ Economou. That is why my family and I wanted to see this book published. This scholarly opus, the new bilingual Typikon, is a polychronion of effort and achievement by talented writers blessed by the Holy Spirit. I think it is fitting that the spiritual vision of a man who was physically blind should be realized in such a glorious way.

**James Economou**

# INTRODUCTION

The present work makes available to the Greek Orthodox Church in America in English translation the most authoritative guide to the order of its services, namely the *Typikon of the Great Church of Christ* by George Violakis, Head Chanter (Protopsaltis) of the Patriarchate of Constantinople at the end of the 19th century.

This treatise was composed after extensive study and deliberation by a committee consisting of four hierarchs and the Head Chanter George Violakis, which was appointed by the Holy Synod of the Patriarchate of Constantinople. It was written in its final form by Violakis himself and published in Constantinople in 1888. Thereafter, the Holy Patriarchate established the Violakis Typikon as the sole authoritative document outlining and stipulating the order (*taxis*) of all the services celebrated within the Church.

Throughout the centuries, the Church has made a continuous effort to make its services more inspiring to its worshipers, thus producing over time several Typika, each one amplifying and refining its predecessor. This sustained effort must have been inspired by St. Paul’s exhortation to us how to conduct our lives as well as how to worship God in His temple: «Πάντα δὲ εὐσχημόνως καὶ κατὰ τάξιν γινέσθω» (“Let all things be done decently and in order.”)[^6] 1 Corinthians 14:40.

It was this desire to preserve and perpetuate the order and beauty of our church services as an intrinsic, vital part of the experience of worship that impelled Dr. George T. Demos, a devoted, highly trained church musician (a medical physician by profession) to republish the Typikon by George Violakis. He wanted to reassert its sacramental authority and bring it anew to the awareness of our Church in this country by casting the book into a bilingual format, the English translation aligned with and facing the original Greek text exactly, page by opposing page. To make the work more meaningful, Dr. Demos added a dictionary of terms related to the Typikon, which he assiduously compiled and adapted from various sources.

To make the work’s contents more accessible to today’s user, Dr. Demos and I updated the complicated section where the yearly shifting Movable Feasts coincide variously with the Immovable Feasts, whose dates

*(Printed Page vii)*

=== LEAF p10 ===

have been fixed for all time. Since the original Violakis Typikon was based on the Julian Calendar, we put next to each Julian date its exactly corresponding date of the Gregorian Calendar, which is in use in the Church today. See the Chart of the Feasts (Πίναξ τοῦ Ἑορτοδρομίου), pp. 924 ff. [For the order of service as well as the hymnology (text and music) for the liturgies of the Sundays and Immovable Feasts coinciding variously with the Movable Feasts, see Theodore Bogdanos, *The Byzantine Liturgy: Hymnology and Order*, Denver, Colorado, 1993, pp. 283 ff.][^7]

The English translation was undertaken by Rev. Robert Athas, who, as both scholar and practicing liturgist, brought to this demanding task an expert, intimate knowledge of Orthodox ritual. Fr. Athas had already translated the Triodion section in the Violakis Typikon as his thesis for his Master of Divinity degree at the St. Vladimir Theological Seminary in New York. In time, I was asked by Dr. Demos to contribute to the further refinement of the translation and the work’s editorial form.

George Demos immersed himself in this project for more than 16 years. He defied physical ailments and protracted therapies — even the threat of impending sudden death — in order to finish this work for the Church. It was in the early hours of the morning that he passed away while inserting some final touches into his computer.

After his death, I proceeded to put the work into its final form as we both had conceived it. Its publication was most generously undertaken by Poti Doukas (an aerospace engineer), a gifted member of the Choir of the Assumption Cathedral in Denver, Colorado, and Dr. George Stefanidakis (a retired chemical engineer), a member of the choir and a chanter at Annunciation Cathedral in Houston, Texas. Through his computer expertise, Dr. Stefanidakis carried out several difficult corrections and updated the entire process of printing the final version. In the meantime, James Economou, a former choir director of the Church, in an extraordinary gesture of generosity, had offered to fully finance the publication of the work.

Finally, those of us who have collaborated on this project bring now this book to God as an offering, laying it like a piece of woven, multicolored tapestry at the footsteps of His throne.

**Theodore Bogdanos**

# INTRODUCTION TO THE ENGLISH TRANSLATION

In 1985, the Denver Diocese Choir Federation held its annual conference in Tulsa, Oklahoma. I was the Proistamenos of the Holy Trinity parish there. Sitting in my office during the Conference one afternoon, chatting with some of the conferees, Dr. George Demos scanned the long wall of bookshelves. His eyes settled on a copy of the thesis I wrote for my Master of Divinity. The thesis was titled “The Triodion in the Typicon of the Great Church.”[^12] George asked if I would like to translate the rest of the Typikon. Not realizing what was involved, I agreed, and twenty years later this volume is the result.

It was not just a work of sitting down with a dictionary but a real collaboration that gave me the opportunity for wonderful conversations with Dr. Demos and with Dr. Theodore Bogdanos. Besides the work of parish and family life which would take my attention for most of the time, the hardest part of this task was remembering that we were in a four-hour difference in time zones. The easiest part was the joy of sharing our mutual love of the Typikon and our excitement in discovering footnotes to the footnotes! And comparing various Typika: Jerusalem, etc.

George Demos’ best direction to me was his insistence that “If you can make me understand it, I know we are on the right track.” I only regret that George did not live to see this work completed. I remember him with great fondness both for his humor and his humanity and his great love for the Church and for this great work of love. May his memory be eternal! Now, my hope is that this English translation of the Typikon will be helpful to all priests, choir directors, chanters and other church musicians.

The Typikon ends with the exclamation: «Τέλος καὶ τῷ Θεῷ δόξα!» (“The end and glory to God!”)[^13] It is truly God Who is the author and finisher of all things and to Him be honor and glory unto the ages of ages! Amen!

**Rev. Robert T. Athas**  
Protopresbyter

# GUIDELINES AND OBSERVATIONS ON THE PREVIOUS EDITIONS OF THE TYPIKON

The synodically appointed five-member Committee consisting of the Reverend Metropolitans of Ainos, Samos, Caesarea, Mytiline and the Protopsaltis [highest-ranking chanter] of the Great Church George Violakis, which reviewed the previous Typika of the Church—such as that by the Protopsaltis Constantine—which are in use today, corrected in its report the errors and inaccuracies of these works as follows:

Occasionally, their various guidelines, which are scattered in many places in their work, differ on what is appropriate to be performed on the commemorations [feasts] of the saints. Every disagreement, therefore, must cease to exist, every doubt must be clearly articulated and corrected, however secondary or minor the contradiction or variation may be.

## ΕΡΜΗΝΕΙΑ ΖΩΝΑΡΑ

Ἡ ἐν ταῖς ἐκκλησίαις γινομένη ψαλμῳδία παράκλησίς ἐστι πρὸς Θεόν, δεομένων ἡμῶν ἵλεων ἔσεσθαι τὸ θεῖον ἡμῖν, ἐφ' οἷς αὐτῷ προσκεκρούκαμεν. “We will that those whose office it is to sing in the churches do not use undisciplined vociferations, nor force nature to shouting, nor adopt any of those modes which are incongruous and unsuitable for the church: but that they offer the psalmody to God, Who is the observer of secrets, with great attention and compunction. At this point the Typikon presents exegetical comments from three great 12th-century scholars of Canon Law: John Zonaras, Theodore Valsamon, and Alexios Aristinos. ## EXEGETICAL COMMENT BY ZONARAS

Zonaras relates the chanters of his time to the sons of Israel, all being the children of God, and holds their reverence and piety in the act of worship as a model to be emulated by the chanters of Zonaras’s own time. Since the chanting in church is essentially a supplication to God, the chanters should sing in a humble and penitent manner, avoiding self-exhibition with musical extravagance such as excessive embellishments or loud voices distracting the worshiper. At the same time, Zonaras insists on good musicianship, condemning formless, arhythmical melodies. The music itself should be appropriate to the sacred place, avoiding any secular or erotic associations. He pleads for solemnity as well as artistic unity in the act of worship.[^32]

## EXEGETICAL COMMENT BY VALSAMON

Valsamon reminds us that churches are houses of prayer. The faithful should pray to God with contrition and humility, avoiding extravagant, unbecoming deportment or attire. Similarly, those who take an active part in performing a liturgical event—clergymen and chanters—should avoid musical self-exhibition as well as theatrical effects in their ritual movement that do not fit the holy and solemn sacred occasion.

Like Zonaras, Valsamon invokes Mosaic law and the piety of the sons of Israel as an example for his time. He goes on to state that his contemporaries, having been enlightened by their faith as Christians, should be even more reverent than their Israeli counterparts. To further authenticate his plea for reverence, he refers to the 98th Canon of the Council in Carthage [A.D. 418 or 419] and to a celebratory homily by St. John Chrysostom on the proper manner of worship.[^33]

## EXEGETICAL COMMENT BY ARISTINOS

“The disordered voice of a chanter is not acceptable.” Invoking even the tradition of Israel (and by implication the manner of worship in David’s Tent), Aristinos castigates “loud, jagged voices” that distract the worshiper from meditation and from prayerful reverence toward God, for God knows the silent, secret thoughts of His creatures; He does not need this tawdry loudness.[^34]

For those who chant psalms are filled with the Holy Spirit, even as those who sing satanic songs are filled with an unclean spirit. What is meant by *with your hearts to the Lord*? It means, paying heed with spiritual understanding; for those who do not attend closely merely chant outwardly, uttering the words with their mouth while their heart wanders elsewhere.’” (Saint John Chrysostom, *Homily XIX on the Epistle to the Ephesians*, Migne ed., Vol. 62).[^38]

## [ADVICE BY THEODORE THE STUDITE]
### [A.D. 759-826]

Our Holy Father Theodore the Studite addresses chanters and canonarchs, exhorting them to refine and further spiritualize their tasks. He first reminds them of the lofty, sacramental character of their ministry. He urges them to obey the established order of service and to be always conscious of the significance and the liturgical form of all types of hymns: e.g. Kathismata, Stichera.[^39]

In chanting and reciting, they should use a clear, well-rounded voice, following Saint Paul’s exhortation that everything in the act of divine worship should be done decently and in order (*kata taxin*). The chanter should exercise restraint and avoid extravagance, such as singing a very high note and then, for theatrical effect, descending suddenly to a very low note. The good chanter should help other aspiring chanters to develop their God-given talent. Thus, God will compensate the chanter’s humanly limited service with infinite blessings.

*(Printed Page 21)*

## ON CHANTERS

Violakis cites the 15th Canon of the Council of Laodicea [A.D. 364], which strictly forbids anyone to chant in church services except canonical chanters who are numbered among the clergy and appointed to this ministry. To avoid disorder and musical confusion (*chasmodia*), only highly trained and experienced chanters are permitted to sing, in order to create ecclesiastical order (*eutaxia*) and evoke in the faithful holy compunction and reverence.[^40]

## GUIDING CRITERIA FOR CHANTERS

Violakis cites the following list of criteria for sacred chanting, formulated by the Patriarchal Music Committee and ratified by the Church:[^41]

1. Chanters should be selected only if they harmonize with one another in vocal timbre and in musical artistry.

2. Isokrates (drone holders) and canonarchs should be appointed, at least three for each choir, and they must rehearse twice or three times a week.

*(Printed Page 23)*

=== LEAF p39 ===

3. ὁρισμὸς τῶν ψαλτέων μαθημάτων καὶ γύμνασις ἐπ’ αὐτῶν·
4. σύστασις τοῦ σεμνοῦ καὶ ἱεροπρεποῦς εἰς πάντα τὰ μέλη τοῦ χοροῦ, ἀποφυγὴ δὲ τοῦ ἐπιτετηδευμένου καὶ ἐκλελυμένου·
5. σύστασις εἰς πάντας προφορᾶς ἀκριβοῦς, ὥστε τὸ ἀκροατήριον οὐχὶ μόνον νὰ ἀκούῃ, ἀλλὰ καὶ νὰ κατανοῇ τὰ ψαλλόμενα οὕτως ὥστε νὰ παράγωνται ἐν ταῖς καρδίαις τῶν ἐκκλησιαζομένων τὰ ἀνάλογα αἰσθήματα·
6. καθορισμὸς ἐκ τῶν προτέρων τῆς οἰκείας ἑκάστῳ ἐκκλησιαστικῷ ᾄσματι χρονικῆς ἀγωγῆς καὶ τοῦ ῥυθμοῦ, καὶ ἀναγραφὴ αὐτοῦ κατὰ τομὴν ἐν τῇ διαρκείᾳ τῶν μελῶν· καὶ
7. προσθήκη ἰδιαιτέρας γραμμῆς ἀναγραφούσης ἐν τῇ διαρκείᾳ τοῦ ψαλλομένου ᾄσματος τὸ μέρος τῶν ἰσοκρατῶν.»

*(Printed Page 24)*

=== LEAF p40 ===

3. Prescribed musical pieces must be assigned for practice, and the choirs must rehearse them thoroughly.

4. Sacred gravity and reverence must be observed by all members of the choir, avoiding any theatrical affectation or laxity.

5. Precise diction must be maintained by all singers, so that the congregation may not only hear but also comprehend the words chanted, awakening in the hearts of the worshipers appropriate feelings of devotion.

6. The proper tempo and rhythm appropriate to each church chant must be determined in advance and marked by melodic divisions (*tomai*) throughout the composition.

7. A distinct stave or musical line indicating the part for the *isokrates* (drone singers) must be added throughout the duration of the chanted hymn.

*(Printed Page 25)*

## Η Θ΄ ΩΡΑ

Ἡ πρὸ τοῦ Ἑσπερινοῦ καθ᾿ ἑκάστην ἡμέραν ἀναγινωσκομένη θ΄ ὥρα ἐστὶ σφραγὶς τῆς Ἀκολουθίας τῆς ἤδη ληγούσης ἐκκλησιαστικῶς ἡμέρας. Καὶ ἂν ἡ ἡμέρα ᾖ Δεσποτικὴ ἢ Θεομητορικὴ ἑορτὴ ἢ μεθέορτος ἢ μνήμη ἑορταζομένου Ἁγίου, Ἀπολυτίκιον καὶ Κοντάκιον λέγονται ἐν ταύτῃ τὰ τῆς ἑορτῆς ἢ τοῦ Ἁγίου· εἰ δὲ μή, τὸ τοῦ τυχόντος Ἁγίου τῆς ἡμέρας Ἀπολυτίκιον, καὶ ἀντὶ Κοντακίου λέγεται τὸ **«Βλέπων ὁ Λῃστὴς τὸν ἀρχηγὸν τῆς ζωῆς»**. *Δόξα*, **«Ἐν μέσῳ δύο λῃστῶν»**. *Καὶ νῦν*, **«Τὸν Ἀμνὸν καὶ Ποιμένα»**. Τῷ Σαββάτῳ ἑσπέρας Ἀπολυτίκιον **«Ἀπόστολοι μάρτυρες»** καὶ Κοντάκιον **«Ὡς ἀπαρχὰς τῆς φύσεως»**· τῇ δὲ Κυριακῇ ἑσπέρας τὸ Ἀναστάσιμον Ἀπολυτίκιον, καὶ ἀντὶ Κοντακίου ἡ Ὑπακοὴ τοῦ Ἤχου. Ἐν ὅλῃ δὲ τῇ Διακαινησίμῳ ἑβδομάδι καὶ τῷ Σαββάτῳ τῆς Διακαινησίμου ἑσπέρας πρὸ τοῦ Ἑσπερινοῦ τοῦ Θωμᾶ, ὡς καὶ τῇ Τετάρτῃ τῆς Ἀποδόσεως τῆς ἑορτῆς τοῦ Πάσχα ἑσπέρας πρὸ τοῦ Ἑσπερινοῦ τῆς Ἀναλήψεως, ἀναγινώσκεται ἡ τοῦ Πάσχα θ΄ ἤτοι τὸ **«Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ»**, **«Προλαβοῦσαι τὸν ὄρθρον»** κτλ. ὡς ἐν τῷ Ὡρολογίῳ διατυποῦται. Τῇ δὲ Τρίτῃ τοῦ Τυφλοῦ ἑσπέρας, πρὸ τοῦ Ἑσπερινοῦ τῆς Ἀποδόσεως τοῦ Πάσχα, ἀναγινώσκεται ἡ θ΄ τρίψαλμος καὶ λέγεται Ἀπολυτίκιον...

# PRETHEORY OF THE TYPIKON
### That Is:
### The Typikon Order of the Services of Vespers, Matins, and the Divine Liturgy
#### For Sundays and the Remaining Feasts of the Entire Year, with All of Their Cases

---

## THE NINTH HOUR[^54]

The Ninth Hour, read daily before Vespers, is the seal of the services of the ecclesiastically closing day. If the day is a Feast of the Lord or of the Mother of God, a post-festal day, or the commemoration of a celebrated Saint, the Dismissal Troparion and Kontakion of the feast or of the Saint are said at this Hour. If not, the Troparion of the ordinary Saint of the day is said; and instead of a Kontakion, there is said: **"The thief, beholding the Author of life"** (*Βλέπων ὁ Λῃστὴς τὸν ἀρχηγὸν τῆς ζωῆς*); *Glory...*, **"In the midst of two thieves"** (*Ἐν μέσῳ δύο λῃστῶν*); *Now and forever...*, **"The Lamb and Shepherd"** (*Τὸν Ἀμνὸν καὶ Ποιμένα*). On Saturday evening: Troparion **"Apostles, martyrs"** (*Ἀπόστολοι μάρτυρες*), and Kontakion **"As the first-fruits of nature"** (*Ὡς ἀπαρχὰς τῆς φύσεως*). On Sunday evening: the Resurrection Troparion, and instead of a Kontakion, the Ypakoe of the Tone [Tone 1–8]. Throughout Bright Week and on the Saturday of Bright Week at evening before the Vespers of St. Thomas, as well as on Wednesday of the Apodosis of the Feast of Pascha at evening before the Vespers of the Ascension, the Paschal Ninth Hour is read, that is: **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** (*Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ θεασάμενοι*), **"They who were with Mary came before the dawn"** (*Προλαβοῦσαι τὸν ὄρθρον*), etc., as set forth in the *Horologion* (*Ὡρολόγιον*). On the Tuesday evening of the Blind Man, before the Vespers of the Apodosis of Pascha, the ordinary three-psalm Ninth Hour is read, and there are said: the Troparion **"The Word, co-eternal with the Father and the Spirit"** (*Τὸν συνάναρχον Λόγον*, Tone 5) and the Kontakion...

*(Printed Page 37)*

=== LEAF p53 ===

...**«Τὸν συνάναρχον λόγον»** καὶ Κοντάκιον **«Τῆς ψυχῆς τὰ ὄμματα»**. Ἐν τῇ Μ. δὲ Τεσσαρακοστῇ ἀναγινώσκεται ὡς ἐστὶ διατεταγμένη ἐν τῷ Ὡρολογίῳ μετὰ τῶν Μακαρισμῶν καὶ τῶν ἀκολούθων αὐτοῖς. Ἀπόλυσις ἐν τῇ θ΄ λέγεται πάντοτε ἡ μικρὰ (ὅρα αὐτὴν κατωτέρω ἐν §16) μετὰ τοῦ χαρακτηριστικοῦ τῆς ἑορτῆς, ἧς ἐλέχθησαν τὸ Ἀπολυτίκιον καὶ τὸ Κοντάκιον.

## Ο ΕΣΠΕΡΙΝΟΣ

### ΠΕΡΙ ΤΟΥ ΕΥΛΟΓΗΤΟΣ Ο ΘΕΟΣ

**2.** Ἀρχὴ τῆς Ἀκολουθίας τοῦ Ἑσπερινοῦ καὶ τῆς τοῦ Ὄρθρου καὶ πάσης ἄλλης ἱερᾶς τελετῆς ἐστιν ἡ ὑπὸ τοῦ Ἱερέως ἐκφωνουμένη εὐχή, **«Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν»**¹, ἐξαιρέσει τῆς Θείας Λειτουργίας, τῆς τελετῆς τοῦ Ἁγίου Βαπτίσματος καὶ τοῦ Μυστηρίου τοῦ Γάμου, πρὸ τῶν ὁποίων ἐκφωνεῖται τὸ **«Εὐλογημένη ἡ Βασιλεία τοῦ Πατρός»**. Ἐν τοῖς Ἑσπερινοῖς δὲ² καὶ τοῖς Ὄρθροις τῆς Διακαινησίμου ἑβδομάδος καὶ τῆς ἡμέρας τῆς Ἀποδόσεως τοῦ Πάσχα, ὡς καὶ πρὸ τοῦ καθ᾿ ἑκάστην ἡμέραν ἀναγινωσκομένου Ἑξαψάλμου, ἐκφωνεῖται τὸ **«Δόξα τῇ Ἁγίᾳ καὶ Ὁμοουσίῳ καὶ ζωοποιῷ καὶ ἀδιαιρέτῳ Τριάδι»**.

### ΠΕΡΙ ΤΟΥ ΠΡΟΟΙΜΙΑΚΟΥ ΨΑΛΜΟΥ

**3.** Ὁ Προοιμιακὸς Ψαλμός, ἡ μεγαλοπρεπεστέρα δοξολογία τοῦ...

---

¹ Ὀφείλει δὲ ὁ Ἱερεὺς πρὸ τῆς ἐκφωνήσεως ταύτης προσκυνῆσαι καὶ ἀσπασθῆναι τὴν Ἁγίαν Τράπεζαν καὶ φορέσαι τὸ ἐπιτραχήλιον· καὶ τοῦτο ἀποτίθησι μόνον μετὰ τὴν Ἀπόλυσιν τοῦ Ἑσπερινοῦ· ἄλλως ἀτακτεῖ καὶ παρανομεῖ.  
² Τὰ ἀρχαῖα Τυπικὰ σημειοῦσιν ὅτι ποιοῦμεν Εὐλογητὸν καὶ ἐν τῷ Ἑσπερινῷ τῆς Καινῆς Δευτέρας, ὡς συνήθως, ἐκφωνοῦντες *Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν* πάντοτε ὅπερ δηλοῖ ὅτι ἡ ἀντικατάστασις τούτου διὰ τοῦ *Δόξα τῇ ἁγίᾳ καὶ ὁμοουσίῳ* ἐστὶ παράτυπος νεωτερισμός· ἀλλὰ νῦν εἴθισται πανταχοῦ σχεδὸν οὕτω καθ᾿ ὅλην τὴν Διακαινήσιμον· μόνον ἀντὶ τοῦ *Δόξα σοι ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν δόξα σοι* μετὰ τὸ *Εὐλογητὸς* σημειοῦσι τὰ ἀρχαῖα Τυπικὰ ὅτι λέγεται τὸ *Χριστὸς ἀνέστη* κατὰ τὴν Διακαινήσιμον ἑβδομάδα.

*(Printed Page 38)*

=== LEAF p54 ===

...**"Blinded in the eyes of my soul"** (*Τῆς ψυχῆς τὰ ὄμματα*). During Great Lent, the Ninth Hour is read as appointed in the *Horologion*, with the Beatitudes (*Μακαρισμοί*) and their concluding prayers. The dismissal at the Ninth Hour is always the Small Dismissal (see below, §16), together with the characteristic invocation of the feast whose Troparion and Kontakion were said.

## VESPERS

### Concerning *Blessed is our God*

**2.** The beginning of the service of Vespers, of Matins, and of every other sacred rite is the prayer intoned by the Priest: **"Blessed is our God"** (*Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*),[^55] with the exception of the Divine Liturgy, the sacrament of Holy Baptism, and the mystery of Holy Matrimony, before which is intoned: **"Blessed is the Kingdom of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit"** (*Εὐλογημένη ἡ Βασιλεία τοῦ Πατρός*). At the Vespers[^56] and Matins of Bright Week and the day of the Apodosis of Pascha, as well as before the daily reading of the Six Psalms (*Ἑξάψαλμος*), there is intoned: **"Glory to the Holy, Consubstantial, Life-Creating, and Undivided Trinity"** (*Δόξα τῇ Ἁγίᾳ καὶ Ὁμοουσίῳ καὶ ζωοποιῷ καὶ ἀδιαιρέτῳ Τριάδι*).

**Patristic Note [St. John Chrysostom on Psalmody]:**  
That which the golden-tongued John said concerning the psalms in general and those of David in particular, we deem beneficial to append here for continual reading and study by every Christian, especially the clergy, for whom the Psalter is a sacred handbook:  
«For what reason was the psalm introduced into our life, and why is this prophecy uttered especially with song?... Listen. When God saw that many men were rather indolent, that they came unwillingly to spiritual reading and did not readily endure the labor it involves, wishing to make the labor more desirable and to alleviate the sense of toil, He blended melody with prophecy, in order that, delighted by the rhythm of the chant, all might with great eagerness offer up sacred hymns to Him. For nothing so uplifts the soul, giving it wings, freeing it from the earth, delivering it from the bonds of the body, inclining it toward the love of wisdom, and causing it to look down upon all earthly concerns, as harmonized song and divine melody composed in meter. To such an extent, indeed, is our nature drawn to songs and chants that even nursing infants, when weeping and distressed, are lulled to sleep thereby.... This also do women, travelers, laborers, and sailors, hastening to comfort the toil of their labors with song, knowing that if the soul hears a melodious chant, it is able more easily to bear all grievous and burdensome things.... From spiritual psalms, great is the profit, abundant the benefit, profound the sanctification, and a foundation for all true philosophy is established, both as the words purify the soul and as the Holy Spirit swiftly descends upon the soul that chants such hymns. For those who chant with understanding invoke the grace of the Spirit.... Where licentious songs are sung, demons congregate; but where spiritual songs resound, the grace of the Spirit alights, sanctifying both mouth and soul.... And just as many wealthy persons, filling a sponge with balsam, wipe their tables clean, so that if any stain remain from the food, it may be wiped away and leave the table clean; so let us also do, filling our mouth with spiritual melody instead of balsam, so that if any stain has arisen in the soul from gluttony, we may wipe it away through that melody, and standing together let us say: **"Thou, O Lord, hast made me glad through Thy deeds..."** [Psalm 91:5 LXX]. Thus those who summon David with his harp summon Christ through him within themselves. And where Christ is present, no demon dares to enter, nor even to look in passing; but peace, love, and all good things flow forth as from fountains.... Where there is a psalm, and prayer, and the choir of prophets, and a mind pleasing to God among the singers, one would not err in calling such a gathering a Church. Even if you do not understand the inner power of the words, nevertheless teach your mouth to speak the words; for the tongue is sanctified by the holy words when they are uttered with a willing mind. If we establish ourselves in this habit, neither deliberately nor through carelessness will we ever neglect this beautiful ministry, custom compelling us even against our will to perform this noble worship daily.» (St. John Chrysostom, *Homily on Psalm 41*, Migne, *PG*, vol. 55).

### Concerning the First Kathisma of the Psalter

**4.** At every Saturday Vespers, the First Kathisma of the Psalter [Psalms 1–8 LXX] is chanted,[^59] even if a Feast of the Mother of God or the commemoration of a celebrated Saint occurs. It is not chanted, however, on the following Feasts of the Lord (even if they should occur on a Sunday): the Feast of the Nativity of Christ, Theophany, and the Transfiguration, at which none of the Resurrection hymns are sung; likewise, it is not chanted on Pentecost Sunday, nor at the post-festal Vespers of Christmas and Theophany when these Feasts fall on a Saturday (although at these Vespers the Resurrection Stichera take precedence). On the following Feasts of the Lord—namely, Palm Sunday, the Sunday of St. Thomas, and the Feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross (when it falls on a Sunday)—the Psalter is chanted, but the Resurrection hymns are omitted.

**Patristic Catena on the Spiritual Power of Psalmody:**[^60]  
St. Basil the Great says: «He blended the delight of melody with divine doctrines, in order that through the pleasant and smooth sound upon our hearing, we might imperceptibly receive the profit of the words, according to the practice of wise physicians, who, when giving bitter medicines to drink to those of poor appetite, often smear the cup with honey.... A psalm is the calm of souls, the arbitrator of peace, quieting the turbid and surging thoughts.... A psalm produces friendship, unites the divided, reconciles enemies; to infants it is security, to youth in their prime an adornment, to the aged a consolation, to women their most fitting ornament, a respite from daily toil, the voice of the Church.» (*Homily on Psalm 1*).  
Theodoret of Cyrus: «For divine grace, having mingled profit with the sweetness of melody, offered to mankind a teaching thrice-desirable and worthy of love.»  
Gregory of Nyssa: «Since that which is natural is agreeable to our nature, and music has been shown to be natural to us, for this reason the great David blended melody with the contemplation of virtues, as though pouring the sweetness of honey upon sublime doctrines.»  
Behold the reasons why our holy Church holds the Psalms of David as the foundation of individual and public prayers, and why in ancient times all the Psalms were chanted, as today we chant Psalms 140, 141, and certain others.

[^48]: The Resurrectional Evlogitaria (*Εὐλογητάρια*, Psalm 118:12 LXX: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord, teach me Thy statutes"** / *Εὐλογητὸς εἶ, Κύριε, δίδαξόν με τὰ δικαιώματά σου* / *Благословенъ еси, Господи, научи мя оправданіемъ Твоимъ*), chanted in Tone 5 on Sundays, followed by the Ypakoe (*Ὑπακοή*), the Anavathmoi (*Ἀναβαθμοί*, the Hymns of Ascents based on the Gradual Psalms 119–133 LXX), the Prokimenon, and the reading of one of the eleven Resurrection Gospels of Matins (*Ἑωθινὸν Εὐαγγέλιον*), commemorating the manifestations of the risen Christ.

[^49]: The Matins Canon (*Κανών*), structured upon the nine biblical canticles (Odes / *ᾠδαί*). Violakis details the variable insertion of the Katavasiai (*Καταβασίαι*), the Small Ektenia and Kathisma after Ode 3, the Kontakion and Ikos (*Κοντάκιον καὶ Οἶκος*) after Ode 6, the Magnificat (*Μεγαλύνει ἡ ψυχή μου τὸν Κύριον*, Luke 1:46–55) with the refrain **"More honorable than the Cherubim"** (*Τὴν τιμιωτέραν τῶν Χερουβίμ*) preceding Ode 9, followed by the Exapostilaria (*Ἐξαποστειλάρια*), the Praises (Lauds / *Αἶνοι*, Psalms 148–150 LXX), and the solemn Great Doxology (*Δοξολογία Μεγάλη*).

[^50]: The Divine Liturgy (*Ἡ Θεία Λειτουργία*). Violakis outlines the statutory entrance into the Eucharistic liturgy, distinguishing the recitation of the Typika (Psalms 102 and 145 LXX) and the Beatitudes (*Μακαρισμοί*, Matthew 5:3–12) on ordinary Sundays from the Daily Antiphons and the Festal Antiphons of the Great Feasts. The rubrics govern the Little Entrance with the Holy Gospel Book, the singing of the Eisodikon (**"Come, let us worship and bow down before Christ..."** / *Δεῦτε προσκυνήσωμεν*), the appointed post-entrance Troparia and Kontakia, the Trisagion (*Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός*), the Epistle and Gospel readings, the Cherubic Hymn (*Χερουβικὸς Ὕμνος*), the hymn to the Mother of God **"It is truly meet"** (*Ἄξιόν ἐστιν*), the Communion Hymn (*Κοινωνικόν*), and the concluding dismissals.

[^51]: The annual cycles of the Byzantine liturgical year: The Menaion (*Μηναῖον*), comprising twelve volumes governing the immovable calendar from September 1 (the Indiction or Ecclesiastical New Year) through August 31; and the Triodion (*Τριῴδιον*), governing the ten-week movable penitential season preceding Holy Pascha. The Triodion spans from the pre-Lenten Sundays (Publican and Pharisee, Prodigal Son, Meatfare, and Cheesefare) through the forty days of Great Lent and Great and Holy Week, regulating the Great Canon of St. Andrew of Crete, the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, and Great Compline.

[^52]: The Pentecostarion (*Πεντηκοστάριον* / Paschal Florilegium / *Цвѣтная Тріодь*), the liturgical book regulating the fifty-day festive cycle extending from the Resurrection of Christ on Holy Pascha through the Sunday of All Saints (the First Sunday after Pentecost). This joyous season encompasses Bright Week (*Διακαινήσιμος Ἑβδομάς*), Antipascha (St. Thomas Sunday), the Sunday of the Myrrh-bearers, Mid-Pentecost (*Μεσοπεντηκοστή*), the Ascension of the Lord, and the Holy Fathers of the First Ecumenical Council in Nicaea (A.D. 325).

[^53]: The Appendix (*Παράρτημα τοῦ Τυπικοῦ*). This specialized section codifies patriarchal ceremonial rites and episcopal services unique to the Great Church of Constantinople: the Patriarchal celebrations of the Akathist Hymn and the Twelve Passion Gospels, Church Consecrations, the Enthronement and Return of the Ecumenical Patriarch, the Indiction service on September 1, liturgical schedules (*Horologion*), episcopal ordinations (*Cheirotonia*), conciliar concelebrations (*Sylleitourgon*), funeral and memorial services (*Mnemosyna*), and the *Eortodromion* (*Ἑορτοδρόμιον* / Table of Feasts), which mathematically harmonizes the eighteen immovable feasts occurring between January 11 and May 25 with the movable dates of the Triodion and Pentecostarion.


## Cohort 6 Footnotes

[^54]: The Ninth Hour (*Θ΄ Ὥρα*) in the Byzantine and Ruthenian synthesis. Commemorating the death of Christ upon the Cross at the ninth hour of the day (approx. 3:00 PM; Matthew 27:46–50), the Ninth Hour occupies a canonical pivot in the daily liturgical cycle. As the concluding office of the preceding liturgical day, it seals the diurnal services before Vespers introduces the liturgical commemoration of the following calendar day at sunset. Violakis codifies the exact structural rules for integrating the variable propers: when the day is a Feast of the Lord, of the Mother of God, or a celebrated Saint, the festal Troparion and Kontakion are recited; on simple weekdays without a festal Kontakion, the ancient penitential troparia are recited: **"The thief, beholding the Author of life"** (*Βλέπων ὁ Λῃστὴς τὸν ἀρχηγὸν τῆς ζωῆς*), followed by *Glory...*, **"In the midst of two thieves"** (*Ἐν μέσῳ δύο λῃστῶν*), and *Now and forever...*, **"The Lamb and Shepherd"** (*Τὸν Ἀμνὸν καὶ Ποιμένα*). On Sunday evening, the Octoechos Ypakoe of the Tone replaces the Kontakion. Throughout Bright Week and on the eve of the Ascension, the entire office is replaced by the Paschal Ninth Hour (*«Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ θεασάμενοι...»*), highlighting the uninterrupted festal joy of the Resurrection.

[^55]: The priest's liturgical preparation and vesting for the opening of Vespers (Violakis, p. 38, note 1). Before intoning the foundational exclamation **"Blessed is our God always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages"** (*Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν πάντοτε...*), the priest is canonically obligated to make three reverent prostrations (*metanias*) before the Holy Table, kiss the Holy Table and the hand-cross, and vest in the Epitrachilion (*ἐπιτραχήλιον*), reciting the prescribed vesting prayer. In accordance with the *Sluzhebnik* (*Ἱερατικόν*), the priest may not remove the Epitrachilion until the final dismissal of the service; Violakis strongly censures any laxity in this regard, declaring that a priest who fails to observe this order acts disorderly and unlawfully (*ἀτακτεῖ καὶ παρανομεῖ*).

[^56]: The canonical dispute regarding the opening exclamation of Bright Week Vespers (Violakis, p. 38, note 2). Violakis notes that ancient Sabbaite and Constantinopolitan Typika (including the Venetian printed Typikon of 1685) appoint the standard opening exclamation **"Blessed is our God always..."** (*Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν...*) even for the solemn Vespers of Bright Monday (the Agape Vespers), followed immediately by the Paschal troparion **"Christ is risen from the dead"** (*Χριστὸς ἀνέστη ἐκ νεκρῶν*) instead of the ferial invocations. Violakis classifies the modern practice of opening Paschal Vespers with the Trinitarian exclamation **"Glory to the Holy, Consubstantial, Life-Creating, and Undivided Trinity"** (*Δόξα τῇ Ἁγίᾳ καὶ Ὁμοουσίῳ...*) as an irregular post-Byzantine innovation (*παράτυπος νεωτερισμός*), though he acknowledges its virtually universal adoption in contemporary patriarchal, cathedral, and parish practice.

[^57]: The Introductory Psalm (*Προοιμιακὸς Ψαλμός*, Psalm 103 LXX: **"Bless the Lord, O my soul"** / *Εὐλόγει ἡ ψυχή μου τὸν Κύριον* / *Благослови, душе моя, Господа*). As the cosmic hymn of creation, Psalm 103 depicts the creation and providential sustenance of the universe by God the Word, setting the theological framework of the evening service as humanity's grateful contemplation of creation and longing for redemption. The psalm is omitted entirely only during Bright Week (from the Agape Vespers of Pascha Sunday through Bright Saturday) and at the Vespers of the Apodosis of Pascha, where the service opens directly with the Paschal Stichera and the Great Litany. From the Sunday of St. Thomas until the Apodosis of Pascha, the psalm is preceded not by the three-fold **"Come, let us worship"** (*Δεῦτε προσκυνήσωμεν*), but by **"Christ is risen"** chanted thrice (once by the clergy in the sanctuary and twice by the choirs).

[^58]: St. John Chrysostom's Homily on Psalm 41 [Psalm 41 LXX / Psalm 42 MT] (*Εἰς τὸν Ψαλμὸν ΜΑ΄*, PG 55:155–160; Violakis, pp. 40–41, note 3). Violakis appends this extensive patristic treatise to impress upon clergy and cantors the profound therapeutic, moral, and pneumatological efficacy of liturgical psalmody. Chrysostom argues that God, perceiving human weakness and reluctance toward spiritual instruction, mingled sweet melody with prophetic revelation, comparing this divine economy to wise physicians who smear the rim of a medicine cup with honey so that sick children will drink the curative potion. Sacred psalmody sanctifies the tongue, purges bodily passions, attracts the grace of the Holy Spirit, and repels demonic temptation, transforming both the temple and the domestic hearth into a true spiritual sanctuary (*Ἐκκλησία*).

[^59]: The First Kathisma of the Psalter (*Α΄ Κάθισμα τοῦ Ψαλτηρίου*, Psalms 1–8 LXX: **"Blessed is the man"** / *Μακάριος ἀνήρ* / *Блаженъ мужъ*) at Saturday Great Vespers. In the Sabbaite monastic tradition, the entire Psalter is chanted weekly, with the First Kathisma appointed for Saturday Vespers as the opening of the Sunday liturgical cycle. Violakis codifies the parish practice of the Great Church: the Kathisma is omitted whenever a Despotic Feast (Feast of the Lord) falls on Sunday—namely, the Nativity of Christ (December 25), Theophany (January 6), and the Transfiguration (August 6)—as well as on Pentecost Sunday, because the festive propers entirely replace the weekly psalmody. However, on Palm Sunday, St. Thomas Sunday, and the Exaltation of the Holy Cross falling on Sunday, the Kathisma is chanted, while the Resurrectional hymns of the Octoechos are omitted.

[^60]: The First Stasis of the Psalter (*Α΄ Στάσις τοῦ Ψαλτηρίου*, Psalms 1–3 LXX) on weekday feasts, accompanied by the Patristic Catena from Basil the Great, Theodoret of Cyrus, and Gregory of Nyssa (Violakis, pp. 42–43). On Feasts of the Mother of God and Polyeleos-rank Saints falling on Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, or Friday, the first stasis of the First Kathisma (Psalms 1–3 LXX: **"Blessed is the man"** / *Μακάριος ἀνήρ*) is chanted at Vespers. The patristic catena emphasizes: St. Basil (*Homily on Psalm 1*, PG 29:209–212) defining psalmody as the calm of souls, arbitrator of peace, and voice of the Church; Theodoret of Cyrus (*Interpretatio in Psalmos*, PG 80:860) praising divine grace that blended pleasure with spiritual profit; and Gregory of Nyssa (*In Inscriptiones Psalmorum*, PG 44:433) demonstrating how David blended melody with virtue to pour sweet honey upon sublime doctrines.

[^61]: The dual liturgical character of the Meeting of the Lord (*Ὑπαπαντὴ τοῦ Κυρίου*, February 2) and the Annunciation (*Εὐαγγελισμὸς τῆς Θεοτόκου*, March 25) (Violakis, pp. 44–45, note 4). Although historically grouped among the Feasts of the Mother of God, both feasts commemorate events central to the Incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ, and therefore possess the liturgical rank, privileges, and structural characteristics of Feasts of the Lord (*Δεσποτικαὶ ἑορταί*). Consequently, when either feast falls on a Saturday or Monday, the First Stasis of the Psalter is omitted at Vespers, in accordance with the statutory rule governing Despotic Feasts.

[^62]: The Lucernarium Psalmody (*Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*, Psalms 140, 141, 129, and 116 LXX) and the evening censing ritual (Violakis, pp. 44–45, note 5). In daily Vespers, all verses of the Lucernarium psalms are chanted in the appointed Tone of the week [Tone 1–8]. On Great Feasts, parish practice omits the intermediate verses from **"Set, O Lord, a watch before my mouth"** (*Θοῦ, Κύριε, φυλακήν*), jumping directly to the verses matching the number of appointed Stichera (10, 8, or 6). When the Second Choir begins the second verse, **"Let my prayer be set forth before Thee as incense"** (*Κατευθυνθήτω ἡ προσευχή μου*), the priest blesses incense and censes the Holy Table on all four sides, the sanctuary, the iconostasis, and the entire congregation. If an Entrance (*Εἴσοδος*) with the censer is appointed, the priest vests in the Phelonion (*φελώνιον*) at the beginning of *Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*; if no Entrance is appointed, he censes wearing only the Epitrachilion.

[^63]: The canonical precedence of the Resurrectional Octoechos on Sundays. Throughout the entire liturgical year, Sunday services (Saturday Great Vespers and Sunday Matins) belong primarily to the celebration of the Lord's Resurrection. The Resurrectional hymns of the Octoechos (Stichera, Doxastika, Dismissal Troparia, Kathismata, Ypakoe, Anavathmoi, Canons, Kontakia, Ikoses, Exapostilaria, Eothina Gospels, and Beatitudes) hold absolute priority, preceding the propers of the Mother of God and the commemorations of Saints. Violakis codifies the exact hierarchical ranking governing how festal, post-festal, and sanctoral hymns are intercalated with the weekly Resurrectional propers.
