## IN LOVING MEMORY OF OUR PARENTS AND GRANDPARENTS

**Christ Economou and Sophia Economou**  
**Reverend Antonios Kalogeropoulos and Presvytera Katina Kalogeropoulos**[^5]

Christ Economou was born in Rendina, Greece in 1890. His grandfather, the village priest, placed him at the psaltiri (chanter’s stand) around the age of nine. Left legally blind by a high fever, Christ memorized from hearing what other chanters had learned from books. Arriving in America at age 21, he served the church in every city in which he lived, always without remuneration for himself and without reimbursement for the materials he purchased for the choir or the chanters. In 1913, Christ became the First Chanter in the “new” church of Sheboygan, Wisconsin. He went on to serve in churches in Washington State, California, Texas, and finally Tulsa, Oklahoma. In Tulsa, Christ organized a women’s choir, possibly the first women’s Greek Orthodox choir in America. Later, men joined and it became a typical four-part choir. He recruited and trained 15 other chanters during his tenure and continued as First Chanter until his death in 1973. During his 74 years of service to the church, Christ missed only two Sundays, and that was because of illness. His devotion and stewardship were recognized by Bishops and Archbishops, and he received the title of Tonsured Anagnostis.

*(Printed Page v)*

=== LEAF p8 ===

Although Christ Economou [the cantor] had no formal music training in his youth, he studied Byzantine music most of his life, and received vocal and instrumental music lessons throughout his time in America. He constantly transcribed Byzantine music into Western notation. In 1945, Christ wrote a concise version of the Typikon. The small book spelled out the important points of the services and pointed out mistakes in the original Violakis Typikon. If there were questions or differences in a section, he suggested how they could be resolved.

The study of the Typikon was a passion for my father, Christ Economou. That is why my family and I wanted to see this book published. This scholarly opus, the new bilingual Typikon, is a polychronion of effort and achievement by talented writers blessed by the Holy Spirit. I think it is fitting that the spiritual vision of a man who was physically blind should be realized in such a glorious way.

**James Economou**

# INTRODUCTION

The present work makes available to the Greek Orthodox Church in America in English translation the most authoritative guide to the order of its services, namely the *Typikon of the Great Church of Christ* by George Violakis, Head Chanter (Protopsaltis) of the Patriarchate of Constantinople at the end of the 19th century.

This treatise was composed after extensive study and deliberation by a committee consisting of four hierarchs and the Head Chanter George Violakis, which was appointed by the Holy Synod of the Patriarchate of Constantinople. It was written in its final form by Violakis himself and published in Constantinople in 1888. Thereafter, the Holy Patriarchate established the Violakis Typikon as the sole authoritative document outlining and stipulating the order (*taxis*) of all the services celebrated within the Church.

Throughout the centuries, the Church has made a continuous effort to make its services more inspiring to its worshipers, thus producing over time several Typika, each one amplifying and refining its predecessor. This sustained effort must have been inspired by St. Paul’s exhortation to us how to conduct our lives as well as how to worship God in His temple: «Πάντα δὲ εὐσχημόνως καὶ κατὰ τάξιν γινέσθω» (“Let all things be done decently and in order.”)[^6] 1 Corinthians 14:40.

It was this desire to preserve and perpetuate the order and beauty of our church services as an intrinsic, vital part of the experience of worship that impelled Dr. George T. Demos, a devoted, highly trained church musician (a medical physician by profession) to republish the Typikon by George Violakis. He wanted to reassert its sacramental authority and bring it anew to the awareness of our Church in this country by casting the book into a bilingual format, the English translation aligned with and facing the original Greek text exactly, page by opposing page. To make the work more meaningful, Dr. Demos added a dictionary of terms related to the Typikon, which he assiduously compiled and adapted from various sources.

To make the work’s contents more accessible to today’s user, Dr. Demos and I updated the complicated section where the yearly shifting Movable Feasts coincide variously with the Immovable Feasts, whose dates

*(Printed Page vii)*

=== LEAF p10 ===

have been fixed for all time. Since the original Violakis Typikon was based on the Julian Calendar, we put next to each Julian date its exactly corresponding date of the Gregorian Calendar, which is in use in the Church today. See the Chart of the Feasts (Πίναξ τοῦ Ἑορτοδρομίου), pp. 924 ff. [For the order of service as well as the hymnology (text and music) for the liturgies of the Sundays and Immovable Feasts coinciding variously with the Movable Feasts, see Theodore Bogdanos, *The Byzantine Liturgy: Hymnology and Order*, Denver, Colorado, 1993, pp. 283 ff.][^7]

The English translation was undertaken by Rev. Robert Athas, who, as both scholar and practicing liturgist, brought to this demanding task an expert, intimate knowledge of Orthodox ritual. Fr. Athas had already translated the Triodion section in the Violakis Typikon as his thesis for his Master of Divinity degree at the St. Vladimir Theological Seminary in New York. In time, I was asked by Dr. Demos to contribute to the further refinement of the translation and the work’s editorial form.

George Demos immersed himself in this project for more than 16 years. He defied physical ailments and protracted therapies — even the threat of impending sudden death — in order to finish this work for the Church. It was in the early hours of the morning that he passed away while inserting some final touches into his computer.

After his death, I proceeded to put the work into its final form as we both had conceived it. Its publication was most generously undertaken by Poti Doukas (an aerospace engineer), a gifted member of the Choir of the Assumption Cathedral in Denver, Colorado, and Dr. George Stefanidakis (a retired chemical engineer), a member of the choir and a chanter at Annunciation Cathedral in Houston, Texas. Through his computer expertise, Dr. Stefanidakis carried out several difficult corrections and updated the entire process of printing the final version. In the meantime, James Economou, a former choir director of the Church, in an extraordinary gesture of generosity, had offered to fully finance the publication of the work.

Finally, those of us who have collaborated on this project bring now this book to God as an offering, laying it like a piece of woven, multicolored tapestry at the footsteps of His throne.

**Theodore Bogdanos**

# INTRODUCTION TO THE ENGLISH TRANSLATION

In 1985, the Denver Diocese Choir Federation held its annual conference in Tulsa, Oklahoma. I was the Proistamenos of the Holy Trinity parish there. Sitting in my office during the Conference one afternoon, chatting with some of the conferees, Dr. George Demos scanned the long wall of bookshelves. His eyes settled on a copy of the thesis I wrote for my Master of Divinity. The thesis was titled “The Triodion in the Typicon of the Great Church.”[^12] George asked if I would like to translate the rest of the Typikon. Not realizing what was involved, I agreed, and twenty years later this volume is the result.

It was not just a work of sitting down with a dictionary but a real collaboration that gave me the opportunity for wonderful conversations with Dr. Demos and with Dr. Theodore Bogdanos. Besides the work of parish and family life which would take my attention for most of the time, the hardest part of this task was remembering that we were in a four-hour difference in time zones. The easiest part was the joy of sharing our mutual love of the Typikon and our excitement in discovering footnotes to the footnotes! And comparing various Typika: Jerusalem, etc.

George Demos’ best direction to me was his insistence that “If you can make me understand it, I know we are on the right track.” I only regret that George did not live to see this work completed. I remember him with great fondness both for his humor and his humanity and his great love for the Church and for this great work of love. May his memory be eternal! Now, my hope is that this English translation of the Typikon will be helpful to all priests, choir directors, chanters and other church musicians.

The Typikon ends with the exclamation: «Τέλος καὶ τῷ Θεῷ δόξα!» (“The end and glory to God!”)[^13] It is truly God Who is the author and finisher of all things and to Him be honor and glory unto the ages of ages! Amen!

**Rev. Robert T. Athas**  
Protopresbyter

# GUIDELINES AND OBSERVATIONS ON THE PREVIOUS EDITIONS OF THE TYPIKON

The synodically appointed five-member Committee consisting of the Reverend Metropolitans of Ainos, Samos, Caesarea, Mytiline and the Protopsaltis [highest-ranking chanter] of the Great Church George Violakis, which reviewed the previous Typika of the Church—such as that by the Protopsaltis Constantine—which are in use today, corrected in its report the errors and inaccuracies of these works as follows:

Occasionally, their various guidelines, which are scattered in many places in their work, differ on what is appropriate to be performed on the commemorations [feasts] of the saints. Every disagreement, therefore, must cease to exist, every doubt must be clearly articulated and corrected, however secondary or minor the contradiction or variation may be.

The 9th Hour of every day, continuing the 1st, 3rd, and 6th [Hours] and belonging to that day, which closes ecclesiastically before Vespers, must follow the order that the Committee has prescribed in an analogous situation with the 9th Hour being done before the Vespers of the Sunday of Thomas[^18] as well as before the Vespers of Wednesday and Thursday before the Feast of the Ascension.

## ΕΡΜΗΝΕΙΑ ΖΩΝΑΡΑ

Ἡ ἐν ταῖς ἐκκλησίαις γινομένη ψαλμῳδία παράκλησίς ἐστι πρὸς Θεόν, δεομένων ἡμῶν ἵλεων ἔσεσθαι τὸ θεῖον ἡμῖν, ἐφ' οἷς αὐτῷ προσκεκρούκαμεν. Οἱ δὲ παρακαλοῦντές τε καὶ δεόμενοι, ταπεινὸν ἦθος καὶ κατανενυγμένον ἔχειν ὀφείλουσι· τὸ δὲ βοᾶν καὶ κραυγάζειν, οὐ κατεσταλμένου ἤθους, ἀλλὰ θρασέος ἐστὶ καὶ γεγαυρωμένου. ## FROM THE SIXTH ECUMENICAL SYNOD IN TRULLO [A.D. 691]

“We will that those whose office it is to sing in the churches do not use undisciplined vociferations, nor force nature to shouting, nor adopt any of those modes which are incongruous and unsuitable for the church: but that they offer the psalmody to God, Who is the observer of secrets, with great attention and compunction. At this point the Typikon presents exegetical comments from three great 12th-century scholars of Canon Law: John Zonaras, Theodore Valsamon, and Alexios Aristinos. Because of the repetitive, idiomatic, and often obscure language therein, only a summary of the key points of these comments is given here.

## EXEGETICAL COMMENT BY ZONARAS

Zonaras relates the chanters of his time to the sons of Israel, all being the children of God, and holds their reverence and piety in the act of worship as a model to be emulated by the chanters of Zonaras’s own time. Since the chanting in church is essentially a supplication to God, the chanters should sing in a humble and penitent manner, avoiding self-exhibition with musical extravagance such as excessive embellishments or loud voices distracting the worshiper. At the same time, Zonaras insists on good musicianship, condemning formless, arhythmical melodies. The music itself should be appropriate to the sacred place, avoiding any secular or erotic associations. He pleads for solemnity as well as artistic unity in the act of worship.[^32]

## EXEGETICAL COMMENT BY VALSAMON

Valsamon reminds us that churches are houses of prayer. The faithful should pray to God with contrition and humility, avoiding extravagant, unbecoming deportment or attire. Similarly, those who take an active part in performing a liturgical event—clergymen and chanters—should avoid musical self-exhibition as well as theatrical effects in their ritual movement that do not fit the holy and solemn sacred occasion.

Like Zonaras, Valsamon invokes Mosaic law and the piety of the sons of Israel as an example for his time. He goes on to state that his contemporaries, having been enlightened by their faith as Christians, should be even more reverent than their Israeli counterparts. To further authenticate his plea for reverence, he refers to the 98th Canon of the Council in Carthage [A.D. 418 or 419] and to a celebratory homily by St. John Chrysostom on the proper manner of worship.[^33]

## EXEGETICAL COMMENT BY ARISTINOS

“The disordered voice of a chanter is not acceptable.” Invoking even the tradition of Israel (and by implication the manner of worship in David’s Tent), Aristinos castigates “loud, jagged voices” that distract the worshiper from meditation and from prayerful reverence toward God, for God knows the silent, secret thoughts of His creatures; He does not need this tawdry loudness.[^34]

### [A.D. 759-826]

Our Holy Father Theodore the Studite addresses chanters and canonarchs, exhorting them to refine and further spiritualize their tasks. He first reminds them of the lofty, sacramental character of their ministry. In chanting and reciting, they should use a clear, well-rounded voice, following Saint Paul’s exhortation that everything in the act of divine worship should be done decently and in order (*kata taxin*). The chanter should exercise restraint and avoid extravagance, such as singing a very high note and then, for theatrical effect, descending suddenly to a very low note. The good chanter should help other aspiring chanters to develop their God-given talent. Thus, God will compensate the chanter’s humanly limited service with infinite blessings.

## ON CHANTERS

Violakis cites the 15th Canon of the Council of Laodicea [A.D. 364], which strictly forbids anyone to chant in church services except canonical chanters who are numbered among the clergy and appointed to this ministry. To avoid disorder and musical confusion (*chasmodia*), only highly trained and experienced chanters are permitted to sing, in order to create ecclesiastical order (*eutaxia*) and evoke in the faithful holy compunction and reverence.[^40]

## GUIDING CRITERIA FOR CHANTERS

1. Chanters should be selected only if they harmonize with one another in vocal timbre and in musical artistry.

2. Isokrates (drone holders) and canonarchs should be appointed, at least three for each choir, and they must rehearse twice or three times a week.

## Cohort 3 Footnotes

[^20]: Archon Protopsaltis Constantine Byzantios, in his 1838 and 1851 editions of the Typikon, had curtailed the commemoration of celebrated saints occurring on Sunday by prescribing solely the Sunday Resurrection Kathismata across the first two Psalter stichologies of Matins (*Orthros*), postponing any Kathisma of the saint to the conclusion of the second stichology or omitting it altogether (as for Prophet Elijah or Saint Marina). The 1888 Patriarchal Commission of George Violakis reformed this curtailment by intercalating one Kathisma of the celebrating saint into each of the first two stichologies and chanting the post-Polyeleos Kathismata after the third stichology, thereby restoring the ancient cathedral and monastic balance between the Sunday Resurrectional office and the Menaion feast.

[^21]: The 1838 Typikon of Constantine frequently employed the abbreviated rubric "the Beatitudes are said" (*λέγονται οἱ Μακαρισμοί*) or "and so forth" (*καὶ καθεξῆς*), without explicitly directing whether the troparia of Ode 6 of the Saint's canon were to be appended to the Resurrection Beatitudes (as on the 2nd and 5th Sundays of Great Lent) or omitted. This liturgical ellipsis caused recurring perplexity among parish clergy and cantors; the 1888 Violakis Typikon resolves this ambiguity by providing detailed rubrical specifications for every liturgical case.

[^22]: The Sunday Midnight Office (*Mesonyktikon* / *Полунощница*) celebrates Christ's nocturnal Resurrection from the dead. While the Great Horologion (*Mega Horologion*) directs that the Ypakoe (*Ὑπακοή*) of the Tone of the week be chanted on Sundays in place of the weekday penitential troparia, Constantine Byzantios had prescribed the three penitential troparia (*"Have mercy on us, Lord, have mercy on us"*) on all Sundays throughout the liturgical year. The 1888 Violakis Commission restored the Ypakoe for all ordinary Sundays and Sundays of the Pentecostarion, restricting the three penitential troparia strictly to the penitential cycle of the Lenten Triodion.

[^23]: *Chasmodia* (*χασμῳδία*), denoting in classical Greek rhetoric and liturgical musicology a hiatus, audible disharmony, or choir disorder caused by singers scrambling through disparate liturgical volumes (Menaia, Festal Horologion, Anthologia), was a persistent problem in nineteenth-century parish choirs. By printing the complete texts of the Festal Antiphons and their corresponding Megalynaria directly in the body of the Typikon, the Commission ensured smooth and uninterrupted celebration of the Divine Liturgy.

[^24]: Question 1 submitted by the 1888 Patriarchal Commission to Ecumenical Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod concerned the calendar concurrence when December 24 (Paramoni of the Nativity of Christ) falls on a Sunday. Under this concurrence, the Royal Hours are transferred to Friday and the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom is celebrated on Sunday morning. Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod formally ratified the rule that the Sunday Resurrectional hymns are omitted at Saturday Great Vespers to preserve the solemnity of the Paramoni. The 1685 Venetian edition cited by the Commission is the *Typikon of the Ecclesiastical Service* (*Τυπικὸν τῆς ἐκκλησιαστικῆς ἀκολουθίας*).

[^25]: Question 2 resolved the selection of the Troparion for the Royal Hours of the Nativity and Theophany when either feast occurs on a Sunday or Monday. Because the Royal Hours cannot be celebrated on a Saturday or Sunday, they are anticipated on Friday morning. The Holy Synod decreed that on that Friday the Forefeast Troparia are chanted (*"Make ready, Bethlehem"* / *"Make ready, Zabulon"*) rather than the proper Troparia of the Eve (*"When recorded in the census"* / *"The Jordan River turned back"*).

[^26]: Question 3 addressed the Paschal Entrance Hymn (*Eisodikon* / *Входный стих*) *«Ἐν ἐκκλησίαις εὐλογεῖτε τὸν Θεόν, Κύριον ἐκ πηγῶν Ἰσραήλ»* (Psalm 67:27 LXX: *"In assemblies bless God, the Lord from the fountains of Israel"*). Although rubrics in certain printed editions of the Pentikostarion suggested reverting to the ordinary Little Entrance chant *«Δεῦτε προσκυνήσωμεν»* (*"Come, let us worship"*) on weekdays following Thomas Sunday, Patriarch Dionysios V and the Synod established that the proper Paschal Eisodikon must be chanted at every Divine Liturgy celebrated through the Apodosis of Pascha (*Ἀπόδοσις*).

[^27]: Question 4 addressed the abusive practice in nineteenth-century Constantinopolitan parishes of omitting the singing of the **"Alleluia"** (*Ἀλληλούϊα*) and its accompanying psalm verses after the reading of the Acts of the Apostles during Bright Week (*Diakainesimos*). Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod reaffirmed the ancient order of the Pentikostarion, ruling that the chanting of the Alleluia with its appointed verses is mandatory and must not be omitted.

[^28]: Question 5 resolved the initial sacerdotal exclamation opening Vespers and Matins throughout Bright Week. Older typika and the 1685 Venice edition appointed the ordinary blessing *«Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν»* (*"Blessed is our God"*), whereas the 1838 Typikon of Constantine had introduced the solemn initial doxology *«Δόξα τῇ ἁγίᾳ καὶ ὁμοουσίῳ καὶ ζωοποιῷ καὶ ἀδιαιρέτῳ Τριάδι»* (*"Glory to the holy, and consubstantial, and life-creating, and undivided Trinity"*). The Holy Synod ratified the use of *«Δόξα τῇ ἁγίᾳ...»* as the initial blessing for all daily offices during Bright Week, reflecting the continuous solemnity of the Paschal Vigil.

[^29]: Question 6 addressed the appointment of Pentecost hymnody during holy ordinations (*cheirotoniai* / *рукоположения*). The Patriarchal Archieratikon directs that during the candidate's triple circumambulation of the Holy Table (*Agia Trapeza*), the choir chants appointed troparia from Pentecost alongside the traditional ordination hymns (*"Holy Martyrs"*, *"Glory to Thee, Christ God"*, *"Rejoice, O Isaiah"*). The Holy Synod entrusted the definitive rubrical codification of these hymns to the Commission itself.

[^30]: Question 7 concerned the liturgical hymn chanted immediately following the canonical election of an Ecumenical Patriarch. Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod rejected this proposed change, directing that the time-honored Marian hymn be preserved in accordance with established patriarchal tradition (*«διότι οὕτω συνειθίσθη»*).

[^31]: Canon LXXV (75) of the Council in Trullo (the Quinisext Ecumenical Council, convoked in Constantinople in 691–692 AD under Emperor Justinian II) constitutes the fundamental canonical legislation of the Eastern Orthodox Church governing church singing.
