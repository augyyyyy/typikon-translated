

## Cohort 1 Footnotes

[^1]: The *Typikon of the Great Church of Christ* (*Τυπικὸν τῆς τοῦ Χριστοῦ Μεγάλης Ἐκκλησίας*), compiled by George Violakis (Protopsaltis of the Ecumenical Patriarchate) and published in Constantinople in 1888 under Patriarch Dionysios V, represents the definitive codification of the Neo-Sabaitic cathedral and parochial rite for the Greek-speaking Orthodox world. It superseded the earlier 1838 and 1851 editions compiled by Constantine the Protopsaltis.

[^2]: The frontispiece reproduces the famous late ninth-century lunette mosaic over the Imperial Door leading from the inner narthex into the nave of Hagia Sophia. Christ Pantokrator is enthroned in glory, with His right hand raised in blessing and His left holding an open Gospel codex with the Johannine incipits: *«Εἰρήνη ὑμῖν. Ἐγώ εἰμι τὸ φῶς τοῦ κόσμου»* (John 20:19, 26; John 8:12). Emperor Leo VI ("the Wise", r. 886–912) kneels in humble proskynesis before His feet, imploring divine mercy.

[^3]: Constantine Byzantios (d. 1862), Archon Protopsaltis of the Great Church of Christ, published the first official reformed patriarchal Typikon in Constantinople in 1838, re-edited in 1851. Violakis’s 1888 edition thoroughly systematized and revised Constantine’s texts with synodal authority.

[^4]: Protopresbyter Robert T. Athas (in Greek, Epameinondas Th. Athasakos) completed his translation of the Lenten Triodion rubrics of Violakis at St. Vladimir’s Orthodox Theological Seminary (Crestwood, NY) prior to this comprehensive collaborative edition.

[^5]: Presvytera (Greek: *Πρεσβυτέρα*) is the traditional title of respect accorded to the wife of an Eastern Christian presbyter (priest), corresponding to Church Slavonic *Panimatka* or *Matushka*.

[^6]: 1 Corinthians 14:40: *«πάντα δὲ εὐσχημόνως καὶ κατὰ τάξιν γινέσθω»*. In the liturgical tradition of the Christian East, this apostolic precept forms the foundational theological rationale for the *taxis* (Slavonic: *chyn*, *ustav*) governing divine worship.

[^7]: Theodore Bogdanos (Professor Emeritus, University of Georgia) was a distinguished musicologist, scholar, and protopsaltis whose manual *The Byzantine Liturgy: Hymnology and Order* (Denver, 1993) provided the musical and rubrical framework for liturgical synthesis between the Julian and Gregorian calendar reckonings.

[^11]: The traditional liturgical prayer for the reposed (*Aionia i mnimi* / *Вѣчная память* / *Memory Eternal*) derives from Psalm 111:6 (LXX) and the Orthodox Funeral and Memorial Services (*Mnemosynon* / *Panakhyda*). It is inscribed here as an episcopal blessing upon the late Dr. George T. Demos (1934–2012), editor and compiler of this bilingual edition.

[^12]: The Lenten Triodion (*Τριῴδιον*) contains the liturgical propers from the Sunday of the Publican and Pharisee through Holy Saturday. Fr. Athas's thesis critically examined how George Violakis codified the ancient cathedral rite of Constantinople for parish use, synthesizing the monastic Typikon of Saint Sabbas (*Hagio-Sabaitic* synthesis) with the urban pastoral needs of nineteenth-century parishes.

[^13]: *«Τέλος καὶ τῷ Θεῷ δόξα!»* ("The end and glory to God!") is the time-honored Greek scribal doxological formula (*teleutaios stichos*), universally appended by Byzantine copyists, liturgists, and typikographers at the explicit of a completed codex, affirming that divine wisdom is both the source and terminus of all sacred labor.

[^14]: The Greek half-title *ΜΕΤΑΦΡΑΣΙΣ ΤΥΠΙΚΟΥ* (*Translation of the Typikon*) formally introduces the core liturgical translation section of the work, separating the preliminary front matter and introductory commentaries from the operative rubrical text.

[^15]: This phonetic table reflects the modern standard Greek (Demotic/Katharevousa) pronunciation system employed across the Ecumenical Patriarchate and the Greek Orthodox Archdiocese of America, differing fundamentally from the reconstructed classical Erasmian phonology by observing iotacism (whereby *η, ι, υ, ει, οι, υι* converge upon the vowel sound [i]) and soft voiced spirants.

[^16]: The editorial convention establishes a rigorous, three-tier typographic taxonomy: standard parentheses `(...)` preserve the original 1888 text of George Violakis; curly brackets `{...}` designate translator additions inserted by Fr. Robert Athas to elucidate elliptic rubrics; and square brackets `[...]` contain critical expansions, biblical citations, and cross-references contributed by editors Dr. George Demos and Dr. Theodore Bogdanos.

[^17]: The Patriarchal Synodal Commission of 1888 was convoked under Ecumenical Patriarch Dionysios V to undertake a comprehensive critical review of the existing liturgical typika in active use—principally the 1838 and 1851 editions compiled by Archon Protopsaltis Constantine Byzantios. Composed of four senior metropolitans (Methodios of Ainos, Gabriel of Samos, Procopios of Caesarea, and Methodios of Mytiline) alongside Archon Protopsaltis George Violakis, the Commission resolved hundreds of contradictions and ambiguities between printed books and lived patriarchal cathedral practice.

[^18]: The Ninth Hour (*Enati Ora* / *Девятый час*), commemorating the Death of Christ upon the Cross at 3:00 PM, constitutes the canonical capstone of the daily daytime cursus. Because the liturgical day ends at sunset, the Ninth Hour belongs to the preceding day. The Commission codified its mandatory celebration prior to Great Vespers on the Sunday of St. Thomas (Antipascha) and before the Wednesday and Thursday Vespers of the Leavetaking of Pascha and the Ascension, establishing rubrical symmetry across the Paschal season.

[^19]: The Festal Dismissal (*Apolysis*) on Great Feasts of the Lord (*Despotikai Heortai*) takes the identical proper invocation at both Great Vespers and the Divine Liturgy. Archon Protopsaltis Constantine had argued in his 1838/1851 editions that the Resurrection formula *«Ὁ ἀναστὰς ἐκ νεκρῶν»* was strictly forbidden at Sunday of All Saints Vespers because Christ's Resurrection occurred at midnight. The 1888 Commission formally overturned Constantine's restriction, decreeing that Sunday Vespers participates in the resurrectional glory of the Lord's Day.


## Cohort 3 Footnotes

[^20]: Archon Protopsaltis Constantine Byzantios, in his 1838 and 1851 editions of the Typikon, had curtailed the commemoration of celebrated saints occurring on Sunday by prescribing solely the Sunday Resurrection Kathismata across the first two Psalter stichologies of Matins (*Orthros*), postponing any Kathisma of the saint to the conclusion of the second stichology or omitting it altogether (as for Prophet Elijah or Saint Marina). The 1888 Patriarchal Commission of George Violakis reformed this curtailment by intercalating one Kathisma of the celebrating saint into each of the first two stichologies and chanting the post-Polyeleos Kathismata after the third stichology, thereby restoring the ancient cathedral and monastic balance between the Sunday Resurrectional office and the Menaion feast.

[^21]: The 1838 Typikon of Constantine frequently employed the abbreviated rubric "the Beatitudes are said" (*λέγονται οἱ Μακαρισμοί*) or "and so forth" (*καὶ καθεξῆς*), without explicitly directing whether the troparia of Ode 6 of the Saint's canon were to be appended to the Resurrection Beatitudes (as on the 2nd and 5th Sundays of Great Lent) or omitted. This liturgical ellipsis caused recurring perplexity among parish clergy and cantors; the 1888 Violakis Typikon resolves this ambiguity by providing detailed rubrical specifications for every liturgical case.

[^22]: The Sunday Midnight Office (*Mesonyktikon* / *Полунощница*) celebrates Christ's nocturnal Resurrection from the dead. While the Great Horologion (*Mega Horologion*) directs that the Ypakoe (*Ὑπακοή*) of the Tone of the week be chanted on Sundays in place of the weekday penitential troparia, Constantine Byzantios had prescribed the three penitential troparia (*"Have mercy on us, Lord, have mercy on us"*) on all Sundays throughout the liturgical year. The 1888 Violakis Commission restored the Ypakoe for all ordinary Sundays and Sundays of the Pentecostarion, restricting the three penitential troparia strictly to the penitential cycle of the Lenten Triodion.

[^23]: *Chasmodia* (*χασμῳδία*), denoting in classical Greek rhetoric and liturgical musicology a hiatus, audible disharmony, or choir disorder caused by singers scrambling through disparate liturgical volumes (Menaia, Festal Horologion, Anthologia), was a persistent problem in nineteenth-century parish choirs. By printing the complete texts of the Festal Antiphons and their corresponding Megalynaria directly in the body of the Typikon, the Commission ensured smooth and uninterrupted celebration of the Divine Liturgy.

[^24]: Question 1 submitted by the 1888 Patriarchal Commission to Ecumenical Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod concerned the calendar concurrence when December 24 (Paramoni of the Nativity of Christ) falls on a Sunday. Under this concurrence, the Royal Hours are transferred to Friday and the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom is celebrated on Sunday morning. Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod formally ratified the rule that the Sunday Resurrectional hymns are omitted at Saturday Great Vespers to preserve the solemnity of the Paramoni. The 1685 Venetian edition cited by the Commission is the *Typikon of the Ecclesiastical Service* (*Τυπικὸν τῆς ἐκκλησιαστικῆς ἀκολουθίας*).

[^25]: Question 2 resolved the selection of the Troparion for the Royal Hours of the Nativity and Theophany when either feast occurs on a Sunday or Monday. Because the Royal Hours cannot be celebrated on a Saturday or Sunday, they are anticipated on Friday morning. The Holy Synod decreed that on that Friday the Forefeast Troparia are chanted (*"Make ready, Bethlehem"* / *"Make ready, Zabulon"*) rather than the proper Troparia of the Eve (*"When recorded in the census"* / *"The Jordan River turned back"*).

[^26]: Question 3 addressed the Paschal Entrance Hymn (*Eisodikon* / *Входный стих*) *«Ἐν ἐκκλησίαις εὐλογεῖτε τὸν Θεόν, Κύριον ἐκ πηγῶν Ἰσραήλ»* (Psalm 67:27 LXX: *"In assemblies bless God, the Lord from the fountains of Israel"*). Although rubrics in certain printed editions of the Pentikostarion suggested reverting to the ordinary Little Entrance chant *«Δεῦτε προσκυνήσωμεν»* (*"Come, let us worship"*) on weekdays following Thomas Sunday, Patriarch Dionysios V and the Synod established that the proper Paschal Eisodikon must be chanted at every Divine Liturgy celebrated through the Apodosis of Pascha (*Ἀπόδοσις*).

[^27]: Question 4 addressed the abusive practice in nineteenth-century Constantinopolitan parishes of omitting the singing of the **"Alleluia"** (*Ἀλληλούϊα*) and its accompanying psalm verses after the reading of the Acts of the Apostles during Bright Week (*Diakainesimos*). Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod reaffirmed the ancient order of the Pentikostarion, ruling that the chanting of the Alleluia with its appointed verses is mandatory and must not be omitted.

[^28]: Question 5 resolved the initial sacerdotal exclamation opening Vespers and Matins throughout Bright Week. Older typika and the 1685 Venice edition appointed the ordinary blessing *«Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν»* (*"Blessed is our God"*), whereas the 1838 Typikon of Constantine had introduced the solemn initial doxology *«Δόξα τῇ ἁγίᾳ καὶ ὁμοουσίῳ καὶ ζωοποιῷ καὶ ἀδιαιρέτῳ Τριάδι»* (*"Glory to the holy, and consubstantial, and life-creating, and undivided Trinity"*). The Holy Synod ratified the use of *«Δόξα τῇ ἁγίᾳ...»* as the initial blessing for all daily offices during Bright Week, reflecting the continuous solemnity of the Paschal Vigil.

[^29]: Question 6 addressed the appointment of Pentecost hymnody during holy ordinations (*cheirotoniai* / *рукоположения*). The Patriarchal Archieratikon directs that during the candidate's triple circumambulation of the Holy Table (*Agia Trapeza*), the choir chants appointed troparia from Pentecost alongside the traditional ordination hymns (*"Holy Martyrs"*, *"Glory to Thee, Christ God"*, *"Rejoice, O Isaiah"*). The Holy Synod entrusted the definitive rubrical codification of these hymns to the Commission itself.

[^30]: Question 7 concerned the liturgical hymn chanted immediately following the canonical election of an Ecumenical Patriarch. The Commission recommended substituting the traditional Marian Megalynarion *«Ἄξιόν ἐστιν ὡς ἀληθῶς μακαρίζειν σὲ τὴν Θεοτόκον»* (*"It is truly right to bless thee, O Theotokos"*) with the Trinitarian hymn *«Ἄξιόν ἐστιν ὡς ἀληθῶς τὴν Ὑπέρθεον ὑμνεῖν Τριάδα»* (*"It is truly right to hymn the Supremely Divine Trinity"*). Patriarch Dionysios V and the Holy Synod rejected this proposed change, directing that the time-honored Marian hymn be preserved in accordance with established patriarchal tradition (*«διότι οὕτω συνειθίσθη»*).

[^31]: Canon LXXV (75) of the Council in Trullo (the Quinisext Ecumenical Council, convoked in Constantinople in 691–692 AD under Emperor Justinian II) constitutes the fundamental canonical legislation of the Eastern Orthodox Church governing church singing. The canon strictly prohibits unruly vocal shouting, artificial vocal distortion, and the introduction of secular, theatrical songs (*thymelika mele*) into divine worship, commanding that all sacred psalmody be offered with compunction (*katanyxis*) and inner spiritual vigilance to God, Who searches the hearts of men.

[^32]: John Zonaras (*Ἰωάννης Ζωναρᾶς*), renowned 12th-century Byzantine canonist, theologian, and chronicler, served as Great Drungarios of the Watch and First Imperial Secretary (*Protasekretis*) in Constantinople before retiring to the Monastery of Saint Glykeria. His authoritative commentary on Trullo Canon 75 interprets the scriptural mandate of piety given to the sons of Israel as applying preeminently to Christian chanters, vigorously condemning secularized theatrical melodies, theatrical posturing, and musical self-aggrandizement in the house of God.

[^33]: Theodore Balsamon (*Θεόδωρος Βαλσαμών*), titular Greek Orthodox Patriarch of Antioch and prominent 12th-century canonist, served as Nomophylax and Chartophylax of the Great Church of Hagia Sophia. In his commentary on Canon 75 of Trullo, Balsamon emphasizes that church buildings are consecrated houses of prayer, citing Canon 98 (often numbered 103 or 108 in Western codices) of the Council of Carthage (419 AD) and Saint John Chrysostom's homilies on Isaiah (*In illud: Vidi Dominum*, PG 56:97–142) to forbid unseemly physical gestures, dancing, and theatrical histrionics by cantors during sacred psalmody.


## Cohort 4 Footnotes

[^34]: Alexios Aristenos (*Ἀλέξιος Ἀριστηνός*), 12th-century Byzantine canonist who served as Nomophylax, Orphanotrophos, and Great Oikonomos of the Great Church of Hagia Sophia under Emperors John II Komnenos and Manuel I Komnenos, authored a concise, systematic commentary (*Epitomē*) on the Canons of the Apostles, the Ecumenical and Local Synods, and the Church Fathers. In his canonical gloss on Canon 75 of the Council in Trullo (691–692 AD), Aristenos establishes that vocal chanting in the sanctuary must be offered with deep spiritual compunction (*katanyxis*) and inner sobriety to God, Who examines the hidden thoughts of the heart, strictly proscribing cacophonous shouting, jagged cries, and secular vocal mannerisms that disrupt liturgical prayer.

[^35]: Saint John Chrysostom (*Ἰωάννης ὁ Χρυσόστομος*, c. 347–407 AD), Archbishop of Constantinople and Doctor of the Church. The passage excerpted here derives from his *Homily IV on Isaiah 6:1* (*In illud: Vidi Dominum homilia IV*, PG 56:120–126; Migne ed., Vol. 6, pp. 99–102). In this severe exhortation on liturgical dignity, Chrysostom contrasts the sublime, trembling worship of the Seraphim chanting the Trisagion before the Throne of God with the irreverent behaviour of worldly churchgoers who emulate actors, dancers, and mimes, hopping about and shouting in confusion. This patristic excerpt was omitted in the 1990s Denver bilingual printing and is critically restored here in accordance with the MTS-1 epigraphic mandate.

[^36]: Saint John Chrysostom, *Homily XI on the Gospel of Saint Matthew* (PG 57:193–202; Migne ed., Vol. 57, p. 200). Chrysostom cites the prophetic oracle of Isaiah (Isaiah 29:13 LXX: *«Ἐγγίζει μοι ὁ λαὸς οὗτος ἐν τῷ στόματι αὐτοῦ, καὶ ἐν τοῖς χείλεσιν αὐτῶν τιμῶσί με, ἡ δὲ καρδία αὐτῶν πόρρω ἀπέχει ἀπ' ἐμοῦ»*; quoted also by Christ in Matthew 15:8 and Mark 7:6) to admonish Christians who imagine that reciting two or three psalms casually and rushing through customary prayers mechanically without compunction or interior attention suffices for their salvation.

[^37]: Saint John Chrysostom, *Homily X on the Acts of the Apostles* (PG 60:85–92; Migne ed., Vol. 60). Chrysostom laments the spiritual frivolousness of congregations that attend divine services as though attending a theatrical spectacle of kitharodes and tragedians, where temporal entertainment produces no enduring spiritual fruit. He sharply contrasts the youth who eagerly memorize and sing demonic theater songs at home with the negligence of those who depart from the house of God without retaining a single divine hymn or prophetic word in their hearts.

[^38]: Saint John Chrysostom, *Homily XIX on the Epistle to the Ephesians* (PG 62:129–136; Migne ed., Vol. 62). Exegeting Ephesians 5:18–21 (*«Πληροῦσθε ἐν Πνεύματι, λαλοῦντες ἑαυτοῖς ψαλμοῖς καὶ ὕμνοις καὶ ᾠδαῖς πνευματικαῖς, ᾄδοντες καὶ ψάλλοντες ἐν τῇ καρδίᾳ ὑμῶν τῷ Κυρίῳ»*), Chrysostom defines liturgical psalmody as a spiritual cup of sobriety that dispels sinful drunkenness and sharpens mental clarity. He insists that true liturgical chanting requires active intellectual vigilance (*synesis*), warning that those who utter sacred words while allowing their hearts to wander in worldly distractions chant only with the lips and not unto the Lord.

[^39]: Saint Theodore the Studite (*Θεόδωρος ὁ Στουδίτης*, 759–826 AD), Abbot of the famous monastery of St. John the Baptist of Studion in Constantinople and champion of holy icons. The instruction excerpted here is preserved in his *Small Catecheses* (Catechesis 29/30: *To the Canonarch* / *«Πρὸς τὸν κανονάρχην»*). The canonarch (*κανονάρχης* / *канонарх*) directed the antiphonary psalmody, intoned the incipits of the Kathismata and Stichera, regulated the pitch and tempo of the two choirs (*choroi*), maintained melodic discipline without theatrical leaps or exaggerated dynamics, and instructed younger monks in ecclesiastical chant.

[^40]: Canon XV (15) of the Local Council of Laodicea (c. 363–364 AD) decreed: *«Περὶ τοῦ μὴ δεῖν πλὴν τῶν κανονικῶν ψαλτῶν, τῶν ἐπὶ τὸν ἄμβωνα ἀναβαινόντων καὶ ἀπὸ διφθέρας ψαλλόντων, ἑτέρους τινὰς ψάλλειν ἐν ἐκκλησίᾳ»* (*"No others shall sing in the church, save only the canonical singers, who go up upon the ambo and sing from a parchment book"*). This canonical boundary was enacted to preserve musical order (*eutaxia*) and textual solemnity in cathedral and parish assemblies, preventing uneducated or chaotic congregational shouting (*chasmodia*).

[^41]: The Patriarchal Music Committee (*Πατριαρχικὴ Μουσικὴ Ἐπιτροπή*) was established in Constantinople in 1881–1883 under Ecumenical Patriarch Joachim III (1878–1884, 1901–1912) to reform church music notation, standardize liturgical melodies, and establish choral discipline. Its members included leading musicologists and cantors of the Patriarchate. The seven governing criteria for liturgical choirs cited by George Violakis were published in the official periodical of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, *Ekklisiastiki Alitheia* (*Ἐκκλησιαστικὴ Ἀλήθεια*), 8th Year (1883), pp. 119–121, mandating acoustic vocal blend, mandatory rehearsals of *isokrates* and canonarchs, sacred deportment, intelligible enunciation, explicit rhythmic divisions (*tomai*), and notation of the drone (*ison*).


## Cohort 5 Footnotes

[^42]: The dual-column pagination schema of the 1990s Denver bilingual edition coordinates two distinct text traditions. The first column reproduces the printed pagination of the official 1888 Constantinople editio princeps of George Violakis (*Τυπικὸν τῆς τοῦ Χριστοῦ Μεγάλης Ἐκκλησίας*, Constantinople: Patriarchal Press, 1888), which spans pages 1 through 445. The second column designates the facing bilingual pagination of the Denver publication (Greek original text on even-numbered folios, English translation on facing odd-numbered folios, pages 1 through 937), concluding with the specialized Dictionary of Typikon Terms (*Λεξικὸν τοῦ Τυπικοῦ*, pp. TD-1–TD-88). The front-matter leaves use Greek alphabetic numerals (`α΄` to `ιγ΄`) in the first column aligned with Roman lower-case and Arabic numerals (`i` to `27`) in the second column.

[^43]: The *Protheoria* (*Προθεωρία τοῦ Τυπικοῦ*, "Pretheory of the Typikon" or General Rubrics) constitutes the liturgical foundation of both the Byzantine and Ruthenian typikon traditions. Rooted in the ancient Sabbaite and Studite monastic codes, the *Protheoria* systematizes the invariable statutory sequence, bodily actions, and ceremonial movements of the daily cycle of services—the Ninth Hour (*Θ΄ Ὥρα*), Daily and Great Vespers (*Ἑσπερινός*), the Midnight Office (*Μεσονυκτικόν*), Matins (*Ὄρθρος*), and the Divine Liturgy (*Θεία Λειτουργία*)—establishing canonical rules for incipits, kathismata, psalmody, stichera, the Lucernarium, antiphons, and dismissals prior to the specific variable propers of the liturgical year.

[^44]: The Introductory Psalm (*Προοιμιακὸς Ψαλμός*, Psalm 103 LXX: **"Bless the Lord, O my soul"** / *Εὐλόγει ἡ ψυχή μου τὸν Κύριον* / *Благослови, душе моя, Господа*). In Byzantine and UGCC usage, this psalm opens Great and Daily Vespers, praising the wisdom and majesty of God in the creation of the cosmos. During its recitation or chanting, the priest, uncovered and standing before the closed holy doors, reads the seven silent Prayers of the Lucernarium (*Εὐχαὶ τοῦ Λυχνικοῦ*) from the *Sluzhebnik* (*Ἱερατικόν*), recalling the exile of Adam outside paradise praying for redemption.

[^45]: The First Kathisma of the Psalter (*Α΄ Κάθισμα τοῦ Ψαλτηρίου*, Psalms 1–8 LXX: **"Blessed is the man"** / *Μακάριος ἀνήρ* / *Блаженъ мужъ*) and its First Stasis (*Α΄ Στάσις*, Psalms 1–3 LXX). In ancient monastic typika, the entire Psalter is divided into twenty kathismata, with the First Kathisma appointed for Saturday Great Vespers. In the Constantinopolitan parish reform codified by Constantine the Protopsaltis and George Violakis, the chanting of the First Kathisma is reserved for Great Feasts and Saturday evenings, with ordinary parish practice frequently reciting or chanting only the First Stasis (*«Μακάριος ἀνήρ»*), an urban condensation documented throughout the *Protheoria*.

[^46]: Psalm 140 LXX (**"Lord, I have cried unto Thee, hear me"** / *«Κύριε ἐκέκραξα πρὸς σέ, εἰσάκουσόν μου»* / *«Господи, воззвахъ къ Тебѣ, услыши мя»*), followed by Psalms 141, 129, and 116 LXX, forming the ancient Lucernarium (Lamp-lighting) psalmody of Vespers. Violakis specifies the precise melodic intonation according to the Tone of the week (Tone 1 to Tone 8) and regulates the distribution of the 10, 8, or 6 appointed Stichera between the Octoechos, the Menaion, and the Triodion, accompanied by the evening censing of the entire temple and iconostasis.

[^47]: The Artoklasia (*Ἀρτοκλασία* / *Лития и благословеніе хлѣбовъ*, Blessing of the Loaves) is celebrated at the conclusion of the Litiya at Great Vespers on the eve of Great Feasts. In the Ruthenian and UGCC tradition, a table or the Tetrapod in the center of the nave holds a vessel containing five loaves of bread, wheat, wine, and oil. The priest, accompanied by the deacon censing the offerings, chants the ancient troparion **"Rejoice, O Virgin Mother of God"** (*Θεοτόκε Παρθένε*) and intones the prayer of blessing: **"Lord Jesus Christ our God, Who didst bless the five loaves in the wilderness and therewith didst satisfy the five thousand..."**

[^48]: The Resurrectional Evlogitaria (*Εὐλογητάρια*, Psalm 118:12 LXX: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord, teach me Thy statutes"** / *Εὐλογητὸς εἶ, Κύριε, δίδαξόν με τὰ δικαιώματά σου* / *Благословенъ еси, Господи, научи мя оправданіемъ Твоимъ*), chanted in Tone 5 on Sundays, followed by the Ypakoe (*Ὑπακοή*), the Anavathmoi (*Ἀναβαθμοί*, the Hymns of Ascents based on the Gradual Psalms 119–133 LXX), the Prokimenon, and the reading of one of the eleven Resurrection Gospels of Matins (*Ἑωθινὸν Εὐαγγέλιον*), commemorating the manifestations of the risen Christ.

[^49]: The Matins Canon (*Κανών*), structured upon the nine biblical canticles (Odes / *ᾠδαί*). Violakis details the variable insertion of the Katavasiai (*Καταβασίαι*), the Small Ektenia and Kathisma after Ode 3, the Kontakion and Ikos (*Κοντάκιον καὶ Οἶκος*) after Ode 6, the Magnificat (*Μεγαλύνει ἡ ψυχή μου τὸν Κύριον*, Luke 1:46–55) with the refrain **"More honorable than the Cherubim"** (*Τὴν τιμιωτέραν τῶν Χερουβίμ*) preceding Ode 9, followed by the Exapostilaria (*Ἐξαποστειλάρια*), the Praises (Lauds / *Αἶνοι*, Psalms 148–150 LXX), and the solemn Great Doxology (*Δοξολογία Μεγάλη*).

[^50]: The Divine Liturgy (*Ἡ Θεία Λειτουργία*). Violakis outlines the statutory entrance into the Eucharistic liturgy, distinguishing the recitation of the Typika (Psalms 102 and 145 LXX) and the Beatitudes (*Μακαρισμοί*, Matthew 5:3–12) on ordinary Sundays from the Daily Antiphons and the Festal Antiphons of the Great Feasts. The rubrics govern the Little Entrance with the Holy Gospel Book, the singing of the Eisodikon (**"Come, let us worship and bow down before Christ..."** / *Δεῦτε προσκυνήσωμεν*), the appointed post-entrance Troparia and Kontakia, the Trisagion (*Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός*), the Epistle and Gospel readings, the Cherubic Hymn (*Χερουβικὸς Ὕμνος*), the hymn to the Mother of God **"It is truly meet"** (*Ἄξιόν ἐστιν*), the Communion Hymn (*Κοινωνικόν*), and the concluding dismissals.

[^51]: The annual cycles of the Byzantine liturgical year: The Menaion (*Μηναῖον*), comprising twelve volumes governing the immovable calendar from September 1 (the Indiction or Ecclesiastical New Year) through August 31; and the Triodion (*Τριῴδιον*), governing the ten-week movable penitential season preceding Holy Pascha. The Triodion spans from the pre-Lenten Sundays (Publican and Pharisee, Prodigal Son, Meatfare, and Cheesefare) through the forty days of Great Lent and Great and Holy Week, regulating the Great Canon of St. Andrew of Crete, the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, and Great Compline.

[^52]: The Pentecostarion (*Πεντηκοστάριον* / Paschal Florilegium / *Цвѣтная Тріодь*), the liturgical book regulating the fifty-day festive cycle extending from the Resurrection of Christ on Holy Pascha through the Sunday of All Saints (the First Sunday after Pentecost). This joyous season encompasses Bright Week (*Διακαινήσιμος Ἑβδομάς*), Antipascha (St. Thomas Sunday), the Sunday of the Myrrh-bearers, Mid-Pentecost (*Μεσοπεντηκοστή*), the Ascension of the Lord, and the Holy Fathers of the First Ecumenical Council in Nicaea (A.D. 325).

[^53]: The Appendix (*Παράρτημα τοῦ Τυπικοῦ*). This specialized section codifies patriarchal ceremonial rites and episcopal services unique to the Great Church of Constantinople: the Patriarchal celebrations of the Akathist Hymn and the Twelve Passion Gospels, Church Consecrations, the Enthronement and Return of the Ecumenical Patriarch, the Indiction service on September 1, liturgical schedules (*Horologion*), episcopal ordinations (*Cheirotonia*), conciliar concelebrations (*Sylleitourgon*), funeral and memorial services (*Mnemosyna*), and the *Eortodromion* (*Ἑορτοδρόμιον* / Table of Feasts), which mathematically harmonizes the eighteen immovable feasts occurring between January 11 and May 25 with the movable dates of the Triodion and Pentecostarion.


## Cohort 6 Footnotes

[^54]: The Ninth Hour (*Θ΄ Ὥρα*) in the Byzantine and Ruthenian synthesis. Commemorating the death of Christ upon the Cross at the ninth hour of the day (approx. 3:00 PM; Matthew 27:46–50), the Ninth Hour occupies a canonical pivot in the daily liturgical cycle. As the concluding office of the preceding liturgical day, it seals the diurnal services before Vespers introduces the liturgical commemoration of the following calendar day at sunset. Violakis codifies the exact structural rules for integrating the variable propers: when the day is a Feast of the Lord, of the Mother of God, or a celebrated Saint, the festal Troparion and Kontakion are recited; on simple weekdays without a festal Kontakion, the ancient penitential troparia are recited: **"The thief, beholding the Author of life"** (*Βλέπων ὁ Λῃστὴς τὸν ἀρχηγὸν τῆς ζωῆς*), followed by *Glory...*, **"In the midst of two thieves"** (*Ἐν μέσῳ δύο λῃστῶν*), and *Now and forever...*, **"The Lamb and Shepherd"** (*Τὸν Ἀμνὸν καὶ Ποιμένα*). On Sunday evening, the Octoechos Ypakoe of the Tone replaces the Kontakion. Throughout Bright Week and on the eve of the Ascension, the entire office is replaced by the Paschal Ninth Hour (*«Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ θεασάμενοι...»*), highlighting the uninterrupted festal joy of the Resurrection.

[^55]: The priest's liturgical preparation and vesting for the opening of Vespers (Violakis, p. 38, note 1). Before intoning the foundational exclamation **"Blessed is our God always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages"** (*Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν πάντοτε...*), the priest is canonically obligated to make three reverent prostrations (*metanias*) before the Holy Table, kiss the Holy Table and the hand-cross, and vest in the Epitrachilion (*ἐπιτραχήλιον*), reciting the prescribed vesting prayer. In accordance with the *Sluzhebnik* (*Ἱερατικόν*), the priest may not remove the Epitrachilion until the final dismissal of the service; Violakis strongly censures any laxity in this regard, declaring that a priest who fails to observe this order acts disorderly and unlawfully (*ἀτακτεῖ καὶ παρανομεῖ*).

[^56]: The canonical dispute regarding the opening exclamation of Bright Week Vespers (Violakis, p. 38, note 2). Violakis notes that ancient Sabbaite and Constantinopolitan Typika (including the Venetian printed Typikon of 1685) appoint the standard opening exclamation **"Blessed is our God always..."** (*Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν...*) even for the solemn Vespers of Bright Monday (the Agape Vespers), followed immediately by the Paschal troparion **"Christ is risen from the dead"** (*Χριστὸς ἀνέστη ἐκ νεκρῶν*) instead of the ferial invocations. Violakis classifies the modern practice of opening Paschal Vespers with the Trinitarian exclamation **"Glory to the Holy, Consubstantial, Life-Creating, and Undivided Trinity"** (*Δόξα τῇ Ἁγίᾳ καὶ Ὁμοουσίῳ...*) as an irregular post-Byzantine innovation (*παράτυπος νεωτερισμός*), though he acknowledges its virtually universal adoption in contemporary patriarchal, cathedral, and parish practice.

[^57]: The Introductory Psalm (*Προοιμιακὸς Ψαλμός*, Psalm 103 LXX: **"Bless the Lord, O my soul"** / *Εὐλόγει ἡ ψυχή μου τὸν Κύριον* / *Благослови, душе моя, Господа*). As the cosmic hymn of creation, Psalm 103 depicts the creation and providential sustenance of the universe by God the Word, setting the theological framework of the evening service as humanity's grateful contemplation of creation and longing for redemption. The psalm is omitted entirely only during Bright Week (from the Agape Vespers of Pascha Sunday through Bright Saturday) and at the Vespers of the Apodosis of Pascha, where the service opens directly with the Paschal Stichera and the Great Litany. From the Sunday of St. Thomas until the Apodosis of Pascha, the psalm is preceded not by the three-fold **"Come, let us worship"** (*Δεῦτε προσκυνήσωμεν*), but by **"Christ is risen"** chanted thrice (once by the clergy in the sanctuary and twice by the choirs).

[^58]: St. John Chrysostom's Homily on Psalm 41 [Psalm 41 LXX / Psalm 42 MT] (*Εἰς τὸν Ψαλμὸν ΜΑ΄*, PG 55:155–160; Violakis, pp. 40–41, note 3). Violakis appends this extensive patristic treatise to impress upon clergy and cantors the profound therapeutic, moral, and pneumatological efficacy of liturgical psalmody. Chrysostom argues that God, perceiving human weakness and reluctance toward spiritual instruction, mingled sweet melody with prophetic revelation, comparing this divine economy to wise physicians who smear the rim of a medicine cup with honey so that sick children will drink the curative potion. Sacred psalmody sanctifies the tongue, purges bodily passions, attracts the grace of the Holy Spirit, and repels demonic temptation, transforming both the temple and the domestic hearth into a true spiritual sanctuary (*Ἐκκλησία*).

[^59]: The First Kathisma of the Psalter (*Α΄ Κάθισμα τοῦ Ψαλτηρίου*, Psalms 1–8 LXX: **"Blessed is the man"** / *Μακάριος ἀνήρ* / *Блаженъ мужъ*) at Saturday Great Vespers. In the Sabbaite monastic tradition, the entire Psalter is chanted weekly, with the First Kathisma appointed for Saturday Vespers as the opening of the Sunday liturgical cycle. Violakis codifies the parish practice of the Great Church: the Kathisma is omitted whenever a Despotic Feast (Feast of the Lord) falls on Sunday—namely, the Nativity of Christ (December 25), Theophany (January 6), and the Transfiguration (August 6)—as well as on Pentecost Sunday, because the festive propers entirely replace the weekly psalmody. However, on Palm Sunday, St. Thomas Sunday, and the Exaltation of the Holy Cross falling on Sunday, the Kathisma is chanted, while the Resurrectional hymns of the Octoechos are omitted.

[^60]: The First Stasis of the Psalter (*Α΄ Στάσις τοῦ Ψαλτηρίου*, Psalms 1–3 LXX) on weekday feasts, accompanied by the Patristic Catena from Basil the Great, Theodoret of Cyrus, and Gregory of Nyssa (Violakis, pp. 42–43). On Feasts of the Mother of God and Polyeleos-rank Saints falling on Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, or Friday, the first stasis of the First Kathisma (Psalms 1–3 LXX: **"Blessed is the man"** / *Μακάριος ἀνήρ*) is chanted at Vespers. The patristic catena emphasizes: St. Basil (*Homily on Psalm 1*, PG 29:209–212) defining psalmody as the calm of souls, arbitrator of peace, and voice of the Church; Theodoret of Cyrus (*Interpretatio in Psalmos*, PG 80:860) praising divine grace that blended pleasure with spiritual profit; and Gregory of Nyssa (*In Inscriptiones Psalmorum*, PG 44:433) demonstrating how David blended melody with virtue to pour sweet honey upon sublime doctrines.

[^61]: The dual liturgical character of the Meeting of the Lord (*Ὑπαπαντὴ τοῦ Κυρίου*, February 2) and the Annunciation (*Εὐαγγελισμὸς τῆς Θεοτόκου*, March 25) (Violakis, pp. 44–45, note 4). Although historically grouped among the Feasts of the Mother of God, both feasts commemorate events central to the Incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ, and therefore possess the liturgical rank, privileges, and structural characteristics of Feasts of the Lord (*Δεσποτικαὶ ἑορταί*). Consequently, when either feast falls on a Saturday or Monday, the First Stasis of the Psalter is omitted at Vespers, in accordance with the statutory rule governing Despotic Feasts.

[^62]: The Lucernarium Psalmody (*Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*, Psalms 140, 141, 129, and 116 LXX) and the evening censing ritual (Violakis, pp. 44–45, note 5). In daily Vespers, all verses of the Lucernarium psalms are chanted in the appointed Tone of the week [Tone 1–8]. On Great Feasts, parish practice omits the intermediate verses from **"Set, O Lord, a watch before my mouth"** (*Θοῦ, Κύριε, φυλακήν*), jumping directly to the verses matching the number of appointed Stichera (10, 8, or 6). When the Second Choir begins the second verse, **"Let my prayer be set forth before Thee as incense"** (*Κατευθυνθήτω ἡ προσευχή μου*), the priest blesses incense and censes the Holy Table on all four sides, the sanctuary, the iconostasis, and the entire congregation. If an Entrance (*Εἴσοδος*) with the censer is appointed, the priest vests in the Phelonion (*φελώνιον*) at the beginning of *Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*; if no Entrance is appointed, he censes wearing only the Epitrachilion.

[^63]: The canonical precedence of the Resurrectional Octoechos on Sundays. Throughout the entire liturgical year, Sunday services (Saturday Great Vespers and Sunday Matins) belong primarily to the celebration of the Lord's Resurrection. The Resurrectional hymns of the Octoechos (Stichera, Doxastika, Dismissal Troparia, Kathismata, Ypakoe, Anavathmoi, Canons, Kontakia, Ikoses, Exapostilaria, Eothina Gospels, and Beatitudes) hold absolute priority, preceding the propers of the Mother of God and the commemorations of Saints. Violakis codifies the exact hierarchical ranking governing how festal, post-festal, and sanctoral hymns are intercalated with the weekly Resurrectional propers.
