[^148]: Feast of the Consecration of the Temple of the Holy Resurrection in Jerusalem and Forefeast of the Holy Cross on Sunday (Violakis, pp. 166–169, September 13, §§1–4). September 13 commemorates the Dedication or Consecration (*Ἐγκαίνια*) of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre (the Temple of the Resurrection, *Ἀνάστασις*) in Jerusalem, constructed under the Emperor Saint Constantine the Great and consecrated in A.D. 335. In Byzantine liturgical theology, this feast serves as the historical and liturgical threshold for the Universal Exaltation of the Precious and Life-Creating Cross on the following day (September 14), since the Holy Cross was first solemnly displayed and exalted before the assembled Christian multitude on the morrow of this very consecration. The day is also observed as the Forefeast (*Προεόρτια*) of the Precious Cross and commemorates the Holy Hieromartyr Cornelius the Centurion. When September 13 concurs with the Lord's Day (§§2–4), the office combines the Sunday Resurrectional order with the festal propers of the Consecration and the Forefeast. At Saturday Great Vespers, the ten Stichira at "Lord, I have cried" are partitioned into four of the Resurrection, three of the Consecration, and three of the Forefeast taken from Small Vespers (*Ὑψουμένου σου Δέσποτα*), crowned by the Doxastikon of the Consecration (*Τὴν μνήμην τῶν Ἐγκαινίων*) and the First Dogmatic Theotokion of the Tone. The Old Testament readings of the Consecration (1 Kings 8:22–30, the dedication prayer of Solomon; Proverbs 3:19–34, the establishment of the earth by Wisdom; Proverbs 9:1–11, Wisdom hath builded Her house) reflect the christological fulfillment of the temple in the Body of Christ and the Church. At Matins, the Triadic Canon and hymns precede the dismissal troparia: the Resurrection Troparion, the Troparion of the Consecration (*Ὡς τοῦ ἄνω στερεώματος*), and the Forefeast Troparion (*Τὸν ζωοποιὸν Σταυρὸν τῆς σῆς ἀγαθότητος*). The hymnographic canon combines the Resurrection Canon with the Canon of the Cross (Tone 4, by St. Cosmas of Maiuma) and the Canon of the Consecration (Tone 4, by St. John of Damascus), punctuated by the Katavasiai of the Holy Cross (*Σταυρὸν χαράξας*). At the Divine Liturgy, the Beatitudes combine four of the Tone with four from Ode 6 of the Canon of the Consecration; the Epistle (Galatians 6:11–18) and Gospel (John 3:13–17) are taken from the Sunday before the Exaltation, while the Zadostoinyk remains the ordinary *Axion Estin*.

[^149]: Translation and Commemoration of St. John Chrysostom on the Feast of the Universal Exaltation of the Precious Cross (Violakis, pp. 168–169, September 14, Rubrical Note). Historically, Saint John Chrysostom, Archbishop of Constantinople and Doctor of the Universal Church, fell asleep in the Lord on September 14, A.D. 407, at Comana in Pontus during his second exile. Because September 14 is dedicated entirely to the Universal Exaltation of the Precious and Life-Creating Cross—a feast ranking as a Great Feast of the Lord which completely absorbs all other commemorations—the liturgical constitution of the Byzantine rite transfers the liturgical celebration of Saint John Chrysostom to November 13. The rubrical note in the Violakis Typikon explicitly records that this transfer was instituted precisely so that the hymns, readings, and liturgical praises of Saint John Chrysostom might remain complete, intact, and undiminished (*ἀνελλιπῆ καὶ ἀμείωτα*), without being displaced or curtailed by the festal hegemony of the Exaltation. This principle reflects the traditional Byzantine typikon jurisprudence regarding feast day displacement and ensures the full commemoration of both the Precious Cross and the universal hierarch.

[^150]: Sunday Precedence, Festal Absorption, and the Liturgical Rite of the Procession of the Holy Cross at Matins (Violakis, pp. 170–171, September 14, §§5–6). The Universal Exaltation of the Precious and Life-Creating Cross on September 14 constitutes one of the Twelve Great Feasts of the Lord (*Δεσποτικαὶ ἑορταί*). When September 14 coincides with the Lord's Day (§5), an exceptional rule of festal absorption governs the services: the entire Sunday Resurrectional office is superseded and eliminated, and the festal propers of the Holy Cross are chanted exclusively (*οὐδὲν τῶν Ἀναστασίμων ψάλλομεν, ἀλλ’ ἅπαντα τὰ τῆς ἑορτῆς*). Only two elements of Sunday character are retained: the chant *Anastasin Christou theasamenoi* ("Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ") at Matins, which is uniquely appointed to be chanted on the Exaltation regardless of the day of the week on which the feast falls, signifying the indissoluble bond between the Cross and the Resurrection; and the Matins Gospel reading (John 12:28–36), read from the Royal Doors. At the conclusion of the Great Doxology, during the slow and ornate chanting of the Asmatikon Trisagion (*Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός*), the solemn liturgical Procession of the Precious Cross takes place. The officiating priest, having censed the Cross on the Holy Table where it rests on a tray amidst sprigs of sweet basil and three lighted candles, lifts the tray upon his head. Preceded by lighted tapers, the six-winged liturgical fans (*Exapteryga*), the chanters, and the Primikyrios bearing the two-wicked candle (*Didamvulon*), the priest circles the Holy Table from right to left (counter-clockwise) and carries the Cross through the Holy Doors to the center of the temple. There, circling the adorned Tetrapod three times, he stops facing East, elevates the tray, proclaims the liturgical cry *Sophia, orthoi!* ("Wisdom! Let us attend!"), and deposits the Cross upon the Tetrapod for the ceremony of the Elevation.

[^151]: The Solemn Rite of the Fivefold Elevation of the Holy Cross with Five Hundred Kyrie Eleisons (Violakis, pp. 172–173, September 14, §6 continued). The ceremonial pinnacle of the feast of the Exaltation is the Fivefold Elevation (*Ὕψωσις*) of the Precious Cross, historically commemorating both the recovery of the Cross by Saint Helen in A.D. 326 and its recovery from the Persians by Emperor Heraclius in A.D. 628, followed by its solemn elevation by Patriarch Macarius and Patriarch Zacharias before the weeping and rejoicing faithful. The liturgical rite prescribed in the Violakis Typikon preserves the ancient Constantinopolitan patriarchal cathedral ritual: After the priest censes the Cross on the Tetrapod from all four sides and the Dismissal Troparion (*Σῶσον, Κύριε, τὸν λαόν σου*) is sung thrice, the priest makes three prostrations, lifts the Cross adorned with basil, and facing East proclaims the First Petition of the Insistent Litany (*Ἐλέησον ἡμᾶς, ὁ Θεός*). While the choirs chant one hundred *Kyrie eleisons*, the priest signs thrice with the Cross, slowly bows his body until his head is one span from the earth, and then slowly straightens until he stands erect as the hundredth chant concludes. He then turns successively to the remaining quarters of the world to execute the remaining four Elevations: the Second Elevation facing North for all pious Orthodox Christians; the Third Elevation facing West for the ruling Patriarch and Hierarchs; the Fourth Elevation facing South for the absent bishops and dioceses; and the Fifth Elevation facing East for all clergy, benefactors, and the salvation of all Orthodox souls. Across the five elevations, five hundred *Kyrie eleisons* are chanted, symbolizing the total consecration and cosmic sanctification of the four corners of the world through the power of the Wood of the Cross. Following the elevations, the Patriarch or bishop ascends the dais, elevates the Cross with the Kontakion *Ὁ ὑψωθεὶς ἐν τῷ Σταυρῷ*, and leads the clergy and faithful in the adoration hymn: *Τὸν Σταυρόν σου προσκυνοῦμεν, Δέσποτα* ("We venerate Thy Cross, O Master"), followed by the distribution of the sanctified basil sprigs.

[^152]: The Festal Antiphons of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross and Non-Hierarchical Parish Rubrics (Violakis, pp. 174–175, September 14, §7 and Rubrical Note). The rubrical note on page 174 provides the essential canonical differentiation between cathedral celebrations presided over by the Patriarch or an active bishop and celebrations in ordinary parish or monastic churches served by a priest alone. In the absence of a bishop, the single priest executes the fivefold elevation unassisted, modifying the fourth petition to intercede directly for the parish wardens (*Ἐπίτροποι*), parish community, and benefactors. At the Divine Liturgy (§7), the standard Sunday or weekday Typika psalms are entirely replaced by the three proper Festal Antiphons of the Exaltation, drawn from the messianic and cruciform verses of the Septuagint Psalter: Antiphon 1 is taken from Psalm 21 LXX (*Ὁ Θεός, ὁ Θεός μου, πρόσχες μοι*), the prophetic psalm of the Passion of the Messiah, chanted with the Marian refrain *Taîs presveíais tês Theotókou*; Antiphon 2 is derived from Psalm 73 LXX (*Ἵνα τί, ὁ Θεός, ἀπώσω εἰς τέλος;*), which recounts the affliction of the sanctuary and confesses God as the eternal King Who wrought salvation upon the earth, chanted with the invariable christological refrain: *Σῶσον ἡμᾶς, Υἱὲ Θεοῦ, ὁ σαρκὶ σταυρωθείς, ψάλλοντάς σοι· Ἀλληλούϊα* ("Save us, O Son of God, Who wast crucified in the flesh, who sing unto Thee: Alleluia"), which is sung on the feast regardless of the day of the week; Antiphon 3 is taken from Psalm 98 LXX (*Ὁ Κύριος ἐβασίλευσεν, ὀργιζέσθωσαν λαοί*), proclaiming the divine sovereignty and the exaltation of His footstool, interspersed with the festal Dismissal Troparion: *Σῶσον, Κύριε, τὸν λαόν σου*. The critical footnote on page 174 records an erratum of the first printed edition of the Violakis Typikon, which accidentally omitted the rubric header for Antiphon 3 in Tone 1, restored by the liturgical redactor through concordance with the authoritative Constantinopolitan Menaion.
