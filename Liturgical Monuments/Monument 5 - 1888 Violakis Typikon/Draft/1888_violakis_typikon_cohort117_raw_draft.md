### Non-Liturgical Attire of the Clergy

The **MANDYAS** (*Μανδύας, ὁ*; mantle / Slav. *mantiya* / *мантія*): The mantle of the bishop is also a monastic garment, which, like a cloak, covers the whole body with the exception of the head. All monks, when present at divine services, must be robed in their mantles. The mantle is not technically speaking a liturgical vestment, but at Vespers and Matins (*Orthros*) it is worn when a bishop first arrives for the divine service and presides from the episcopal throne; it is later removed with assistance by the priests or deacons before he is vested for the Divine Liturgy. In Byzantine Greek practice, the bishop descends from the throne after the chanting of the Katavasiai, enters the sanctuary through the Holy Doors (*Royal Doors* / *Beautiful Gate*) where, after reverencing the Book of the Gospels lying upon the Holy Table, he proceeds to the Diakonikon where the mantle is removed, and if he is to celebrate the Divine Liturgy, he begins vesting with full episcopal vestments. In Slavic practice, after the bishop descends from the throne as described above, he proceeds to the center of the soleas where the mantle is removed and, if he is to celebrate, he is publicly vested for the Divine Liturgy with the assistance of the deacon or priest.

One peculiarity of a bishop's mantle is that it is not black, like the monastic mantle, but usually purple or of some other color. Upon it are sewn the so-called "Tables of the Law" (*Slav. Skrizhali* / *скрижали*), and, in particular, the "Fountains" (*Slav. Istotchniki* / *источники*). The *Tables* (square plaques of fabric sewn at the neck and feet) typify the Old and the New Covenants, from which the ministers of God must draw their doctrine. The *Fountains* are ribbons or stripes of textile, usually red and white in hue, sewn horizontally round about the mantle, representing the streams of divine teaching that flow from the mouth of the bishop. Small bells are attached to the mantle of a bishop, and to his Sakkos, as they were upon the hem of the robe of Aaron, the High Priest of the Old Testament.

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Clerical Non-Liturgical Attire: Skouphos Concluded – Vestry / Skevophylakion – Vigil – Vima / Sanctuary Part 1) [Demos, p. 1148 / TD-169]

A soft fabric cap which in Byzantine Greek tradition is usually flat or pleat-folded on top, while in Slavic tradition it is sewn pointed and peaked. It is worn for daily non-liturgical use, particularly by monastics and awarded clergy.

The **VESTRY** (*Σκευοφυλάκιον, τό* or *Ἱεροφυλάκιον, τό*; sacristy / Slav. *sosudokhranilishche* / *сосудохранилище*): These Greek designations signify the sacred depository where the sacred vestments, holy vessels, and liturgical implements are securely preserved. In early Christian antiquity, the *Skevophylakion* was a separate architectural annex erected adjacent to the sanctuary wherein these holy items were kept when not in use. When required for the Divine Liturgy, they were solemnly carried into the temple in public procession. The Great Entrance of the Divine Liturgy today mystically recalls that ancient transfer.

The **VIGIL** (*Ἀγρυπνία, ἡ*; Slav. *Vsenoshchnoe Bdenie* / *Всенощное бдѣніе* / All-Night Vigil): Watchfulness, alertness, or vigilance; derived from the Greek verb *ἀγρυπνέω* (*agrypneo*), signifying to remain wakeful, watchful, or intently attentive upon a sacred duty. Hence, *agrypnia* denotes literally "without sleep" or "sleepless devotion" (Latin *invigilare*). In Byzantine practice, the All-Night Vigil comprises Great Vespers with the Liti and Artoklasia, Matins (*Orthros*), the First Hour, Third Hour, and Sixth Hour, culminating in the Divine Liturgy. If the Divine Liturgy is celebrated immediately following these offices, the First through the Sixth Hours may be omitted or recited separately. The All-Night Vigil, on account of its great length, is not customarily celebrated in ordinary parochial churches, though it is sometimes observed on major solemnities. In monasteries, it is regularly celebrated on Great Feasts of the Lord and the Mother of God, and on the patronal feast of the monastic community.

Just as the Narthex was historically the station of the penitents and the catechumens, and the Nave was the congregation hall of the baptized faithful, so the Sanctuary is the sacred precinct of the ordained clergy. Although penitents and catechumens in modern times stand integrated with the faithful within the nave, the sanctuary continues to be reserved unto the sacred ministers of the Church.

Although the iconostasis physically partitions the sacred space within the temple, its mystical purpose is to unite humanity with the Divine: through the holy icons of Christ, the Mother of God, the angelic orders, and the saints, the earthly congregation joins in offering perpetual adoration unto the Holy Trinity.

Regardless of the physical orientation of a local church building, the sanctuary remains an awe-inspiring, holy place. None may enter the Vima unworthily or without the blessing of the priest for an assigned ministry. Sacred clergy and blessed servers alone may enter within the sanctuary, and only after preparing themselves with prayer to stand before the Holy Table and minister alongside the unseen angelic hosts who perpetually surround the Holy of Holies. While all the baptized participate in the royal priesthood of believers according to their respective orders, the sacred ministers execute their stewardship before Christ that all may "glorify God in every place of His dominion" (Psalm 102:22 [MT 103:22]).

## Extended Dictionary of Typikon Related Terms (Ypakoe Concluded – Yperagia – Ypomnema – Zeon Part 1) [Demos, p. 1150 / TD-171]

In contemporary liturgical usage, the **Ypakoe** is the title of the proper Resurrectional troparion chanted during Sunday Matins (*Orthros*). It changes according to the appointed Tone of the week from the Octoechos, occurring immediately after the Evlogitaria (*Resurrectional Troparia with the refrain from Psalm 118:12 [MT 119:12]*: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord, teach me Thy statutes"** / *«Εὐλογητὸς εἶ, Κύριε, δίδαξόν με τὰ δικαιώματά Σου»*). The Ypakoe of Pascha is the most renowned instance of this genre, chanted after the Third Ode of the Paschal Canon, during the Paschal Hours, and following the Little Entrance at the Paschal Divine Liturgy.

In the Greek language, *ypakoe* signifies "obedient hearing" or attentive listening (from *hypo*, under, and *akouo*, to hear). The designation indicates a musical and theological response, taking the form of a proper Troparion. Historically and rubrically, the Ypakoe is appointed: (1) at Sunday Compline; (2) at the Sunday Midnight Office; (3) at Sunday Matins before the Antiphons of the Tones and the Matins Gospel; and (4) after the Third Ode of the Matins Canon, taking the place of the Kathisma Hymn (*modified from APL*).

Furthermore, the Ypakoe serves as a short poetic strophe that replaces the third Kathisma hymn at Sunday Matins and upon certain Great Feasts of the Lord. In common parish praxis it is read rather than chanted. In Byzantine Greek usage it is also read during the Sunday Hours in place of the Kontakion. In strict Athonite monastic practice, it is chanted at the Divine Liturgy after the Dismissal Troparia (lit. "Apolytikia", *ἀπολυτίκια*) and before the Kontakia (*AE*).

The **YPERAGIA** (*Ὑπεραγία, ἡ*; Most Holy): The exalted liturgical title given to the Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of God, signifying "All-Holy" or "More Holy than all creation."

The **YPOMNEMA** (*Ὑπόμνημα, τό*; Synaxarion commentary or notice): In the context of this Typikon, *Ypomnema* denotes an explanatory notice, rubric, or historical memorandum appended to clarify liturgical commemorations and feast-day appointments.

Etymologically derived from *hypo* (under) and *mneme* (memory / remembrance), it signifies a memorial record or postscript. In translation, it designates the additional rubrical commentary and scholarly explanation subjoined to the rubrics of a feast to illuminate its ecclesiastical significance.
