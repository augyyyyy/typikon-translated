[^124]: Sacerdotal Ecphonesis, the Voice of the Assembly, and the Choral Amen (Violakis, pp. 126–127, §1 & Note 1). This rubric and Note 1 underscore the fundamental dialogical structure of Byzantine liturgical prayer. In Note 1, Violakis emphasizes: *"At every ecphonesis of the Priest concluding with 'Amen,' the choir is obligated, as representing the people, to chant 'Amen' with melody and piety; otherwise it violates its duty as chanter"* (*Ἐν ἑκάστῃ Ἐκφωνήσει τοῦ Ἱερέως καταληγούσῃ εἰς τὸ Ἀμήν, ὁ χορὸς ὀφείλει ἐκ προσώπου τοῦ λαοῦ λέγειν μετὰ μέλους καὶ εὐλαβείας τό Ἀμήν· ἄλλως παραβαίνει καθῆκον αὑτοῦ ὡς ψάλτου*). In ancient Christian ecclesiology, the liturgical *Amen* (Hebrew: "so be it", "truly") is not a passive musical coda, but the indispensable corporate ratification by the assembly of the royal priesthood, sealing the sacerdotal prayers (*ekphōnēseis*) offered at the Holy Table. As St. Justin Martyr witnessed in the second century (First Apology 65, 67) and St. John Chrysostom affirmed in the fourth century (Homilies on Second Corinthians 18), the entire congregation seals the Eucharistic and horological prayers through this single vocal assent. When the chanter or choir answers on behalf of the faithful (*ek prosōpou tou laou*), the response must be executed with attentiveness, reverence, and musical decorum, never clipped, swallowed, or omitted.

[^125]: Sunday Vesperal Psalmody, Stichologia Inviolability, and the Lucernarium Structure (Violakis, pp. 128–129, §1 cont.). Paragraph 1 establishes the canonical order of Saturday evening Vespers inaugurating the Lord's Day (*Kyriakē*). The service commences with the Introductory Psalm (Psalm 103 LXX, *Bless the Lord, O my soul*), followed by the Great Litany and the full First Kathisma of the Psalter (Psalms 1–8 LXX, *Blessed is the man*). The Lucernarium (*Λυχνικόν*, the Lamplighting Psalms: Psalms 140, 141, 129, and 116 LXX) is chanted on ten verses, distributing seven Resurrection Stichira from the Octoechos and three Prosomoia of the occurring Saint from the Menaion (or six of the Resurrection and four of the Saint if a Doxastikon is appointed). Violakis inserts a sharp rubrical injunction: *"It is to be noted that under no circumstances is it permitted for the chanters to omit or swallow words from the above verses which are chanted before the Stichira, because they actually sin; but each chanter is obliged to chant these verses in their entirety, audibly for all the faithful in church."* This reflects a systemic pastoral struggle against the hurried abbreviation of the biblical Psalter in late 19th-century urban parishes. The psalmody culminates in the solemn Vesperal Entrance (*Eisodos*) with the ancient third-century hymn *Phos Hilaron* (**"O Gladsome Light"**), followed by the Saturday Great Prokeimenon: **"The Lord reigns, He is robed in majesty"** (*Ὁ Κύριος ἐβασίλευσεν*, Psalm 92 LXX) chanted thrice in Tone 2 with its biblical verses, and the four Resurrection Aposticha Stichira beginning antiphonally from the Second Choir.

[^126]: The Sunday Midnight Office (*Mesonyktikon*), the Triadic Canons, and Megalynaria (Violakis, pp. 130–131, §2). Paragraph 2 delineates the distinctive theological architecture of the Sunday Midnight Office (*Μεσονυκτικόν*). Unlike the weekday Midnight Office—which centers upon the 17th Kathisma (Psalm 118 LXX, the *Amomos*) and solemn prayers for the departed—the Sunday nocturnal office is explicitly Trinitarian and Paschal in character. Because Sunday celebrates the Lord's Resurrection, the penitential Kathisma is omitted and replaced by the Triadic Canon (*Τριαδικὸς Κανών*) of the Octoechos in the Tone of the week, composed by St. Metrophanes of Smyrna (ninth century). If the Saint of the day possesses a Liti, the Liti stichira are appended to the Canon; otherwise, the choir immediately chants the four solemn Triadic Megalynaria: (1) **"It is truly meet to hymn the transcendent Trinity"** (*Ἄξιόν ἐστιν ὡς ἀληθῶς τὴν ὑπέρθεον ὑμνεῖν Τριάδα*); (2) **"It is truly meet to glorify Thee, O God the Word"** (*Ἄξιόν ἐστιν ὡς ἀληθῶς τοῦ δοξάζειν σε τὸν Θεὸν Λόγον*); (3) **"Let us all hymn in godlike manner with inspired songs"** (*Ὑμνήσωμεν πάντες θεοπρεπῶς ᾄσμασιν ἐνθέοις*); and (4) the Marian Theotokion: **"Beholding Thy Son risen from the dead, O pure Virgin"** (*Ἐκ νεκρῶν ἰδοῦσα τὸν σὸν Υἱόν, Ἄχραντε Παρθένε*). Following the Trisagion Prayers, the Hypakoe of the Tone (or the Dismissal Troparion of a celebrated Saint) is chanted, leading into the litany of supplication and the preliminary dismissal before Matins.

[^127]: Sunday Matins Enarxis, Patristic Injunctions on Silence, and Psalter Stichologia (Violakis, pp. 132–133, §2 cont.). This section codifies the beginning (*Enarxis*) of Sunday Matins (*Ὄρθρος*). The office opens with the Royal Beginning: the Trisagion Prayers, the Royal Troparia for the sovereign and the Church (*Σῶσον Κύριε*, *Ὁ ὑψωθεὶς ἐν τῷ Σταυρῷ*, *Προστασία φοβερά*), and the Litany of Fervent Supplication. After the sacerdotal doxology to the Holy Trinity, the Superior recites the Six Psalms (*Ἑξάψαλμος*, Psalms 3, 37, 62, 87, 102, 142 LXX). Violakis cites the ancient monastic typika (such as the Typikon of St. Sabas) demanding absolute stillness and reverence: *"all present listening in total silence and compunction, and let no one either by coughing or by any other disturbance disrupt the prayerful compunction, as the ancient Typika enjoin."* In patristic tradition, the Six Psalms symbolize the mystical quietude of the night and the Dread Judgment, during which the Priest stands uncovered before the Holy Doors reading the twelve Morning Prayers (*Ἑωθιναὶ Εὐχαί*). Following the Great Litany, the joyful enarxis of the day begins with **"God is the Lord, and hath appeared unto us"** (*Θεὸς Κύριος*, Psalm 117:27 LXX) chanted four times in the Tone of the day with its four appointed verses (Psalm 117:1, 10, 23 LXX). The Psalter stichologia follows, appointing the Kathismata of the Psalter and the Polyeleos (Psalms 134–135 LXX) for festal commemorations, or the 17th Kathisma (*Amomos*, Psalm 118 LXX) when no festal Polyeleos occurs.

[^128]: Distribution and Intercalation of Festal Sessional Hymns at Sunday Matins (Violakis, pp. 134–135, Note 2). In Note 2, Violakis clarifies the structural distribution of Sessional Hymns (*Καθίσματα*, Slavonic *Sedal'ny*) across the three Psalter readings (*Stichologiae*) of Sunday Matins: *"If the celebrated Saint is one of the more noted Saints of the Church, in each of the first two Stichologiae of the Kathismata we chant one Kathisma of the Saint, as noted in the particular services of these Saints"* (*Ἐὰν ὁ ἑορταζόμενος Ἅγιος ᾖ ἐκ τῶν ἐπισημοτέρων... καὶ ἐν ταῖς δυσὶ πρώταις στιχολογίαις τῶν Καθισμάτων ψάλλονται προσόμοια, ὡς ἐν ταῖς ἰδιαιτέραις αὐτῶν Ἀκολουθίαις σημειοῦται*). Under ordinary Sunday order, the first two Stichologiae (the Second and Third Kathismata of the Psalter) are followed exclusively by the Resurrection Sessional Hymns of the Octoechos with their proper Theotokia, while the hymnody of the Saint from the Menaion is relegated to the Third Stichologia (after the Polyeleos or Amomos). When a Saint of Polyeleos rank or higher is commemorated, however, the Menaion Kathisma is advanced into the earlier readings, intercalating festal veneration directly into the weekly Paschal cycle.

[^129]: The Sunday Matins Resurrection Gospel Sequence, Veneration, and Paschal Intercession (Violakis, pp. 134–135, §2 cont.). This rubric details the liturgical climax of Sunday Matins: the proclamation of the Holy Gospel and the veneration of the Resurrection. Following the Canons and Katavasiai, the Deacon intones the dialogue leading into the Morning Gospel (*Ἑωθινὸν Εὐαγγέλιον*), beginning with the solemn acclamation **"Let every breath praise the Lord"** (*Πᾶσα πνοὴ αἰνεσάτω τὸν Κύριον*, Psalm 150:6 LXX) chanted thrice in Tone 2. The Priest proclaims one of the eleven Resurrection Gospels of the weekly Eothinon cycle attributed to Emperor Leo VI the Wise. Following the Gospel, the Superior proclaims the ancient Paschal hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ, let us worship the Holy Lord Jesus..."** (*Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ θεασάμενοι*), and the choir chants Psalm 50 LXX (*Ἐλέησόν με ὁ Θεός*). At the verse **"For behold, Thou hast loved truth..."** (Psalm 50:8 LXX), the Priest carries the Book of the Holy Gospels to the center of the nave [before the Tetrapod] for veneration by the clergy and faithful. The sequence concludes with the post-Gospel stichira celebrating the Apostles, the Theotokos, and the Risen Christ (**"Jesus, having risen from the tomb as He foretold, hath given us life eternal and great mercy"**), followed by the extensive pastoral intercession **"O God, save Thy people, and bless Thine inheritance"** (*Σῶσον ὁ Θεὸς τὸν λαόν σου*), the twelvefold Kyrie eleison chanted in four triads, and the sacerdotal ecphonesis leading into the Ninth Ode of the Matins Canon. Note on liturgical divergence: In classical Sabbaite and Ruthenian/Slavic usage, the Eothinon Gospel is proclaimed before the Canons (immediately following the Polyeleos and Hymns of Ascent); the 1888 Constantinopolitan Typikon of Violakis codifies the parish practice of deferring the Gospel reading until after the Eighth Ode Katavasiai.
