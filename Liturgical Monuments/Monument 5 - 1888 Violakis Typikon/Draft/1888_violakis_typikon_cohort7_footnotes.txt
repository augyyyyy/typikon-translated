[^64]: Distribution of the Lucernarium Stichera (*Κύριε ἐκέκραξα*) on Sundays and Feasts (Violakis, pp. 46–47, §7). The Great Church of Christ maintains a rigorous mathematical hierarchy for the distribution of the ten evening Stichera: on principal Feasts of the Mother of God and on their Apodoses, the festal theme takes pre-eminence after the initial Resurrection proclamation (4 Resurrection + 6 Feast); on ordinary Afterfeast Sundays and commemorations of celebrated Saints (Polyeleos or Doxology rank), the Resurrection holds majority weight (6 Resurrection + 4 Feast or Saint); when a celebrated Saint falls on an Afterfeast Sunday or an Apodosis, a three-way division is observed (4 Resurrection + 3 Feast + 3 Saint). For major patristic and apostolic celebrations (the Archangels, the Forerunner, Peter and Paul, John the Theologian, the Three Hierarchs, the Ecumenical Councils, Demetrius, and Nicholas), the Saint is honored with 6 Stichera against 4 Resurrection Stichera. On Great Feasts of the Lord (Nativity, Theophany, Palm Sunday, St. Thomas Sunday, Pentecost, Transfiguration, and Exaltation of the Cross), the Resurrection hymns are entirely suppressed in favor of the festal propers.

[^65]: The First Evening Doxastikon and Dogmatikon (*Τὸ α΄ Θεοτοκίον τοῦ Ἤχου / Δογματικόν*) (Violakis, pp. 46–49, §8). In the Constantinopolitan and Sabbaite synthesis, the evening *Doxastikon* (*Δοξαστικόν*) occupies the summit of the Lucernarium hymnody. When a Feast of the Mother of God falls on Sunday, its own festal idiomelon serves for both *Glory...* and *Now and forever...*. On Afterfeast Sundays, Apodoses, and commemorations of celebrated Saints, the festal or sanctoral idiomelon is chanted at *Glory...*, followed invariably at *Now and forever...* by the First Theotokion of the Tone (*Τὸ α΄ Θεοτοκίον τοῦ ἤχου*) from the Octoechos, known as the *Dogmatikon* (*Δογματικόν*). Composed by St. John of Damascus, these eight dogmatic hymns expound the mystery of the Incarnation and the two natures in Christ. Violakis details the precise rules for intercalating the Triodion and Pentecostarion Doxastika during Great Lent and Paschaltide, preserving the ancient hierarchy between movable and immovable cycles.

[^66]: The Saturday Vespers Prokimenon and the Great Prokimena (Violakis, pp. 48–49, §9). The universal Prokimenon of Saturday Great Vespers is Psalm 92:1 LXX: **"The Lord is King, He is robed in majesty"** (*Ὁ Κύριος ἐβασίλευσεν, εὐπρέπειαν ἐνεδύσατο*), chanted in Tone 6 with its four appointed verses. Even when major feasts of the Lord or of the Mother of God fall on Sunday, this psalmody is maintained, affirming the primordial Sunday celebration of the Resurrection. The sole exceptions are the afterfeast Vespers of Great Feasts of the Lord (such as Sunday evening of Pascha, Pentecost, Nativity, and Theophany), at which the Great Prokimena in Tone 7 are chanted: **"Who is a god as great as our God?"** (*Τίς θεὸς μέγας*, Psalm 76:14 LXX) and **"Our God is in heaven and on earth"** (*Ὁ δὲ θεὸς ἡμῶν*, Psalm 113:11 LXX). At the Vespers of Holy Saturday, connected with the ancient baptismal Liturgy of St. Basil the Great, the standard Prokimenon is replaced after the Epistle (Romans 6:3–11) by Psalm 81:8 LXX: **"Arise, O God, judge the earth"** (*Ἀνάστα ὁ Θεός*), during which the clergy exchange their dark vestments for white robes.

[^67]: Old Testament Prophecies and Liturgical Epistles at Vespers (Violakis, pp. 48–51, §10). At Saturday Great Vespers, three Old Testament readings (*Paroimiai* / *Παροιμίαι*) are appointed whenever a Feast of the Lord, a Feast of the Mother of God, or a celebrated Saint occurs on Sunday. These readings are introduced by the Deacon's solemn exclamation **"Wisdom! Let us be attentive!"** (*Σοφία, πρόσχωμεν!*). A distinctive archaic feature preserved in the Byzantine Typikon is the chanting of New Testament Apostolic Epistles (*Ἀπόστολος*) at Great Vespers on the feasts of prime Apostles—Peter and Paul (June 29), John the Theologian (May 8), James the Brother of the Lord (October 23), and Andrew the First-Called (November 30). These are delivered with the full solemn liturgical cantillation of the Divine Liturgy, preceded by the Deacon's double exclamation **"Let us be attentive! Wisdom! Let us be attentive!"** (*Πρόσχωμεν, σοφία, πρόσχωμεν!*), witnessing to the ancient cathedral vigil practice of Constantinople.

[^68]: The Rite of the Artoklasia at the Litiya (*Ἡ τάξις τῆς Ἀρτοκλασίας ἐν τῇ Λιτῇ*) (Violakis, pp. 50–51, §11). Derived from the ancient monastic all-night vigil (*Agrypnia*), the Artoklasia (lit. "breaking of bread") provides physical nourishment and spiritual benediction during extended liturgical services. Following the prayer of the bowing of heads, the clergy proceed in solemn procession to the nave before the Tetrapod, where five round wheat loaves, wheat grains, wine, and olive oil are prepared in a specialized four-branched cruet-stand (*artophorion* / *tetrapodion*). The Deacon intones the expansive Litiya intercessions for ecclesiastical, civil, and global preservation. The Priest incenses the offerings crosswise while chanting the troparion **"O Mother of God and Virgin, rejoice"** (*Θεοτόκε Παρθένε*), replaced during Bright Week by the Paschal Troparion **"Christ is risen from the dead"** (*Χριστὸς ἀνέστη*). The rite concludes with the solemn blessing prayer invoking the multiplication of the five loaves in the wilderness (Matthew 14:13–21) and the chanting of Psalm 33:11 LXX: **"The rich have become poor and gone hungry"** (*Πλούσιοι ἐπτώχευσαν*).

[^69]: The Invariant Priority of the Resurrection Aposticha at Saturday Vespers (Violakis, pp. 50–53, §12). The Resurrection Stichera at the Aposticha (*Ἀπόστιχα Ἀναστάσιμα*) of Saturday Vespers form an inviolable structural bedrock in the Byzantine Rite: they are never suppressed, even on Feasts of the Mother of God, Forefeast or Afterfeast Sundays, Apodoses, or celebrations of Polyeleos-rank Saints. During the post-Paschal period (the four Sundays following St. Thomas Sunday: the Myrrh-Bearers, the Paralytic, the Samaritan Woman, and the Blind Man), the First Choir intones the first Resurrection Apostichon of the Tone, followed immediately by the four triumphant Paschal Stichera **"A sacred Pascha"** (*Πάσχα ἱερὸν*) with their biblical verses from Psalm 67 LXX (**"Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered"**), synthesizing the weekly Sunday Octoechos with the unwaning joy of the Fifty Days.

[^70]: The Doxastikon and Theotokion of the Aposticha (Violakis, pp. 52–55, §13). At the Aposticha, the concluding hymnody balances festal commemoration with Marian theology. On Feasts of the Mother of God, Afterfeast Sundays, Feasts of the Lord, and specific commemorative Sundays (such as the Sunday of the Holy Cross or the Sunday of the Myrrh-Bearers), the festal Doxastikon is chanted alone without a concluding Theotokion. When a celebrated Saint is commemorated on Sunday, the Saint's Doxastikon is chanted at *Glory...*, followed at *Now and forever...* by the Aposticha Theotokion from the Octoechos in the same Tone as the Saint's hymn. During the post-Paschal season on weekdays, the Doxastikon of the Saint is paired at *Now and forever...* with the Doxastikon of the preceding Sunday from the Pentecostarion, strictly reserving the great Paschal doxastikon **"It is the day of Resurrection"** (*Ἀναστάσεως ἡμέρα*) for Saturday Vespers and Sunday Matins.

[^71]: The Canticle of Simeon and the Paschal Substitution (Violakis, pp. 54–55, §14). The Canticle of the Righteous Simeon (*Νῦν ἀπολύεις*, Luke 2:29–32) and the Trisagion Prayers (*Τρισάγιον*) form the standard concluding hinge of Byzantine Vespers, leading directly into the Dismissal Troparia. Throughout Bright Week and on the eve of the Ascension (the Vespers of the Apodosis of Pascha), the entire penitential and nocturnal character of this liturgical juncture is transfigured: following the Paschal Doxastikon **"It is the day of Resurrection"** (*Ἀναστάσεως ἡμέρα*), the Trisagion Prayers are omitted completely, and **"Christ is risen from the dead"** (*Χριστὸς ἀνέστη*) is chanted thrice in its standard festive melody, moving immediately into the sacerdotal dismissal.

[^72]: The Hierarchy and Intercalation of Dismissal Troparia at Saturday Vespers (Violakis, pp. 54–55, §15). The Dismissal Troparia (*Ἀπολυτίκια*) sung at the conclusion of Saturday Great Vespers and at Matins at **"God is the Lord"** govern the theological thematic hierarchy of the day. The Resurrection Troparion of the Octoechos always holds absolute precedence, chanted first before any festal or sanctoral troparia. On principal Feasts of the Mother of God and on their Apodoses, the Resurrection Troparion is chanted once and the festal troparion twice (totaling three troparia); on ordinary Afterfeast Sundays, each is chanted once at Vespers, whereas at Matins the Resurrection Troparion is chanted twice and the festal troparion once. When complex coincidences arise—such as the Annunciation falling on the Third Sunday of Lent, Lazarus Saturday, or Palm Sunday—Violakis details the precise triple intercalation order, ensuring that neither the Paschal journey, the cross, nor the incarnation is eclipsed.
