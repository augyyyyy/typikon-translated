[^90]: The Proclamation of the Resurrectional Morning Gospel at Sunday Matins and the Sacerdotal Intercession (*Σῶσον ὁ Θεός*) (Violakis, pp. 76–77, §28, Note 10). In §28, Violakis details the solemn liturgical complex following the Katavasia of the Eighth Ode on ordinary Sundays and Sundays of the Triodion. The Deacon (or Priest) opens the rite with the invitation **"Let us pray to the Lord"**, followed by the sacerdotal exclamation **"For Thou art holy, our God, and Thou dwellest among the saints..."** (*Ὅτι ἅγιος εἶ, ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*), and the antiphonal proclamation of the Morning Prokimenon and **"Let every breath praise the Lord"** (*Πᾶσα πνοὴ αἰνεσάτω τὸν Κύριον*). The Priest proclaims the appointed Resurrectional Eothinon Gospel (from the fixed eleven-pericope cycle of the Resurrection apparitions), followed immediately by the ancient baptismal hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** (*Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ θεασάμενοι*), Psalm 50 LXX (**"Have mercy on me, O God"**), and the Pentecostal-resurrectional troparia (**"Through the intercessions of the Apostles... Through the intercessions of the Theotokos... Jesus, having risen from the grave..."**). On Sundays of the Triodion, these troparia are replaced by the compunctious penitential hymns of St. Andrew of Crete and St. Theodore the Studite: **"The doors of repentance do Thou open unto me, O Giver of Life"** (*Τῆς μετανοίας ἄνοιξόν μοι πύλας, Ζωοδότα*), **"Guide me in the paths of salvation, O Theotokos"**, and **"When I ponder the multitude of my evil deeds..."**. In Note 10, Violakis addresses a widespread liturgical abuse and pastoral anomaly: the sacerdotal intercession **"O God, save Thy people, and bless Thine inheritance..."** (*Σῶσον, ὁ Θεός, τὸν λαόν σου*) and its subsequent exclamation **"Through the mercies and compassions..."** belong strictly and canonically to the presiding Priest according to ancient Byzantine rubrics. However, because the Priest is frequently occupied in the Prothesis chapel performing the rite of Preparation (*Proskomide* / *Προσκομιδή*), this prayer was often delegated by concession to the Deacon. Violakis sternly reasserts the canonical principle: the rubrical order must be preserved (*ἡ τάξις ὅμως τηρητέα*), and the Priest is obliged to interrupt his preparation of the Holy Gifts at that precise liturgical junction, exit the Sanctuary or stand before the Holy Table, and personally intone the solemn intercession.

[^91]: The Stichologia of the Ninth Ode: The Biblical Canticle of the Theotokos vs. Festal Megalynaria (Violakis, pp. 76–79, §29). Paragraph 29 delineates the structural execution of the Ninth Ode at Matins. On normative days throughout the year—including ordinary Sundays, commemorations of celebrated Saints, Afterfeasts, and weekdays from Thomas Sunday to Pentecost—the Ninth Ode begins with the biblical Canticle of the Mother of God, the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55), interspersed with the venerable Megalynarion composed by St. Cosmas the Melodist: **"More honorable than the Cherubim, and beyond compare more glorious than the Seraphim..."** (*Τὴν Τιμιωτέραν τῶν Χερουβίμ*), intoned according to the Tone of the day's Katavasiae. Violakis outlines the systematic exceptions where the Magnificat is suppressed in favor of festal Megalynaria (*Μεγαλυνάρια*) intercalated directly between the troparia of the Ninth Ode of the festal Canon: (1) Great Feasts of the Lord and of the Theotokos; (2) Holy Week; and (3) all Sundays from Pascha until Ascension. On the Apodoses (Leave-takings) of Dominical Feasts, the Ninth Ode is sung without **"More honorable"**, except for the Exaltation of the Holy Cross (September 14) and the Transfiguration (August 6) according to the Typikon of Constantine, which lack proper festal Megalynaria. On Marian Apodoses, if coinciding with Sunday, the Magnificat precedes the festal Ninth Ode and its Megalynaria; on weekdays, the festal Ninth Ode stands alone. Crucial idiosyncratic distinctions govern two major feasts: on the Apodosis of the Meeting of the Lord (February 9), because all fourteen festal Megalynaria are chanted, the Magnificat is completely displaced; on the Apodosis of the Annunciation (March 26), only the Magnificat is sung with the Heirmos and troparia, without festal Megalynaria. Finally, on the Feast of the Three Hierarchs (January 30), all three Ninth Odes are chanted in their entirety without the Magnificat, conforming to the Studite poly-heortological cathedral tradition (clarified in Violakis p. 167, §4, Note 4).

[^92]: The Sunday Trinitarian Doxology (*Ἅγιος Κύριος ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*) and the Hierarchy of Exapostilaria (Violakis, pp. 78–81, §30, Notes * and 11). Paragraph 30 codifies the rubrics governing the Exapostilaria (*Ἐξαποστειλάρια*, hymns of "sending forth" at the dawn) and their introductory Trinitarian acclamation at Matins. Violakis establishes that the solemn singing of **"Holy is the Lord our God"** (*Ἅγιος Κύριος ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*, Psalm 98:5, 9 LXX) is a unique, inviolable hallmark of Sunday Resurrectional Matins, never sung on weekdays even when commemorating celebrated Saints. On Sundays coinciding with Marian Feasts or Apodoses, the Sunday Resurrection Exapostilarion is chanted first, followed by the festal Exapostilarion twice. On Afterfeasts with celebrated Saints, the threefold progression is observed: (1) Resurrection, (2) Saint, and (3) Afterfeast. In Note 11, Violakis explains the performance practice of **"Holy is the Lord our God"**: it is chanted twice in the Tone of the Sunday Resurrection Exapostilarion, followed by the verse **"Exalt the Lord our God, and worship at His footstool, for He is holy"** (Psalm 98:5 LXX), whereafter the choir intones the Exapostilarion itself. On Palm Sunday and Holy Saturday, however, because neither service possesses a proper hymnographic Exapostilarion, **"Holy is the Lord our God"** is chanted three times without the psalm verse, functioning as the Exapostilarion itself. On Pascha and throughout Bright Week, the Paschal Exapostilarion **"Having fallen asleep in the flesh as a mortal..."** (*Σαρκὶ ὑπνώσας*) is chanted immediately without **"Holy is the Lord"**. During the Paschal season, Thomas Sunday features proper Exapostilaria: **"With thy hand, Thomas, probe My wounds..."** (*Ἐμῶν μελῶν χειρί σου*) and **"Today spring is fragrant..."** (*Σήμερον ἔαρ μυρίζει*), intercalating the Saint's Exapostilarion between them when one occurs.

[^93]: The Stichologia of the Praises (*Αἶνοι*): Combining Resurrectional, Festal, and Paschal Hymnody (Violakis, pp. 80–83, §31). In §31, Violakis details the numerical distribution of the Stichira at the Praises (*Ainoi*, Psalms 148, 149, 150 LXX) on Sundays coinciding with diverse liturgical cycles. The standard total allotment is eight stichira (intoned with the final four verses of Psalm 148/149 and four verses of Psalm 150 LXX). On Sundays of Marian Feasts or Apodoses, the choir divides the stichira equally: four Resurrectional Stichira of the Octoechos and four festal Stichira. When a celebrated Saint coincides with an Afterfeast or Apodosis Sunday, the tripartite distribution applies: three Resurrectional, two Afterfeast, and three of the Saint. If the Meeting of the Lord falls on one of the first four Sundays of the Triodion, Violakis distinguishes between the preparatory Sundays (Publican & Pharisee, Prodigal Son: 4 Resurrection + 4 Feast) and the strict pre-lenten Sundays (Meatfare, Cheesefare: 3 Resurrection + 2 Triodion + 3 Feast), preserving the thematic transition to Great Lent. For the Feast of the Annunciation falling on the Third Sunday of Lent (Veneration of the Cross), the Cross takes precedence: 2 Resurrection + 3 Cross + 3 Annunciation; if falling on the Fourth or Fifth Sunday of Lent, 4 Resurrection + 4 Annunciation. On the four Sundays following Thomas Sunday (Myrrhbearers, Paralytic, Samaritan Woman, Blind Man), commemorations of major saints (St. George, St. John the Theologian, Saints Constantine and Helen) introduce the Paschal Stichira: 3 Resurrection + 3 Saint, followed by the joyous Paschal Stichira **"A sacred Pascha hath been revealed to us today"** (*Πάσχα ἱερὸν ἡμῖν σήμερον ἀναδέδεικται*) with their proper Paschal verses (**"Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered..."**).

[^94]: The Eothinon Doxastikon of the Praises and Festal Displacements in Byzantine Cathedral Usage (Violakis, pp. 82–85, §32). Paragraph 32 codifies the theological and hymnographic role of the Eothinon Doxastikon (*Ἑωθινὸν Δοξαστικόν*)—the solemn hymn chanted at **"Glory to the Father..."** before the Great Doxology at Sunday Matins. Composed by the Byzantine Emperor Leo VI the Wise (886–912), the eleven Eothina Doxastika provide a profound liturgical commentary and theological synopsis of the eleven Sunday Morning Gospels. Under normative conditions, the Eothinon Doxastikon is chanted whenever the corresponding Morning Gospel has been proclaimed. Violakis catalogs the systematic exceptions where the Eothinon is displaced by a festal or hagiographical Doxastikon: (1) Feasts and Apodoses of the Theotokos and of the Lord; (2) all Sundays of the Triodion and Pentecostarion; (3) Pre-Nativity and Post-Nativity Sundays; (4) the Sundays of the Holy Fathers of the Ecumenical Councils (Nicaea I, Chalcedon, Nicaea II); (5) major feast days of the Forerunner, Apostles, Evangelists, and Hierarchs; and (6) patronal and titular feasts of the local temple. Significantly, Violakis records the authoritative ruling of Patriarch Constantius I of Constantinople (1830–1834): the Doxastika of St. Gregory the Theologian (January 25), St. John Chrysostom (November 13 / January 27), St. Andrew the First-Called (November 30), and the Great Martyr Euphemia (September 16 / July 11) must also supersede the Sunday Eothinon when falling on a Sunday, specifically honoring the Apostolic founder, the great Patriarchal Doctor-Fathers, and the Patroness and miracle-worker of the Ecumenical Patriarchate in Constantinople whose relics reside in the Patriarchal Church of St. George.

[^95]: The Marian Doxasticon Theotokion: "Most Blessed Art Thou" vs. the Paschal "Day of Resurrection" (Violakis, pp. 84–85, §33). Paragraph 33 governs the concluding Theotokion chanted at **"Now and forever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen."** following the Doxastikon of the Praises and immediately preceding the Great Doxology. The universal Byzantine norm for Sunday Matins is the venerable Dogmatic Theotokion in Tone 2: **"Most blessed art thou, O Virgin Theotokos; for through Him Who was incarnate of thee, Hades is taken captive..."** (*Ὑπερευλογημένη ὑπάρχεις, Θεοτόκε Παρθένε*). Violakis notes that this hymn is so foundational to the Sunday resurrectional mystery that it is preserved even when a Feast of the Mother of God, or its Apodosis, or the Apodosis of a Feast of the Lord occurs on Sunday. The sole universal exception occurs during the post-Paschal season: on the four Sundays following Thomas Sunday (Myrrhbearers, Paralytic, Samaritan Woman, and Blind Man), **"Most blessed art thou"** is replaced by the climactic Paschal doxasticon composed by St. John of Damascus: **"The Day of Resurrection! Let us beam with festive joy, and let us embrace one another..."** (*Ἀναστάσεως ἡμέρα, καὶ λαμπρυνθῶμεν τῇ πανηγύρει*). When major Saints occur on these post-Paschal Sundays, the Sunday Theotokion is sung at **"Now and forever..."**, except on Myrrhbearers Sunday, where **"The Day of Resurrection"** is retained because the day's Doxastikon is itself an Eothinon hymn. On weekdays from Thomas Sunday until the Apodosis of Pascha, commemorations of these Saints appoint the Doxastikon of the preceding Sunday at **"Now and forever..."** rather than the Paschal hymn, preserving the strict hierarchical distinction between Lord's Days and ordinary ferias.
