[^73]: The Sacerdotal Dismissal (*Τελεία / Μεγάλη Ἀπόλυσις*) and Weekday Adaptation (Violakis, pp. 56–57, §16, Notes 6 & 7). In §16, Violakis codifies the full ceremonial structure of the Major Dismissal at the conclusion of Saturday Great Vespers. The rite begins with the diaconal exclamation **"Wisdom!"** (*Σοφία*), the sacerdotal doxology **"Blessed is He Who is, Christ our God"** (*Ὁ ὢν εὐλογητὸς Χριστὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*), and the ancient prayer for the preservation of the Orthodox faith and the Church intoned by the Superior (**"Establish, O Lord God, the holy Orthodox faith..."** [*Στερεώσαι Κύριος ὁ Θεὸς τὴν ἁγίαν πίστιν...*]). When an Artoklasia has taken place, the dialogue is adapted: the Deacon intones **"Let us pray to the Lord"** (*Τοῦ Κυρίου δεηθῶμεν*), and the Bishop or Priest imparts the blessing **"The blessing of the Lord and His mercy come upon you..."** (*Εὐλογία Κυρίου καὶ ἔλεος αὐτοῦ...*). In Note 6, Violakis records that this identical Dismissal formula is employed on the remaining days of the week, with the sole omission of the initial Resurrection clause **"May He Who rose from the dead"** (*Ὁ ἀναστὰς ἐκ νεκρῶν*). In Note 7, he observes that ancient Typika designate this full rite as the "Complete" or "Perfect Dismissal" (*τελεία Ἀπόλυσις*), distinguishing it from abbreviated forms.

[^74]: Festal Characteristic Invocations (*Χαρακτηριστικὰ τῆς Ἀπολύσεως*) and the Minor Dismissal (*Μικρὰ Ἀπόλυσις*) (Violakis, pp. 56–59, §16). The Major Dismissal incorporates distinct historical incipits reflecting the theological mystery commemorated on Great Feasts of the Lord (e.g. Nativity: **"He Who was born in a cave and lay in a manger"**; Theophany: **"He Who deigned to be baptized in the Jordan by John for our salvation"**). On the Feast of the Universal Exaltation of the Holy Cross (September 14), the Resurrection phrase **"He Who rose from the dead"** is preserved regardless of the day of the week, anchored in the Matins hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** (*Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ θεασάμενοι*). Violakis notes that among Feasts of the Mother of God, only the Annunciation (March 25) and the Meeting of the Lord (February 2) possess proper characteristic dismissal invocations, which are intercalated into the Sunday Resurrection dismissal when falling on Sunday. Paragraph 16 also defines the Minor Dismissal (*μικρὰ Ἀπόλυσις*), utilized at the Little Hours, Small Vespers, Compline, and the Midnight Office. This form condenses the intercessory catalog, invoking only the Theotokos, the Holy Apostles, and the Saint of the day, followed either immediately by **"Through the prayers..."** (*Δι᾽ εὐχῶν*) or by the litany **"Let us pray for the peace of the world..."** (*Εὐξώμεθα ὑπὲρ εἰρήνης...*).

[^75]: Patristic Theology of Nocturnal Vigils: St. Basil the Great on Antiphonal Psalmody (Violakis, pp. 58–61, §17, Note 8). At the inception of the rubrics for Matins (*Ὄρθρος*), Violakis incorporates a foundational patristic testimony from St. Basil the Great's Epistle 207 (al. 63/67, *Ad clericos Neocaesarienses*, Migne PG 32:759–764). Writing to defend the ascetic vigil practices of his Cappadocian community against charges of liturgical novelty, St. Basil witnesses to the universal fourth-century monastic practice of nocturnal prayer and antiphonal psalmody: the faithful assemble before dawn in the house of prayer, make confession to God in labor, sorrow, and tears, and then arise to sing psalms antiphonally (*διχῇ διανεμηθέντες ἀντιψάλλουσιν ἀλλήλοις*), led by a precentor (*ἑνὶ κατάρχειν τοῦ μέλους*). At daybreak, all offer up in unison the 50th Psalm of confession (Psalm 50 LXX). St. Basil appeals to the common consensus of Egypt, the Thebaid, Libya, Palestine, Arabia, Phoenicia, Syria, and Mesopotamia, demonstrating that nocturnal vigils and psalmody constitute the universal heritage of the Catholic Church.

[^76]: The Sunday Midnight Office (*Μεσονυκτικόν*) and the Monastic Absolution Rite (*Εὐξώμεθα ὑπὲρ εἰρήνης*) (Violakis, pp. 60–61, §17, Note 9). On Sundays, the penitential 17th Kathisma (Psalm 118, *Amomos*) of the daily Midnight Office is replaced by an order celebrating the Holy Trinity: following the introductory prayers and Psalm 50 LXX, the Triadic Canon (*Τριαδικὸς κανών*) from the Octoechos is chanted with the refrain **"Most Holy Trinity, our God, have mercy on us and save us,"** followed by the four Triadic Troparia **"It is truly meet"** (*Ἄξιόν ἐστιν*), the Trisagion Prayers, and the Hypakoe (*Ὑπακοή*) of the Tone. Following the Minor Dismissal, the Deacon or Priest intones the litany of mutual intercession **"Let us pray for the peace of the world..."** (*Εὐξώμεθα ὑπὲρ εἰρήνης τοῦ κόσμου...*). In Note 9, Violakis explains that this concluding petition is an ancient survival of monastic coenobitic discipline (*τῆς μοναχικῆς τάξεως λείψανον*), wherein the monastics prostrated and sought mutual forgiveness (*metanoia*) from the Superior (*proestos*) before departing, as detailed further in §50 of the Protheoria.

[^77]: Festal and Seasonal Transformations of the Midnight Office (Violakis, pp. 60–63, §17). Violakis details the rubrical adjustments required when major commemorations fall on a Sunday: if a Feast of the Mother of God or a celebrated Saint occurs, the Litiya hymns of the feast are chanted after the Triadic Canon (or after Psalm 50 on Great Feasts of the Lord), and the Hypakoe is replaced by the festal Dismissal Troparion. During Great Lent (Sundays of the Triodion), the Hypakoe is replaced by the three Penitential Troparia (**"Have mercy on us, O Lord, have mercy on us"** [*Ἐλέησον ἡμᾶς, Κύριε, ἐλέησον ἡμᾶς*]), except on the Third Sunday of Lent (Veneration of the Cross) or when the Meeting of the Lord or the Annunciation coincides. Throughout Bright Week and on the Apodosis of Pascha, the entire Midnight Office is replaced by the Paschal Nocturns (**"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ... Anticipating the dawn..."** [*Ἀνάστασιν Χριστοῦ... Προλαβοῦσαι τὸν ὄρθρον*]). An editorial correction in the Greek text notes that Violakis's cross-reference to §3, Note 4 (Easter Vespers) is an erratum for §2, Note 8 (Easter Divine Liturgy).

[^78]: The Royal Office (*Βασιλικὰ Τροπάρια*) and the Six Psalms (*Ἑξάψαλμος*) at the Threshold of Matins (Violakis, pp. 62–63, §18). Matins proper begins with the Priest entering the Sanctuary and making the solemn doxology before the Holy Table: **"Blessed is our God, always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages."** The Reader recites the Trisagion Prayers and the Royal Troparia (**"O Lord, save Thy people"**, **"Thou Who wast lifted up upon the Cross"**, and the Theotokion **"O fearsome Protection"**), commemorating Christ as King and invoking divine protection over the Christian realm. Following a short litany, the Priest proclaims the solemn doxology **"Glory to the Holy, Consubstantial, Life-Creating, and Undivided Trinity..."**, after which the Superior reads the Six Psalms (*Ἑξάψαλμος*: Psalms 3, 37, 62, 87, 102, and 142 LXX). In Byzantine liturgical theology, the Hexapsalmos represents the stillness of the nocturnal expectation of the Final Judgment; it is delivered with profound compunction in total silence, and is never omitted except during Bright Week and on the Apodosis of Pascha.

[^79]: The Chanting of "God is the Lord" and the Intercalation of Dismissal Troparia (Violakis, pp. 62–65, §19). The proclamation **"God is the Lord, and hath appeared unto us; blessed is He that cometh in the Name of the Lord"** (Psalm 117:27, 26 LXX) marks the liturgical announcement of the Incarnation at the dawn of Matins. It is chanted four times in the Tone of the week, punctuated by three verses from Psalm 117 LXX: (1) **"Give thanks unto the Lord, and call upon His holy Name"** (v. 1); (2) **"All nations compassed me about..."** (v. 10); and (3) **"This is the Lord's doing, and it is marvelous in our eyes"** (v. 23). The Dismissal Troparia (*Ἀπολυτίκια*) are intercalated immediately thereafter: on ordinary Sundays, the Resurrection Troparion is chanted twice, followed at **"Now and forever..."** by its Theotokion; on Feasts of the Mother of God, the Resurrection Troparion is chanted once and the festal troparion twice; on Afterfeast Sundays, the Resurrection Troparion is doubled and the festal troparion sung once; and when a celebrated Saint occurs, all three troparia (Resurrection, Saint, and Feast) are chanted once each.

[^80]: The Lenten Alleluia and the Nocturnal Canticle of Isaiah (Violakis, pp. 64–65, §19). On fasting weekdays of Great Lent (Monday through Friday), the festal proclamation **"God is the Lord"** is suppressed and replaced by **"Alleluia"** (*Ἀλληλούϊα*) chanted four times (thrice after each verse) in Tone 8 [Tone 4 Plagal]. The four verses are taken from the Fifth Biblical Ode, the Canticle of the Prophet Isaiah (Isaiah 26:9–15 LXX): (1) **"From the night my spirit waketh early unto Thee, O God, for Thy commandments are a light upon the earth"** (v. 9); (2) **"Learn righteousness, ye that dwell upon the earth"** (v. 9); (3) **"Jealousy shall seize upon an untaught people, and now fire shall devour the adversaries"** (v. 11); and (4) **"Add evils unto them, O Lord, add evils unto them, unto the glorious of the earth"** (v. 15). This ancient penitential substitution emphasizes vigilance, judgment, and compunction, replacing the festive joy of the dawn with ascetic longing for the light of Christ.

[^81]: The Memorial Alleluia of Saturday and the Ancestral Soul Saturdays (*Σάββατα τῶν Ψυχῶν*) (Violakis, pp. 64–65, §19). On ordinary Saturdays throughout the year that lack the commemoration of an illustrious Saint, the ancestral Sabbath character of resting in Christ is manifested by replacing **"God is the Lord"** with the Memorial **"Alleluia"** in Tone 2, accompanied by the appointed verses for the departed: **"Blessed are they whom Thou hast chosen and taken to Thyself, O Lord"** (Psalm 64:5 LXX) and **"And their memorial is from generation to generation"** (Psalm 101:13 LXX). These verses are followed by the general troparia for martyrs and the departed: **"Apostles, Martyrs, and Prophets"** and **"Remember, O Lord, as Thou art good, Thy servants."** On the two universal Saturdays of Souls (*Σάββατα τῶν Ψυχῶν*)—Meatfare Saturday and the Saturday before Pentecost—the services are dedicated entirely to the memory of all departed Orthodox Christians from the ages, featuring the classic funeral Apolytikion in Tone 8: **"O Thou Who with profound wisdom and love for mankind orderest all things..."** (*Ὁ βάθει σοφίας*).
