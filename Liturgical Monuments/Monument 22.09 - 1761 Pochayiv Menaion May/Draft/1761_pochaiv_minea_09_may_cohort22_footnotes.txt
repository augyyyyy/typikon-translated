[^402]: The canon is authored by St. Joseph the Hymnographer (c. 816–886), one of the principal canonographers of the Byzantine liturgical tradition.
[^403]: The hymnographic imagery of a "hospitable star" (Slavonic: *гостинну тя звѣзду*) plays on the Greek etymology of the name Therapon (*Θεράπων*, "servant, attendant, healer") and the virtue of hospitality/service.
[^404]: An explicit epigraphic wordplay on the name Therapon: "bearing thy name in truth and fittingly, O martyr" (*истинно нося и подобно мучениче, твое званіе воистинну*), referring to his vocation as a true servant (*therapon*) of Christ.
[^405]: St. Nicetas the Confessor, Bishop of Chalcedon (late 8th – early 9th century), renowned for his pastoral charity, defense of holy icons under Leo the Armenian, and subsequent exile.
[^406]: In Byzantine hymnography, the patriarch Joseph is pre-eminently remembered for his chastity and temperance (*цѣломудріе*, Greek *σωφροσύνη*), having resisted the advances of Potiphar's wife (Gen. 39).
[^407]: St. Ignatius, brother and fellow-ascetic of St. Nicetas of Chalcedon, commemorated alongside him in the Byzantine synaxaria.
[^408]: An epigraphic wordplay on the name Nicetas (*Νικήτας*, from *νίκη*, victory): "as one named after victory (*побѣды яко тезоименитъ*), thou didst inherit a crown of miracles from the Crown-giver."
[^409]: An explicit theological reference to the Iconoclast controversy: by defending the venerable icon of Christ, St. Nicetas defended the reality of the Incarnation and restored the divine image in man (*еже по образу спаслъ еси*).
[^410]: Reference to St. Nicetas's heavenly patron and namesake (*тезоименитому*), the 4th-century Great Martyr Nicetas the Goth (commemorated Sept. 15).
