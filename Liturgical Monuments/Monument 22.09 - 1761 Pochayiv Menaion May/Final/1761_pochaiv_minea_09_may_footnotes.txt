# FOOTNOTES AND SCHOLARLY COMMENTARY



[^1]: Jeremiah was cast into the muddy cistern of Malchiah son of the king (Jeremiah 38:6 [LXX 45:6]).

[^2]: Slavonic: "собо́ръ їнле́въ" (typographical contraction for the assembly of the Israelites, referencing their hostility to Jeremiah; cf. Jeremiah 38:6).

[^3]: Cf. Lamentations 4:20: "The breath of our nostrils, the anointed of the Lord, was taken in their pits, of whom we said, Under his shadow we shall live among the heathen."

[^4]: Baruch 3:36–38 (LXX: 3:36–37): "This is our God, and there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of him... Afterward did he shew himself upon earth, and conversed with men." Historically attributed liturgically to the prophecy of Jeremiah.

[^5]: Corinthians pericope 186 corresponds to 2 Corinthians 4:6–15.

[^6]: Luke pericope 14 corresponds to Luke 4:22–30.

[^7]: The name Athanasius (Greek Ἀθανάσιος) derives from athanasia (ἀθανασία), meaning "immortality."

[^8]: St. Germanus of Constantinople refers to St. Athanasius as the "gold-flowing Nile" of Alexandria, evoking both Egyptian geography and his Greek name meaning "immortal."

[^9]: A reference to St. Athanasius's early apologetic treatise Contra Gentes (Against the Heathen / On the Idols), written prior to the Arian controversy.

[^10]: Apollinarianism denied the existence of a rational human soul and mind in Christ, asserting that the Divine Logos took the place of the human spirit. St. Athanasius refuted this in his Tomus ad Antiochenos and letter To Epictetus.

[^11]: Slavonic "А҆́лмазъ" / "А҆дама́нтъ" renders Greek ἀδάμας (adamant, diamond), a classic patristic epithet for St. Athanasius of Alexandria.

[^12]: In this 1761 Pochaiv edition, Fol. 6 (Leaves p11–p12) is bound before Fol. 7 (Leaves p13–p14). Fol. 6r opens abruptly with the third Sticheron at the Praises for St. Athanasius (*By the radiance of thy divine words...*), whereas Fol. 7 contains the preceding portion of Matins (Canon Odes 7–9 and Praises Stichera 1–2). The catchword at the bottom of Fol. 7v (*Слове́съ*) confirms that Fol. 6r immediately succeeds Fol. 7v in liturgical sequence.

[^13]: The printed rubric abbreviation *прⷣ* indicates that the preceding stichera are of the Afterfeast of Mid-Pentecost, while the Doxasticon *Glory* is dedicated to St. Athanasius.

[^14]: St. Theodosius of the Caves († 1074) is commemorated on May 3 (his falling asleep) and August 14 (the translation of his relics). The Pochaiv Menaion places the brief common service to the Holy Martyrs Timothy and Maura first, noting that the full festal service to St. Theodosius follows immediately thereafter (beginning on Leaf p16).

[^15]: The acrostic in Church Slavonic reads: *Тїмо́ѳеа и҆ ма́ѵры болѣ́зни чьствꙋ́ю* ('I honor the sufferings of Timothy and Maura'), composed by St. Joseph the Hymnographer.

[^16]: This phrase directly completes the second troparion of Ode 7 of the Canon of St. Athanasius from Fol. 5v (Leaf p10: *...нече́стїа загради́телѧ [роди́тель, и҆згна́нїа на тѧ̀ воздви́жетъ]*), which was interrupted on Leaf p10 by the binder's transposition of Fol. 6.

[^17]: The Slavonic text reads *королю̀* ('unto the king'), reflecting the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth royal prayer formula characteristic of 18th-century Ruthenian Basilian editions printed at the Pochaiv Lavra, rather than the Synodal Russian *цр҃ю̀* ('unto the tsar').

[^18]: This opening phrase on Leaf p15 directly completes the third troparion of Ode 6 of the Canon of Timothy and Maura from Fol. 8v [Leaf p18] (*...любо́вїю до конца̀ вѣнча́вшаго / Хрⷭ҇тꙋ̀ ду́шы приваза́сте...*), due to the inverted gathering in the physical exemplar.

[^19]: According to their Passio, Timothy and Maura hung facing each other on their crosses for nine days and nights before surrendering their spirits to God.

[^20]: This opening phrase on Leaf p17 directly completes the first troparion of Ode 1 of the Canon of Timothy and Maura from Fol. 6v [Leaf p12] (*...ꙗ҆́кѡ да тѣ́хъ / тѣ́хъ мольба́ми ѿ бѣ́дъ и҆зба́вимсѧ*).

[^21]: The Doxasticon at Small Vespers jointly invokes both founding fathers of the Kyiv Caves Lavra, St. Anthony († 1073) and St. Theodosius († 1074), celebrating them as the twin pillars of Rus' monasticism.

[^22]: This clause completes the Doxasticon at "Lord, I have cried" for St. Theodosius from Fol. 10v (Leaf p20).

[^23]: St. Theodosius constructed the great stone Dormition Cathedral of the Kyiv Caves Monastery (consecrated in 1089), dedicated to the Mother of God, establishing the monastery's enduring status as the earthly house of the Theotokos.

[^24]: This sticheron contrasts the spiritual charisms of the two founders of the Kyiv Caves Lavra: St. Anthony, who like Gideon asked for dew and like Elijah brought down fire from heaven, and St. Theodosius, who boldly rebuked Prince Sviatoslav II Yaroslavich of Kyiv for driving out his brother Iziaslav I in 1073.

[^25]: The Kyiv Caves Patericon records that before the construction of the Dormition Cathedral, a miraculous pillar of light and fire extended from earth to heaven, revealing the exact site chosen by the Mother of God for the Lavra's great church.

[^26]: According to the Life of St. Theodosius by St. Nestor the Chronicler, when the saint was brought to church on the eighth day after his birth, the priest, seeing through the Spirit what he would become, named him Theodosius ('given by God') and dedicated him to God's service.

[^27]: In his youth at Kursk, St. Theodosius baked the prosphora (altar breads) with his own hands out of humility and love for the Divine Liturgy, enduring harsh rebuke and beatings from his mother, who deemed such menial manual labor shameful for a youth of noble boyar lineage.

[^28]: St. Theodosius twice attempted to make a pilgrimage to the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, but was turned back each time by divine dispensation to fulfill his destiny as the founder of Rus' monasticism in Kyiv.

[^29]: St. Theodosius secretly wore heavy iron chains around his loins from his youth. When his mother discovered them clinging to his flesh and soaked in dried blood, she tore them off him in anger and beat him severely.

[^30]: After tracking down Theodosius at the Kyiv Caves, his mother was so moved by his spiritual exhortations and filial love that she renounced the world herself, took the monastic habit at the Monastery of St. Nicholas in Kyiv, and spent the remainder of her days in ascetic repentance.

[^31]: The Kyiv Caves Patericon relates that while St. Theodosius was travelling on monastery business, a nobleman's servant mistook the shabbily dressed hegumen for a common laborer, commanded him to get into the wagon and drive the horses while the servant slept, only for the servant to be terrified upon entering Kyiv to see princes and boyars dismounting to bow low before Theodosius.

[^32]: The Kyiv Caves Patericon relates that when the monastery's cellarer was despairing over having no gold to buy provisions for the brethren, an unknown handsome youth appeared in the courtyard, handed St. Theodosius a bag containing three bags of gold ducats, saying it was sent by God, and immediately vanished.

[^33]: The Kyiv Caves Patericon relates that when Grand Prince Iziaslav Yaroslavich came to the monastery to dine with St. Theodosius, the cellarer informed the hegumen that there was no more mead in the cellars; but St. Theodosius commanded him to go back and check the vessels in faith, whereupon they were found miraculously filled to the brim with sweet mead.

[^34]: The title 'capital city of Rus'' (Slavonic: *началограде росскїй*) designates Kyiv, the cradle of Eastern Slavic Christianity and monastic life, extolling St. Theodosius as the spiritual father and protector of the mother of Rus' cities.

[^35]: The Kyiv Caves Patericon relates that a band of robbers came by night to rob the monastery church of the Dormition, but were astounded to see the church lifted high off the ground, floating in the air, while angelic singing resounded from within. Terrified by the miracle, the robbers fled in awe, later came to St. Theodosius in repentance, and embraced monastic tonsure.

[^36]: The appointed liturgical readings for St. Theodosius are Hebrews 13:7–16 (pericope 334) and Matthew 11:27–30 (pericope 47), standard festal lections for venerable monastic founders.

[^37]: The physical Pochaiv 1761 edition contains a typographical metathesis: *ѿ ненаикисоꙋ́мѡжныя* (read *ѿ неискусомужныя*, 'from her that knew not man', referring to the Virgin Mother of God).

[^38]: The Slavonic acrostic reads: *Пелагі́и хвалꙋ̀ и҆́стиннꙋ плету̀* ('I weave a true praise for Pelagia'), corresponding to the Greek acrostic of the canon composed by St. Theophanes the Branded in Tone 4.

[^39]: The hymn refers to St. Pelagia's rejection of the imperial bridal robes brought to her from the son of Emperor Diocletian. After receiving holy baptism (the 'bath of regeneration', Titus 3:5) from Bishop Linus of Tarsus on Mount Taurus, she distributed her luxurious garments to the poor and clothed herself in white baptismal robes.

[^40]: St. Pelagia of Tarsus was condemned by Emperor Diocletian to be burned alive within a red-hot bronze/copper bull (Slavonic: *конобъ*, Greek: βοῦς χαλκοῦς). Entering the blazing vessel voluntarily after making the sign of the Cross, she surrendered her soul unto Christ as a sweet-smelling whole burnt offering.

[^41]: St. Irene the Great-Martyr of Magedon (1st–2nd c.), born Penelope, daughter of the pagan prince Licinius in Persia or Thrace, was instructed in the Christian faith by her tutor Apellian and baptized by St. Timothy, disciple of the Apostle Paul.

[^42]: The Slavonic acrostic reads: *Твои́ми мя̀ мл҃твами мꙋ́нце і҆ри́но покры́й* ('Shield me by thy prayers, O martyr Irene'), representing the Greek canon composed by St. Joseph the Hymnographer.

[^43]: A theological wordplay upon the martyr's name Irene (from the Greek Εἰρήνη, 'peace', Slavonic: *миръ*), celebrated here as *мироименитая* ('named for peace'), whose endurance brought the peace of Christ to pagans and tormentors alike.

[^44]: In the Ode 6 Theotokion, the printed Slavonic text *Про́бли* represents an abbreviated or defective form for *Проѡбразѝ* (Greek: προετύπωσε, 'prefigured'), alluding to Moses the God-seer beholding the burning bush on Mount Sinai unconsumed (Exodus 3:2).

[^45]: On May 6 the Ruthenian Church celebrates the commemoration of the Holy and Righteous Job the Long-Suffering, whose endurance amid bodily affliction and catastrophic bereavement stands as an Old Testament archetype of Christ's Passion.

[^46]: The Slavonic acrostic reads: *Многострада́льнаго досто́йнѡ пѣ́ти і҆́ѡва* ('Worthily to hymn the long-suffering Job'), without the Theotokia, corresponding to the Greek canon composed by St. Joseph the Hymnographer.

[^47]: An allusion to the theophany in Job 42:5–6: 'I had heard of Thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye sees Thee; therefore I despise myself, and repent in dust and ashes.'

[^48]: The Epistle lection appointed for Holy and Righteous Job is Galatians 5:22–6:2 (pericope 213), extolling the fruit of the Spirit and the bearing of one another's burdens as the fulfillment of the law of Christ.

[^49]: The Gospel lection appointed for Holy and Righteous Job is Matthew 11:27–30 (pericope 43), the invitation of the Savior to take His yoke and find rest for souls.

[^50]: On May 7 the Church commemorates the miraculous apparition of the luminous Sign of the Precious Cross over Jerusalem on May 7, 351 A.D., stretching from Golgotha to the Mount of Olives, witnessed by the entire population and described in the famous encyclical letter of St. Cyril of Jerusalem to Emperor Constantius II.

[^51]: The Pochaiv 1761 edition witnesses the distinct Ruthenian recension formula *и҆ вѣ́рнаго на́шего короля̀* ('and our faithful king', referring to Augustus III of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth), whereas Russian Synodal editions read *вѣрнаго императора нашего* or *благовѣрныхъ правителей*.

[^52]: A theological wordplay upon the name of the martyr Acacius (Greek: Ἀκάκιος, from ἀκακία, 'guilelessness, innocence', Slavonic: *безѕло́бїе*), celebrating St. Acacius the Centurion of Cappadocia, martyred in Byzantium (303 A.D.).

[^53]: St. Acacius (a Cappadocian centurion martyred in Byzantium under Maximian in 303 A.D.) is celebrated throughout this canon with recurring wordplays on his name (Greek: Ἀκάκιος, 'guileless, innocent', Slavonic: *неѕло́бивъ*).

[^54]: An epigraphic witness to Constantinople ('great among cities and reigning over all'), where St. Acacius the Centurion was martyred. Emperor Constantine subsequently erected a celebrated martyrium church in his honor at the *Heptaskalon*, which was regarded as one of the chief protective shrines and 'crowns' of the imperial capital.

[^55]: On May 7, the Menaion prescribes the Matins canon of the luminous apparition of the Precious Cross to be combined with the canon of the prevailing festal period from the Pentecostarion (e.g., Mid-Pentecost or Pascha), appointing the Pentecostarion canon with the heirmos to 6, and the canon of the Precious Cross to 6.

[^56]: The Ikos of May 7 links the 351 A.D. apparition of the Cross over Jerusalem to the historic vision of Constantine the Great before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312 A.D.), proclaiming the Cross as the perpetual protective sign and 'weapon of peace' granted to the Christian empire.

[^57]: The Epistle lection appointed for the Apparition of the Cross is 1 Corinthians 1:18–24 (pericope 125): 'For the word of the Cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.'

[^58]: The Gospel lection appointed for the Apparition of the Cross is John 19:6–11, 13–20, 25–28, 30–35 (pericope 60), the composite Passion Gospel describing Christ before Pilate, the Crucifixion at Golgotha, and the piercing of His side.

[^59]: May 8 commemorates the Holy Apostle and Evangelist John the Theologian, specifically celebrating the annual miracle in Ephesus where a fine, fragrant, healing dust or 'manna' (*hē konis*) was emitted from his tomb, through which the sick were delivered from various infirmities.

[^60]: St. Arsenius the Great (c. 354–445 A.D.), a Roman senator and tutor to the sons of Emperor Theodosius the Great (Arcadius and Honorius), who renounced the imperial court of Constantinople to embrace radical solitude, fasting, and weeping in the Egyptian desert of Scetis.

[^61]: The first festal Paremia appointed for St. John the Theologian at Great Vespers is 1 John 3:21–4:6, declaring confidence toward God, the commandment of mutual love, and the testing of spirits by the confession of Christ come in the flesh.

[^62]: The second festal Paremia appointed for St. John the Theologian is 1 John 4:11–16, testifying to the mutual love of Christians as the dwelling of God within them and the contemplation of God through the Holy Spirit.

[^63]: The third festal Paremia appointed for St. John the Theologian is 1 John 4:20–5:5, declaring that he who loves God must love his brother also, and that faith in Jesus as the Son of God is the victory that overcomes the world.

[^64]: St. Andrew Pyros (also known as Andrew of Jerusalem or Andrew the Blind, 8th c.), a prolific Byzantine hymnographer who composed numerous idiomelon stichera for major festal liturgies.

[^65]: St. John the Monk (St. John of Damascus, c. 676–749 A.D.), author of the Octoechos and numerous festal idiomelon stichera.

[^66]: An explicit patristic refutation of Arianism: Arius claimed of the Son *ἦν ποτε ὅτε οὐκ ἦν* ('there was once when He was not'), whereas St. John the Theologian affirmed *'In the beginning was the Word'* (John 1:1), thus casting down the heretical assertion.

[^67]: The Matins Gospel appointed for St. John the Theologian is John 21:15–25 (pericope 67), Christ's post-resurrection dialogue with Peter ('Feed My sheep') and the mysterious prophecy regarding the beloved disciple: 'If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee?'
