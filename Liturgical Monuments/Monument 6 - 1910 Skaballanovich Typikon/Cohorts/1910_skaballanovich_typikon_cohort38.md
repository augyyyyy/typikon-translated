### The Order of the Typika

The Order of the Typika (*chin izobrazitel’nykh*), in comparison with the form that it had acquired by the 9th century — that is, the form it exhibits in the Palestinian-Sabbaite Horologion of the Sinai Library MS No. 863 of the 8th–9th century (see above, p. 298) — underwent insignificant development. Compared unto its present form, this monument still lacks Psalms 102 [103] and 145 [146], **“Only-Begotten Son,”** **“Pardon, remit,”** and almost the entire subsequent office (with the exception of **“Lord, have mercy”** thrice, the opening words of Psalm 133 [134], and **“Blessed be the name of the Lord”**); in substitution for the present conclusion, it contained prayers adapted for the reception of the Holy Mysteries (inasmuch as the office itself was designated for this purpose, as is evident from its inscription: *eis tēn metalēpsin*, εἰς τὴν μετάληψιν), namely: **“One is Holy...”**, **“O taste and see that Christ is the Lord,”** and the prayers of Holy Communion. Now it, first of all, gradually loses this last element of its composition — its adaptation unto

### Skete Typika and Rules

Apart from those types of divine worship provided by the various Typika and performed in churches, there existed perpetually, from the very inception of monastic asceticism in the 4th century, a distinct form of private, solitary, skete worship within monastic practice. This possessed its own developmental trajectory and acquired during the period under review a highly intricate structure, not failing, of course, to exert an influence upon the general church cursus. Represented in Nikon of the Black Mountain (11th century) and by a series of Greek and Slavic Typika and Horologia, this type of worship traces its lineage unto St. Pachomius the Great, specifically unto the “Rule given him by an Angel” — that is, unto the primitive rule of Pachomian monasteries set forth above (see pp. 207–209). This rule was somewhat modified and elaborated by later ascetics. Thus Abba Isaiah (an anchorite of Scetis in the 5th century), as reported by Nikon, transmits it in this form: “By day 12 prayers, and for the Ninth Hour 3 prayers, and before each prayer a psalm”; the remainder of St. Isaiah’s daily distribution of time: “At night I sleep 4 hours, 4 hours I stand in chanting, and 4 hours I work; by day I work until the sixth hour, read until the ninth, and from the ninth I attend unto food and unto everything necessary for the cell” [^1741]. According unto the rule of St. Barsanuphius (at first Bishop of Damascus and thereafter an anchorite in Nitria in the mid-5th century): “The Scetiotes have neither hours nor do they chant hymns,” but spend their time in manual labor, meditation, and prayer; during manual labor one must meditate (“study”) or recite psalms, but at the end of each psalm one must say, “O God, have mercy upon me, a wretch,” or another prayer “for deliverance from the old man”; if thoughts assail: “Lord, Thou seest my affliction, help me”; having completed three rows of weaving, one may alternate with reading the lives of the saints for 5 to 8 leaves; at the evening chanting the Scetiotes, according unto Barsanuphius, recite 12 psalms and *(Orig. p. 394)* at night 12, and at the end of each psalm, in place of

=== LEAF p374 ===
the doxology, they say Alleluia and a single prayer: the **“Our Father”** or “Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy on me” [^1742]. As one of the skete rules, Nikon of the Black Mountain cites also the well-known narrative concerning the liturgical service of St. Nilus of Sinai (see above, p. 294).

### The Order of the 12 Psalms and the Chanting of the Psalter

Upon the basis of these rules there emerges in monuments of the 9th and subsequent centuries the distinct “Order of the 12 Psalms,” mentioned above in connection with Compline (see pp. 383–384), which was specifically designed for solitary and cell usage. On the other hand, upon the foundation of these rules there developed in monasticism the custom of reciting the entire Psalter in one's cell daily. This custom even received a specific typikal formulation that adapted the Psalter unto cell execution through the addition unto the kathismata of special prayers, doxologies, and troparia. Such an order is set forth in a 14th-century Slavic manuscript of the Synodal Library under the title: *“Typikon of the Skete Chanting, That Is, Concerning the Cell Rule: An Explanation Concerning the Psalter, How It Is Meet to Chant Individually”* [^1743]. The Typikon requires the recitation of the entire Psalter over the course of a day and night (*“день и нощь”*), dividing it among the Midnight Office, Hours, Vespers, and Matins; unto each service there are assigned 3 kathismata (*“по тристатии”*), and at each kathisma a Trisagion with prostrations, troparia, and a prayer; this order of “skete chanting” even had its own manner of reciting the Psalter — drawn out (troparic): “not hastening, but chanting with stillness, as is the custom of the Scetiotes, in Tone 4 or Tone 8.” He who accomplished such a rule was no longer obliged to attend church services. In the same manuscript there is set forth also the “Order of the 12 Psalms” [^1744], which is recommended unto those who “are unable to complete the whole Psalter”; this order is given in the following form: introductory prayers with Psalm 50 [51], 12 psalms (26, 31, 56, 33, 38, 40, 69, 70, 76, 101, 50, and the Great Doxology) [LXX] with a Trisagion after every three psalms; the troparia and prayers at the end are penitential [^1745]; the illiterate, in lieu of the 12 psalms, must make 300 prostrations with the prayer: “Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner” [^1746]. For the infirm, this relaxation is granted:

“Begin the Psalter as much as thou art able, and having rested a little, begin the Midnight Office, and after the Midnight Office chant 6 kathismata with prostrations and with prayers; having made the dismissal and thanked God, and having taken rest lawfully, rising again before Matins, chant 6 kathismata with prostrations and with prayers; then begin Matins according unto cus*(Orig. p. 395)*tom; whatever remains for thee in the Psalter, chant it during the day; or if thou hast some manual labor and wishest to rest at night, chant 10 kathismata at night and 11 by day (the 21st kathisma being the Biblical Odes); but do not venture

### The Karyes Typikon and the Horologion of Theokaras

Mount Athos accomplished especially much for the development of the Skete Typikon. Among the skete rules that arose here, the most notable are the Karyes Typikon of St. Sava of Serbia of the 13th century and the cell rule set forth in the so-called Horologion of Theokaras (*Chasoslov Fikary*). The first typikon (“The Typikon for the Cell of Karyes in the Name of St. Sabbas”), intended for two hermits (one elder and one disciple), mandates the daily performance of all church services, at which the entire Psalter is chanted (in summertime daily: the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd staseis, that is, kathismata, at Matins; the 4th and 5th at the Hours; the 6th at the Typika; the 7th at Vespers; the remaining in the cells), the canon unto the Mother of God is performed (at Compline), and a prescribed number of prostrations is executed (in Great Lent — 300 per day). On Saturday and Sunday “the entire church office” is chanted (Vigil with Liturgy). A solemn vow is laid upon the hermit-brother: never to abandon the cell of Karyes (“as long as he liveth let him have no liberty to go forth from this cell... save on account of some great calamity”), “that the Protos [the governor of Mount Athos] have authority in nothing” over this cell; and to partake of meals three times a week (on fasting days) solely of bread and water [^1747]. — An even more elaborate form is exhibited by the rule in the “Horologion” compiled in the 14th century by a certain Athonite monk, Thomas Theokaras (*Phikaras*, a Greek who had visited Rus’), the *Askētikē* (or *hiera*) *akolouthia* (Ἀσκητικὴ [or ἱερὰ] ἀκολουθία), an order of cell rule for Scetiotes adopted on Athos and in Russia [^1748]. This order constitutes a reworking of the corporate church services, adapting the latter for cell usage through the addition of new psalms and especially prayers and devotional formularies. Matins according unto this typikon begins with troparia and a prayer invoking the Holy Spirit (cf. the Roman Catholic Tierce); the Six Psalms are divided by a Trisagion into two halves, each half concluding with a prayer; the hymn **“God is the Lord”** is replaced by verses of the Psalter with the refrain “Alleluia,” prefaced by a special prayer with a petition for the acceptance of our chanting (cf. the *praefatio* in the Roman Catholic Mass); before the canon a special prayer is prescribed; at the ninth ode the additions unto the Biblical Odes take on a Eucharistic/thanksgiving character; after the sixth ode follows the “kontakion of the day, and if there be a gathering (*druzhina*), the canonarch reads the canon of the day or from the Oktoechos, but the solitary reads it upon the completion of the odes and their chanting”; at the ninth ode, before other additions, the Prayer of Manasseh is recited, followed by a special prayer; further at Matins follows the “customary office,” and again a prayer. At Vespers, after the customary beginning, a prayer is indicated and 3 staseis with preface, chanting, and *diadochai* (as at Matins and at the canon); at the end of the third stasis there is also an “apostrophe” (a laudatory address unto God), then a prayer; on feasts a special “chanting,” two apostrophes, *(Orig. p. 396)* and after the appointed kathisma (namely

### The Russian Skete Typikon

Transferred, principally from Mount Athos, unto Russia, the Skete Typikon received here, presumably, further development and complications, concerning the extent of which one cannot judge owing unto the non-existence of Greek originals for the Slavo-Russian witnesses of the Skete Typikon. The most ancient of such witnesses is the “Rule of the Holy Mountain” of the Caves Hegumen Dositheus of the 13th century [^1749]; according unto this rule there is daily prescribed half of the Psalter, 600 Jesus Prayers with 300 or 500 prostrations; for the illiterate, however (in lieu of the Psalter), 7,000 prayers. More intricate and later is the Skete Typikon entitled: *“Tradition According unto the Rule for Monks Dwelling in the Skete Life: The Rule Concerning Cell Sobriety and Daily Chanting (katadnevnom, ‘daily’ with the Greek preposition kata, κατὰ), Which We Also Received from Our Fathers,”* found in manuscripts from the 15th century onward [^1750]. Before Sunday and Thursday (if there be no feast), according unto this typikon there is celebrated first Vespers, apparently ordinary, which is followed by the refectory, spiritual conversations, and reading (after which sleep is also permitted); the Vigil from 1–2 hours after midnight, in place of Vespers, has a special introductory part consisting of 3 staseis; in the 1st stasis are 3 kathismata, a canon unto the Most Holy Theotokos or another with censing at the 8th ode; then a reading with conversation and confession unto the superior for the duration of 2–3 hours; in the 2nd stasis are 3 kathismata and a canon unto Jesus; the 3rd stasis consists of 3 kathismata likewise and a Triadicon canon on Sunday, but on feasts of litiya stichera; then Matins of customary composition, but with only a single kathisma — the Blameless (*Amomos*). If only 2 or 3 brethren gather for the service *(Orig. p. 397)*, then in place of this Vigil there are chanted 10 or all 20 kathismata of the Psalter, divided into staseis of 5 kathismata each. On weekdays Matins begins at dawn directly from Psalm 50 [51] (cf. Matins according unto Basil the Great, above, p. 147). The Hours, Typika, Vespers, and Compline are celebrated according unto the general typikon. In addition, daily there are appointed in the winter half of the year, from September unto April (cf. Western monastic rules above, pp. 309ff.), 10 kathismata, 600–1,000 Jesus Prayers, and 300–600 prostrations, and in summer 7 kathismata and prayer according unto devotion [^1751].

### The Influence of the Skete Typikon

The Skete Typikon exerted a profound influence upon the general church cursus, or at least upon the monastic Typikon. Under its influence the generally accepted order of services received additions, especially at the beginning and conclusion of the offices. Thus, presumably under this influence, the Studite-Alexis Matins in later recensions of this Typikon received at the beginning Psalm 6 [^1752]. In Slavic Horologia of the 13th–14th centuries these accretions are exceedingly intricate. Thus in Sophia MS No. 1052 Vespers and Matins receive the following stratifications: after the customary beginning, Psalm 6, Trisagion, a prayer for the blessing of the service, and **“Lord, have mercy”**

=== LEAF p378 ===
12 times; and at Matins, moreover, at the very beginning a brief “prayer that beginneth all chanting,” preceded by an elaborate order of morning prayers with penitential troparia and Psalms 107 [108], 126 [127], 127 [128], and 121 [122] [LXX] [^1753]. Unto the cell-skete rule of the nocturnal and diurnal hours, as we have observed, the Mesoria (inter-hours), Midnight Office, and elaboration of Compline owe their genesis. From this same typikon, presumably, there infiltrated on fasting services the addition of the Trisagion and 12-fold **“Lord, have mercy”** before the concluding prayer, by virtue of which the conclusion of the service is transformed into what is effectively a separate brief office (*“pochasie”*).

### Menologia: The Constantinopolitan Menologion

By the 9th century the Menologion, as we have seen (see above, pp. 320ff.), had attained less development in the Orthodox East than in the West: there, calendars of the 6th–7th centuries already possess commemorations for all days of the year, whereas Eastern Menologia — for instance, the Constantinopolitan — even in the 8th century have them for no more than half of all days in the year. Furthermore, the East has left far fewer monuments permitting us to judge concerning its most archaic calendar. The greatest number of such monuments dating from the 9th century and periods proximate unto it has survived from the Church of Constantinople, whence one may conclude that the local Constantinopolitan calendar achieved broad dissemination throughout the East, displacing local calendars everywhere, wherever such had existed. Thus, the Menologion appended unto the 10th-century Gospel written in Jerusalem, and unto the 11th-century Syriac Gospel from Antioch [^1754], is of the exact type *(Orig. p. 398)* that had already emerged in the 8th-century Constantinopolitan Menologion (described above, pp. 322–323) and is represented by a multitude of Greek manuscripts and recensions of the 9th–11th centuries [^1755].

### Local Menologia: Georgian, Antiochene, Neapolitan, and Sicilian

Gradually the Menologion acquires commemorations for all days of the year [^1756], initially a single commemoration per day [^1757], and subsequently ever more [^1758]. Yet it was not swiftly that this almost uniform Constantinopolitan type of Menologion (differences across varying recensions affect for the most part the dates of only 5 or 6 commemorations in the year) secured universal acceptance across the Orthodox East: alongside it there persisted in certain places Menologia of a distinctive, local type, sometimes revealing kinship and a quasi-succession with the Syriac Menologion of the 4th century (see above, p. 277), and sometimes endeavoring to impart unto themselves a more ecumenical character through the incorporation of Western and other commemorations. Of the first kind is the Menologion provided by the Georgian Festal Menaion with Triodion interpolations in the manuscript of the St. Petersburg Imperial Public Library MS No. 11 (from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius Uspensky) of the 9th century, which has on December 28 the Apostles Peter and Paul; on December 29 John the Evangelist; on January 1 Hierarchs (all of them); on January 29 Venerable Fathers; on January 29

the Apparition of the Cross unto Emperor Constantine; on June 25 the “Nativity of John”; on September 27 the “Dumbness of Zacharias” [^1759]. Such is the most ancient *(Orig. p. 399)* Antiochene Menologion, insofar as it can be reconstructed by aid of the works of St. John Chrysostom and Patriarch Severus of Antioch, and from two Syro-Phoenician Festal Menaia of the 9th century (in the manuscripts of the Sinai Library MSS Nos. 233 and 234): December 24 commemoration of all the Forefathers and Prophets; 25 the Nativity of Christ; 26 commemoration of the Mother of God; 27 St. Stephen; 28 the Apostles Peter and Paul; 29 John the Evangelist and James his brother; 30 the Apostles Thomas and James the Brother of the Lord; 31 all the Apostles; January 1 Basil the Great; 7 John the Forerunner; 15 or 20 the Egyptian Fathers; 24 Paul the Simple (disciple of Antony the Great); 25 Babylas of Antioch; 27 commemorations of (all) the Martyrs; 28 the Apparition of the Cross unto Constantine; 29 St. Ignatius the God-Bearer [^1760]. Among the Menologia converging with Western calendars, the most remarkable is the so-called “Neapolitan Marble Calendar,” presumably of the 9th century, with the latest commemorations from the 7th century, discovered on the reverse side of marble door-frames in the Neapolitan church of St. John the Baptist (6th century). ### Recensions of the Constantinopolitan Menologion: The Menologion of the Great Church

Nevertheless, the Constantinopolitan type of Menologion acquires everywhere a predominant importance, and this principally because, with its further development, it strives increasingly to smooth away its local coloration and become universal throughout the whole Church. This is as yet little observed in so ancient a representative of the Constantinopolitan Menologion as the Typikon of the Great Church (see above, pp. 336–337). Its foundation is some ancient Menologion containing martyrs alone, like the Syriac of the 4th century; Old Testament saints, apostles, hierarchs, and venerables are placed sometimes after the martyrs, but more often between the martyrs of a given day;

=== LEAF p380 ===
this latter circumstance being a sign that these commemorations — martyrs following non-martyrs — were interpolated later. In general, the Menologion was visibly composed with great gradualness and was not edited rigorously by any single individual: duplications of commemorations occur even on the same day with a slight variant of the name. There are from 5 to 7 commemorations per day, many with historical notices. Among Old Testament saints, Ezekiel and Haggai are absent, and an office is provided only for Elisha; among the Apostles, there is no commemoration of James the son of Alphaeus and no offices for Simon, Jude, and Matthew; there is no Synaxis of the Seventy Apostles, though commemorations of certain among them are present; there are no Syrian venerables. Of 130 commemorations with offices, 18 are Constantinopolitan.

### The Menologion of Emperor Basil

The next monument of the Constantinopolitan Menologion in terms of chronology and importance is the “Menologion of Emperor Basil” (not Basil I the Macedonian [† 889], but Basil II the Bulgar-Slayer [† 1025], since there are commemorations from the close of the 10th century), preserved for September through February in a parchment manuscript, presumably the original, adorned with gold and precious stones, in the Vatican Library. Half of this manuscript is occupied by highly artistic miniatures depicting the feats and deaths of the saints, which often take the place of the narratives concerning the saints themselves; the Menologion was transcribed at the behest of the designated emperor (in the prologue it is stated that the Emperor himself “composed” it). The supply of the missing months is found in the Menologion provided by the *Eulogadion* in an 11th-century manuscript and by Menaia in manuscripts of the 11th–13th centuries in the Cryptaferrata (Grottaferrata) Library, where the narratives for the first half of the year coincide literally with the Vatican manuscript (save for the absence of miniatures), while in the second half of the year all the methods of description, style, and length of the biographical notices (the customary length being 70 words, excluding particles) remain identical. The total number of commemorations in the Menologion is approximately 800; of our present commemorations, up to 350 are still absent; many venerables are lacking (of Syrian ones only 6), as well as saints of the 8th–10th centuries, Western saints (e.g., Cassian, Hilary), and Sicilian and Cretan saints; the Holy Mandylion (*Ubrus*) on August 16 is absent, as are the Procession of the Cross on August 1 and the Synaxis of the Seventy Apostles; there are approximately 50 commemorations superabundant *(Orig. p. 401)* in comparison with the present calendar (for instance, Empress Irene on August 7). The compiler evidently had before him the Typikon of the Great Church, repeating its commemorations for many days even in identical order (martyrs frequently standing even ahead of apostles), but he visibly desired to soften the local coloration of the Menologion: of the Constantinopolitan Patriarchs, none of those who were in previous calendars are present (for instance, Nectarius, Gennadius [5th century], Paul II [7th century]), the only additions being Patriarchs Germanus, Tarasius, and Antony II († 896); the first 4 Ecumenical Councils each have a separate day, whereas the Constantinopolitan Councils have a collective day; he endeavors to avoid repetition of commemorations, outstripping in this regard even our present calendar: Great Martyr Euphemia appears solely on July 11, with no commemoration on September 16; St. Maximus solely on August 17, with no commemoration on January 21 [^1763].
