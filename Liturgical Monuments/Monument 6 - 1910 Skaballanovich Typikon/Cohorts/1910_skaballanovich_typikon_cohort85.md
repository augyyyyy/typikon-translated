The hymn **"More Honorable"** is the heirmos of the 9th ode of the triode of Great and Holy Friday, **"Unto Thee I rise early"** (*K Tebe utrenyuyu*); consequently, it belongs unto St. Cosmas of Maiuma, who utilized for it the expression of St. Ephrem the Syrian concerning the Mother of God: "More honorable than the Cherubim and beyond compare of all the heavenly hosts" [^2589]. According unto tradition (of Nikephoros Kallistos), the hymn was composed on Great Friday itself [^2590].

As regards the fortune in the West of the **"Magnificat"** ("Magnificat", a loose translation of the Greek Μεγαλύνει, subjunctive instead of indicative), first of all it must be noted that certain Latin manuscripts of the Gospel and certain writers prior unto the 4th century read in Luke 1:46 "Elizabeth said" (*ait Elisabeth*), and not "Mary"; consequently, they attribute the canticle unto Elizabeth, for which certain modern scholars (for example, Harnack) find grounds both in the text of the canticle and even in our exclamation before the canticle: "The Theotokos… in songs let us magnify" [^2591]. The introduction of the canticle into liturgical use in the West is attributed unto Caesarius of Arles (†542) [^2592]. From Gregory the Great or, rather, Benedict, this canticle came to be used here at Vespers, which "as an evening sacrifice of praise and thanksgiving culminated in it, as in a spiritual sweet-smelling savour par excellence" [^2593], which is expressed also by the censing of the altar taking place at this canticle; such usage constitutes a parallel unto the Canticle of Zechariah at Matins (there the impending salvation, here the accomplished salvation) and the hymn **"Glory to God in the highest"** at the Liturgy, and corresponds unto the evening disposition of the believer, grateful for all the good received during the day. The *Magnificat* is always accompanied and concluded, and in the Ambrosian rite also preceded, by an antiphon appointed for the commemoration of the day (*proprium*) [^2594], which in antiquity was chanted after each verse of the canticle (Introductory Chapters, 307, 317). (In the Mozarabic rite the *Magnificat* is chanted only on certain feasts at *Laudes* with an antiphon before and after [^2595]—*ibid.*, 318). Among the Lutherans the *Magnificat* is likewise chanted at Vespers, but in German translation, with the sub-chanting unto each verse of a short hymn composed in adaptation unto the Biblical text, wherein sometimes one choir chants a verse of the Biblical canticle and the other chants this refrain.

### Melody

=== LEAF p842 ===
Concerning the melody for the **Magnificat** in the present place of the Typikon it is stated only: "chanting with a loud voice." Yet in the rule of September 1, which is distinguished in general *(Orig. p. 696)* by its detail, since this is the 1st day of the ecclesiastical year, the tone is also indicated for this canticle—the 6th (that this instruction possesses general significance for all days is evident from the tones of the kanon for September 1: 1st, 4th, and 8th) and, of course, in its kanonic melody. This is also natural, because the refrain unto the canticle is constituted by the heirmos from the kanon of Tone 6 (**"Unto Thee I rise early"**). Musical tradition shows, however, that this is not the ordinary kanonic melody of Tone 6, but a special, automelon [*samoglasny*] melody.

### According unto Ancient Typika

Greek manuscripts (late) of the Jerusalem Typikon and the "Order" of Patriarch Philotheos express themselves concerning the **More Honorable** only with the word "is chanted according unto verses" (στιχολογεῖται, —οῦμεν); Slavic manuscripts say "is chanted"; the tone is not indicated even in the rule of September 1. The Old Believer Typikon indicates the tone here as well (see below, p. 697). Among Catholics and Lutherans the *Magnificat* is chanted in the 9th tone and constitutes a favorite subject for artistic compositions [^2596].

### Before the Magnificat

The 9th ode of the kanon, or more specifically the Canticle of the Theotokos opening it, is singled out from the series of the others by a special exclamation of the deacon before it, inviting unto the glorification of the Mother of God: **"The Theotokos and Mother of the Light let us magnify in songs"** [^2597], whereby the necessity of special attentiveness unto the canticle is marked (cf. "Let us attend"). In the exclamation the Most Holy Theotokos is named Mother of the Light in correspondence with the glorification of God soon to follow for the appearance of the morning light. The exclamation is pronounced "after the censing of the holy table" (according unto Chapter 9—during the censing), usually upon going forth from the altar before the icon of the Mother of God.

### According unto Ancient Typika

The exclamation without the words "Mother of the Light" serves as an epigraph unto the Canticle of the Theotokos, just as all the other Biblical canticles have epigraphs (Ode 1: **"Let us sing unto the Lord, for He is gloriously glorified"**; Ode 3: **"Holy art Thou, O Lord, and Thee my spirit hymneth"**). In such a form (without "Mother of the Light") the exclamation is found in Slavic Sluzhebniki prior unto 1647 (the Moscow 1658 edition is silent concerning it) [^2598]. ### Bows at the "More Honorable"

The Canticle of the Theotokos is singled out not only from the series of the other odes of the kanon, but from the entire Vigil, by the fact that here alone the Typikon directly requires bows, inviting thereby also to express one's veneration of God in view of the approaching conclusion of the service and its festal hymns; yet in view of the prohibition of kneeling on Sundays—only small bows: "We make also small bows" (it is understood: at each verse). By this similar bows in other places of the service are not excluded, but they are simply not considered there as necessary as here, or in general for some reason are not indicated (perhaps through oversight). With great detail they are indicated in the order of weekday service, and by these instructions one can be guided for Sunday and festal services, replacing prostrations with small bows (as indeed the Old Believers do).

### According unto Ancient Typika

=== LEAF p844 ===
Ancient Typika required a single bow at the 9th ode (see p. 693), assigning it unto its conclusion, as is evident from the note concerning this bow at the Nativity of Christ and elsewhere even in the present Typikon. Subsequently this bow was moved back unto the beginning of the 9th ode, even unto the katavasia of the 8th ode; consequently, other bows were likewise required at the 9th ode itself, assigned more specifically unto the Canticle of the Theotokos. The last stage prior unto the present practice is represented by the Old Believer Typikon: "Be it known also this, that always at the end of the 8th ode, when we wish to begin the 9th ode, we all make a metania once, saying equally: **We praise, we bless…**—we chant according unto verses the 9th ode, chanting with a loud voice **More honorable than the Cherubim**, both choirs make a bow from the waist, and at the last one unto the ground."

### Censing at the "More Honorable"

Censing, being an intensification of prayer and contributing unto the elevation of a prayerful disposition, accompanies the most important parts of divine worship, and throughout the entire Vigil it is performed 4 times. This is the last time. As concluding, it, like all concluding hymns in each series of songs, is dedicated unto the Most Holy Theotokos; it is Her veneration, just as the censing at the Blameless [*Amomoi*] is the veneration of the risen Christ, and at the Polyeleos Megalynarion—of the celebrated saint. As less important than that censing, it is performed by a single deacon, or by the priest if no deacon serves. Hence the ambiguity in the Typikon:
"At the conclusion of the 8th ode, having censed the holy table, the deacon exclaims: **The Theotokos and Mother of the Light…**—The priest or deacon censes the holy altar and the whole church and all the brethren. After the 9th ode, having returned and given up the censer, *(Orig. p. 698)* he says the litany, and the exclamation is from the priest"
(at weekday service, when the deacon does not participate in the serving, this censing is directed to be performed by the priest—see Chapter 9 of the Typikon).

### The Litany after Ode 9

With the 9th ode the third and final division of the kanon concludes, which possesses a conclusion similar unto the preceding two divisions, that is, first of all the little litany. Its exclamation: **"For all the powers of heaven praise Thee"**, on the one hand, at the completion of the lengthy hymn represented by the kanon with its approximately 112 canticles, expresses a humble acknowledgment of the unworthiness of our praise in comparison with heavenly praise; on the other hand, it prefigures the theme of the subsequent part of Matins, linking as it were thereby the end of its 3rd part with the beginning of the 4th.

### Primordial Antiquity of Its Exclamation

Such an exclamation is possessed by the litany here in absolutely all monuments that indicate the litany.

### "Holy is the Lord our God"

If already the 6th ode had after itself a chant more solemn than the 3rd (a kontakion, and not a sessional hymn), then the 9th must have something yet more solemn, something exceptional.

=== LEAF p846 ===
Such indeed proves to be, on Sundays only however, a hymn without a title, but performed according unto the order of **"God is the Lord"**: **"Holy is the Lord our God"** (*Hagios Kyrios ho Theos hemon* [Gr. Ἅγιος Κύριος ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν]), that is, Psalm 98:9 [LXX], the concluding words of this verse; a hymn having, like a prokeimenon, two verses: the 1st verse—the identical passage of the psalm, read with the preceding word: "For (*hoti* [Gr. ὅτι]) holy is the Lord our God"; the 2nd verse—the conclusion of the 2nd verse of the identical psalm: "over all the peoples", with the addition from the basic verse of the words: "our God." Intruding by its distinctive execution (according unto the indicated order with exclamation and chanting) into the smooth flow of the longer preceding and subsequent *(Orig. p. 699)* hymns, this brief hymn by its suddenness directs the special and intensified attention of the worshippers unto a single attribute of God, more than ever revealed unto the world by the Savior's death upon the Cross—an attribute foundational, awesome, and as it were formidable for us, the contemplation of which, however, affords the most exhaustive and most blessed penetration into the Godhead, as is evident from the fact that this attribute alone serves as the subject of the Seraphic praise unto God. This hymn, which was, as discussed below, an exapostilarion and nowadays at times replaces the exapostilarion (on Palm Sunday and Great and Holy Saturday), clearly serves, judging by the manner of its execution, as an identical preparation for the Sunday exapostilarion as **"God is the Lord"** serves for the troparion, whereby the Sunday exapostilarion is singled out from the series of the others. It is chanted (by the choirs alternately, thrice in all) with its preliminary exclamation in the appointed tone, that is, with its solo chanting in this tone, moreover not by the canonarch, but by the deacon, and with an identical chanting, naturally, of the verses as well (usually both the one and the other are simply exclaimed), with the silent participation of the priest. "Then (after the exclamation of the litany) the deacon exclaims: **Holy is the Lord our God** in the appointed tone. Verse 1: **For…**—The priest stands at the customary place."

**"Holy is the Lord"** is the most frequent exapostilarion in the Studite and Evergetis (somewhat less frequently) Typika; for example, it is appointed in the former on September 1, 6, 7, 8, 13, 14, etc. [^2605]; in the latter—on September 4, 7, 8, 13, 14, etc. [^2606]. According unto both Typika this same exapostilarion (Evergetis: *hexapostilarion*), and it alone, was appointed on Sunday, wherein according unto the latter—it was chanted in the tone of the week [^2607]. Its appearance at this place of Matins is explained by the proximity of the end of Matins unto the Liturgy (cf. **"One is Holy"** at the Great Doxology; see Introductory Chapters, 86, 87; cf. the *Kaddish* prayer—with the Seraphic doxology—in the synagogue service—Introductory Chapters, 16, 17).

### The Exapostilarion [Svitylen]

The hymn **"Holy is the Lord"**, executed according unto the order of **"God is the Lord"**, serves as a preparation for the exapostilarion [*svitylen*], which occupies here, after the 9th ode of the kanon, an identical position unto that which the sessional hymn occupies after the 3rd ode, and the kontakion after the 6th. If the kontakion surpasses the sessional hymn in solemnity, then the very place of the exapostilarion at the conclusion of the kanon, after such an ode as the 9th ode, compels one to anticipate from it an even greater solemnity. The exapostilarion, indeed, occupies an exceptional position in divine worship. This is a hymn of Matins repeated nowhere else. As its very name shows (*svitylen* [Hymn of Light, Gr. φωταγωγικόν]), it hymns God as Light and the Giver of Light; thus, the exapostilaria of Lenten

=== LEAF p848 ===
Matins are all filled with the concept of light (**"Thou Who art Light, O Christ, enlighten me through Thyself"**—Tone 8); weekday exapostilaria frequently make use of this concept (**"By Thy holy Angels"** or **"saints"**, **"Who hast enlightened the whole earth"**—exapostilarion of Monday and Saturday); so likewise the exapostilaria of the greatest feasts (the Nativity of Christ: **"Our Savior, the Dayspring from the East, hath visited us from on high, and we who were in darkness and the shadow have found the truth"**; Theophany: **"… hath enlightened those who sat sleeping in darkness and the shadow, for the unapproachable Light hath come and appeared"**; Pentecost: **"The Father is Light, the Word is Light, and the Holy Spirit is Light"**; the Transfiguration: **"O changeless Light, O Word, Light of the unbegotten Father, in Thy manifest light today on Tabor have we seen the Father as Light, and the Spirit as Light, guiding all creation by light"**). In truth, however, such content is not mandatory for an exapostilarion: usually they simply glorify the celebrated event (cf. **"Having fallen asleep in the flesh"**), for which reason exapostilaria are more frequently termed *hexapostilaria* [Gr. ἐξαποστειλάρια].

The designation "svitylen" [Hymn of Light] is invariably applied only unto Lenten exapostilaria, which all speak of light; whereas the hymns occupying their place at weekday, Sunday, and festal Matins in Greek liturgical books are called only *hexapostilaria*; in Slavic books, however, both one and the other; in relation unto weekday hymns the designation *svitylen* is applied in the Typikon, Chapters 9 and 16, while the designation *exapostilaria* is in the Octoechos and the Horologion [*Sledovannaya Psaltir'*]; unto Sunday hymns—the designation *svitylen* is in the Typikon here, while *exapostilaria* is in the Octoechos; in relation unto festal hymns—*svitylen* is in the Menaion, and *exapostilaria* in the Triodion.

The name "exapostilarion" (*exapostilarion* [Gr. ἐξαποστειλάριον] from *exapostello* [Gr. ἐξαποστέλλω]—I send forth, dispatch) is explained either by the fact that they glorify the sending forth of the Apostles unto the preaching, of which mention is indeed made in the Sunday exapostilaria (Mark of Ephesus) [^2613], yet only in the Sunday ones, and by no means in all of them; or, what is more probable, by the fact that for chanting it a chanter goes forth into the center of the church [^2614], which even nowadays is required for certain exapostilaria (see Great and Holy Monday). — The melody of the exapostilaria *(Orig. p. 701)* does not follow the melody of the tone, but is completely automelon [*samoglasen*] (close unto the kontakion melody), whereby the exapostilarion is likewise singled out from all the hymns of Matins. On great feasts it, like the troparion, is chanted thrice.

### History

The exapostilarion is mentioned in the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* [^2615]. In the Jerusalem *Akolouthia* of the 9th–12th centuries it is indicated at the present place of Matins, with the addition of a prosomoion [*podoben*] or the note "automelon" [*samoglasen*], sometimes with an indication of the tone [^2616].

### Sunday Exapostilaria

Sunday exapostilaria are distinguished from the other changeable hymns of the Sunday Vigil in that they are closely bound up with the content of the morning Gospel and therefore glorify the multifaceted celebrated event from a single aspect, moreover from an even more particular aspect than that unfolded in the Gospel: in them "it is spoken of the going of the myrrh-bearing women unto the Apostles and of the Apostles unto the Gentiles for the preaching of the Resurrection" [^2617]. An identical content, but apparently broader, among the hymns of Sunday Matins is possessed further only by the stichera at "Glory…" of the Praises [Lauds], which are close in placement unto the exapostilaria, and which for that reason are already directly termed "evangelical" [*eothina*]. Whereas festal exapostilaria are appointed to be chanted twice, sometimes thrice, the Sunday exapostilarion is chanted only once, perhaps because it is twice as long as all the other exapostilaria, or because **"Holy is the Lord"** is also, properly speaking, an exapostilarion. Sunday exapostilaria, like the exapostilaria of saints and in contrast unto great-festal exapostilaria, have, as do all Sunday hymns, a Theotokion. Whereas all Sunday Theotokia, excluding the unchangeable ones (after the troparia **"The Angelic Council"** and after the Praises stichera), glorify the Mother of God without relation unto the event of the Resurrection, very rarely hinting at the latter by isolated expressions, each exapostilarion Theotokion is entirely dedicated unto the depiction of that participation which She took in this event (**"With the disciples Thou didst rejoice…"**—Exapostilarion 1; **"When Thou didst see Thy Son risen from the tomb…"**—Exapostilarion 9; **"Unto the Lord Who rose on the third day from the tomb, O Virgin, pray…"**—Exapostilarion 10), with the sole exception of Exapostilarion 8. Such exceptional content of these Theotokia fully corresponds unto the exceptional character in the series of hymns of the exapostilarion, which, like the hypakoe and the kontakion, is chanted only once at the Vigil. The Sunday joy, as it were continually intensifying and more fully encompassing the whole being of the worshippers, toward the end of the kanon reaches that degree when the worshipper can no longer think of anything except the Resurrection; therefore thenceforth the Theotokia also (after the exapostilarion and the Praises) become resurrectional, while the final Sunday troparion hath no Theotokion at all. *(Orig. p. 702)*

If a saint hath an exapostilarion (and the latter is possessed also by many minor saints—see, for example, September 27), then at "Glory…" his exapostilarion is chanted, but at "Both now…": not the Theotokion of the Menaion exapostilarion (which always hath a Theotokion attached unto itself), but the Sunday Theotokion. Thus it is done even for great saints. This is in view of the special—resurrectional—

=== LEAF p850 ===
content of these Theotokia. Only on Feasts of the Lord (which unite their service with the Sunday service), Feasts of the Theotokos, and their forefeasts and afterfeasts is the Sunday Theotokion of the exapostilarion replaced by their exapostilarion.

### History

The Sunday exapostilaria were composed (probably together with their Theotokia), as their inscription in the Octoechos likewise indicates, by the Byzantine Emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus (912–959), the son of Emperor Leo VI the Wise, the author of the morning evangelical stichera. Such authorship secured for them rapid dissemination (cf. **"Only-Begotten Son"**, the creation of Emperor Justinian): in the capacity even of model melodies [prosomoia] they are already indicated in the well-known Jerusalem *Akolouthia* of the 9th–12th centuries; judging by the fact that in all three instances of such indications the 1st Sunday exapostilarion (**"Let us go up with the disciples"**) is invariably named as the model melody [^2618], one may think that all 11 exapostilaria possessed a single melody. Mention is made of them, under the general name, however, of "exapostilaria", by the Studite Typika of Western recensions [^2619], but for some reason they are not known unto the Studite-Alexian and Evergetis Typika: perhaps they were excluded here from the service when the Gospel was transferred first unto the 6th ode of the kanon, and then before the kanon.
