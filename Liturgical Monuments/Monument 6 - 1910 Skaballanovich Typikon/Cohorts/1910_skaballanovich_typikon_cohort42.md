=== LEAF p411 ===
conducted among them in good order, especially in church, to preserve the mind unenticed by anything worldly, to go unto church at the first bell-ringing (*blagovest*), to stand there attentively from the very beginning; with particular reverence and attentiveness must one stand during the Six Psalms; entering into the church at that time is strictly forbidden; those unable to stand throughout it thus—the elderly and the sick—let them stand in the narthex; attentive, slow chanting with a meek, even, and clear voice is required; conversations are forbidden upon the cliroi, as well as at the refectory: at the latter it is likewise forbidden to look about or where one should not, for example, at how others eat; it is forbidden also to murmur against the quality of the food served [^1823]. Then, under the special title: "Of the Monastery of Stoudios," a series of disciplinary excerpts from the *Hypotyposis* is given: concerning the monastic officials, concerning confession unto the hegumen at the 4th ode of the canon, concerning an elder-brother appointed during the Forty-Day Fast to remind of death, concerning a two-to-three-week trial prior unto admission into the monastery, concerning the reading of books from the library on feast days, concerning the hour of the Divine Liturgy during fasts, concerning proportion in the services, concerning a penance for one who breaks dishware at the refectory, and concerning the quantity of clothing [^1824]. Following the exposition of the order of the Divine Liturgy, the aforementioned MSS of the Typikon contain two further disciplinary articles: concerning the three ranks of feasts from Nikon of the Black Mountain (see above, p. 408) and the following "Canons" (*κανόνες*) of Nicephorus, Patriarch of Constantinople: dispensation for an ailing monk during the Forty-Day Fast to take cooked food, oil, and wine; prohibition against traveling on Sunday except under necessity and constraint; prohibition against receiving Communion from a priest who does not fast on Wednesday and Friday; rejection of apocryphal books: the Apocalypses of Paul, Ezra, and Zosimas, the "Two Martyrdoms" of St. George, of Cyricus and Julitta, and the books of Macarius and Diadochus; permission for laboring monks during the Forty-Day Fast to eat bread at the 9th hour [3:00 PM] and to sup in the evening, with a prohibition against relaxing unto oil and wine under pretext of labor; permission for hired workmen to partake of food on non-fasting days from the 3rd hour [9:00 AM], and during fasts at the 6th hour [12:00 PM] (according unto the Holy Mountain Typikon, see above, p. 422). In Slavic copies of the Jerusalem Typikon, almost all these disciplinary rubrics disappear, at least as specialized sections; but in place of this, as we have seen, there appear in these Typika our present disciplinary excerpts from Nikon of the Black Mountain (in MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 329/384 of the 14th century, still in a separate gathering sewn unto the front; in later MSS, for example No. 678/386 of the 15th century, already in the very *(Orig. p. 434)* text: apparently, these excerpts were introduced into the Typikon by Athanasius of Vysotsk; see above, p. 375).

### The Markian Chapters

Thus, by the 13th–14th centuries, all the most essential parts of the Typikon had gradually taken shape: besides the fundamental Menologion-Triodion section, there was also the general liturgical section (the daily cycle of services) and the disciplinary section. Later

=== LEAF p412 ===
than these parts, there entered into the Typikon as an independent and separate section yet another part: liturgical rubrics for the coincidence of fixed feasts with Sundays or movable feasts. In the most ancient Typika, these rubrics are very brief and indefinite, being provided in the Menologion under the corresponding days of the month. Thus, already in the Studite-Alexis Typikon under September 8, the observation is made that if it fall upon a Sunday, "the Sunday service is chanted first," and that one must act likewise if the memory of an apostle or evangelist fall upon a Sunday; of the details of the service, an instruction is given only regarding the troparia at the conclusion of Vespers and at the Divine Liturgy. Under March 25, the identical observation (in the Studite-Alexis Typikon) is very extensive; it provides for the coincidence of the Annunciation with an ordinary Saturday and Sunday, with Lazarus Saturday, Palm Sunday, all days of Holy Week, and with the Sunday and Monday of Holy Pascha, providing rubrics regarding all stichera, troparia, and canons. This exhausts the material in the Studite-Alexis Typikon corresponding unto the content of our present Markian Chapters. These observations are not inscribed here under the name of Mark. The identical form and scope are possessed by these observations in the Western Studite Typika and in the Evergetis Typikon; here too the name of Mark does not appear over them, which is particularly characteristic for the Western Studite Typika in view of their proximity unto the place of activity of Mark, Bishop of Otranto. The identical scope and form are possessed by the rubrics concerning the coincidence of fixed feasts with movable ones in the most ancient copies of the Jerusalem Typikon, the Greek MS of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum, Sevastyanov collection No. 35/491, and the Georgian Shiomghvime MS; here these rubrics are very close unto the Studite ones, with the exception, naturally, of the peculiarities of Jerusalem practice, and likewise do not bear the name of Mark. In later Greek copies of the Jerusalem Typikon, as already in MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 381 of the 13th–14th centuries, these rubrics are supplemented by marginal annotations, so that around the Annunciation the margins of several pages are covered with them. But in such an ancient copy of the Jerusalem Typikon as the Greek MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 456, probably of the 13th century, all observations of this kind are gathered into one place, divided into 95 chapters, and inscribed: "Compendium (*σύνταγμα*) of Mark, sinful hieromonk, compiled concerning the doubtful matters (*ἀποῤῥούμενα*) of the Typikon" (fol. 165). Thereafter, in the most ancient Slavic Typika, MSS of the Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 328/383 and 329/384 of the 14th century, there is an identical compendium of these observations with the heading (in the first): "Of Mark the sinful hieromonk concerning the omissions of the Typikon" (fol. 189). Subsequently, in many subsequent Slavic and Greek MSS, such a compendium is found with varying numbers of chapters and *(Orig. p. 435)* with an identical name in the inscription, for example: "Of the most sacred Mark of Hydrous [Otranto]," "Of Mark, most holy oeconomos," and the like. Unbound, as previously, unto the days of the month, these compendia, the attribution of which unto Mark, Bishop of Otranto of the 9th–10th centuries (see above, p. 413), rests solely upon the witness of these MSS of the

=== LEAF p413 ===
Typikon, acquired here the capacity to expand significantly and to generalize their content—to provide rubrics for the coincidence with Sundays and movable feasts not only of individual feasts, but of entire classes thereof: such as a feast of Christ in general or of the Theotokos with a Sunday; the forefeast, afterfeast, or Apodosis of a Great Feast with a Sunday; a great (vigil-rank), polyeleos, or six-stichera saint with a Sunday, with a Saturday, and the like. This we observe already in the most ancient recensions of the Markian Chapters bearing his name (for example, the Greek Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 456 of the 13th century, and the Slavic Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 of the 14th century). In later Slavic manuscripts, as already in No. 678/386 of the 15th century, these compendia are divided into two parts: the first, containing general rubrics concerning feasts of the Lord and of the Theotokos and their forefeasts and afterfeasts on a Sunday, and concerning saints on Sunday and Saturday, is placed separately in the first general part of the Typikon setting forth the daily services, and the name of Mark is for the most part not inscribed over it; while the second, bearing the name of Mark, is distributed according unto the days of the months (that is, it assumes its original form). So it is likewise in our present Typikon, though the general part is abridged: treating only of saints on Sunday and Saturday. In yet later Slavic manuscripts from the 16th century onward, for example MSS of the Museum of the Kyiv Theological Academy Aa 194 and 196, rubrics are added concerning the coincidence of temple feasts with movable feasts (the "Temple Chapters" of our present Typikon). In Greek manuscripts, however, up unto the printed edition of the Greek Typikon, and within that printed edition itself, the Markian Chapters are placed in a single section, though they are subsequently repeated under the days of the months (in a shorter, more ancient recension), while the temple chapters are absent.

### Compendia of Brief Hymns in the Typikon and Similar Material

Yet even this is not all that entered into the composition of ancient Typika and enters into our present one. Already the most ancient Studite-Alexis Typikon in MS of the Moscow Synodal Typography Library No. 285/142/1206 of the 11th–12th centuries places upon its pages, alongside rubrical observations, also the most essential and most frequently employed brief hymns, such as: prokeimena, alleluiaria, kontakia, and troparia. Such material, in greater or lesser volume, enters likewise into all subsequent copies of the Typikon. Other sections of more or less related content also find their way into it: for example, notices concerning the lives of commemorated saints (in the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th–10th centuries), the order of the Divine Liturgy (MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 381), expositions of essential dogmas, and the Paschal Tables (in late Greek copies).

### The Architecture and Plan of the Typikon in its Historical Evolution

=== LEAF p414 ===
With the identical gradualness wherewith the content was composed and the volume of the Typikon grew, its plan also was elaborated. The simplest plan is found in the Typikon *(Orig. p. 436)* of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th–10th [printed: 9th–5th] centuries, which possesses only rubrical observations for various days of the year (of the Menologion and Triodion). But as prototypes of the plan of our present Typikon served the *Hypotyposis*, attributed unto St. Theodore the Studite, and its revision, the *Diatyposis* of St. Athanasius. Beginning with an exposition of the Paschal service (a special section entitled: "How we proceed in the assemblies of the holy and glorious three-day Resurrection"), these monuments present in the first place a series of purely liturgical, disconnected observations: concerning the services of Pentecost, the Fast of the Holy Apostles, and onward for the most essential fixed feasts and periods of the ecclesiastical year, followed by the Forty-Day Fast (a special section "Concerning the Holy Forty-Day Fast"); in the disciplinary part that follows, discussion treats first of the monastic officials, of comportment during divine services, and following several minor observations of a disciplinary character, the monuments proceed unto the monastic refectory table (in the sections "Concerning the quantity and quality of food and drink and concerning good order at tables," "Concerning the holy and great Forty-Day Fast," and "Concerning the Annunciation," the last paragraph being of more liturgical content), unto the labors of the monks ("Concerning proportion in services"), and unto their clothing ("Concerning the quantity of clothing, footwear, the arrangement of beds, and the like"—only in the *Hypotyposis*). The identical plan is preserved in general by the Studite-Alexis Typikon, which presents first the Triodion rubrics (from the Sunday of the Publican), then the Menologion (in MSS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 333/381 of the 14th century and No. 906/382 of the 15th century, the Menologion comes earlier—a later transposition, as demonstrated by the preface unto the Menologion in the second MS); the disciplinary part that follows initially seems likewise intended to follow the *Hypotyposis*, speaking first of the table; but subsequently it abandons a consistent plan, endeavoring merely to arrange subjects by some resemblance: as, for example, the paragraphs concerning monastic officials stand almost all side by side, as do the paragraphs concerning comportment during divine worship. The Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th–10th centuries initiated a different plan of Typika, placing the Menologion prior unto the Triodion. Such an arrangement is maintained by all the Western Studite Typika and the Evergetis Typikon. The Typikon of the Great Church and the Western Studite Typika do not possess a disciplinary section, but the Typika of the *Calabrian Recension* (of the Monastery of St. Nicholas of Casole) begin with a general liturgical section containing (in 28 chapters) instructions concerning various points of the daily cycle of worship: for example, concerning the chanting of **"God is the Lord"** and the Alleluia, concerning the bell-ringing, concerning the censing, concerning the kathismata at Matins and Vespers on simple days and on feasts, concerning the suspension of prostrations on certain days, concerning kathismata in fasts and collaterally concerning the table on ordinary days and during fasts, concerning the stichera at services—Sunday, weekday, and festal—concerning the canons at various services, concerning readings, and concerning Compline [^1825]. Similar chapters were found also in Typika of the Sicilian Recension, but situated between

=== LEAF p415 ===
the Menologion and the Triodion [^1826]. The Evergetis Typikon, following the Menologion and Triodion portions, presents the order of Sunday Vigil and the Divine Liturgy, makes general observations concerning weekday *(Orig. p. 437)* services, provides the liturgical prokeimena, Sunday and weekday, the alleluia verses of Vespers, speaks of Saturday Vespers and the panikhida for the departed, of the comportment of the canonarch, of censing, and of entering and exiting the church during the service, and thereafter in a special "Prologue" presents the disciplinary part of the Typikon ("Exposition and Depiction of Life in the Monastery"). As it were a third epoch in the elaboration of the plan for the Typikon was created by the Jerusalem Typika, which, retaining the order of Constantinople and the Western Studite Typika in having the Triodion follow the Menologion, prefixed unto this "Synaxarion" and principal part of the Typikon a general liturgical and disciplinary part; wherein, in accordance with their chief distinctive feature—the Sunday Vigils—they began the Typikon precisely with the order of this Vigil and of Sunday services in general, continuing it with the order of weekday and Saturday services, at times also of festal services, and proceeding thereafter unto general rubrical observations, unto fasts, and in general unto the order of monastic life; while following the Synaxarion part there followed, so to speak, "appendices"—troparia, prokeimena, temple chapters, and the Paschal Tables. Yet this composition grew and became complicated gradually. The most ancient recension of the Jerusalem Typikon, represented by the Georgian Shiomghvime MS and the oldest Greek MSS of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum, Sevastyanov collection No. 491/35, and of the Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 456 and 381 (13th–14th centuries), contains the following sections: the order of Small Vespers (Greek only); ### The Plan of the Greek Printed Editions of the Jerusalem Typikon

Beyond these supplementary articles, the development of the Jerusalem Typikon in its Greek copies did not advance, as shown by its printed editions of the 16th century, which not only lack any other articles beyond those provided by the described manuscripts, but even reduce the number of these articles, gravitating generally closer unto the ancient MSS. The printed editions of the Greek Jerusalem Typikon were produced in Venice: the first in 1545 [^1831], the second with slight additions (two new disciplinary articles) in 1577, and subsequent ones in 1603 and 1643 without alterations from 1577. The printed edition of the Greek Jerusalem Typikon is divided into 5 parts: general liturgical-disciplinary, the Menologion, *(Orig. p. 440)* the Triodion, Markian Chapters, and supplementary articles; but the editors divide the Typikon into two parts, assigning unto the first three a common numbering of 100 chapters, the Markian Chapters likewise 100, while the supplementary articles, unnumbered by chapters, they regard

=== LEAF p418 ===
simply as an appendix. The first general liturgical-disciplinary part, divided into 10 chapters, constitutes an almost verbatim reproduction of the identical part of the most ancient Greek manuscripts, for example, MS of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum, Sevastyanov collection fols. 1–19, in comparison with which and other known Greek MSS there is here only one additional article—Chapter 7: "Concerning which canons are to be preferred": "It should be known that if the Menaion contains for the memory of any saint canons by various hymnographers, then if there is a canon of Kyr Cosmas, it is preferred; if there are canons of Kyr John and others, that of John is preferred, for he takes precedence over others; if of Kyr Theophanes and others, that of Kyr Theophanes is preferred, for he takes precedence over others; if of Kyr Joseph, it is preferred unto the remaining hymnographers. If there are no such (canons of Joseph), then that of Kyr John; if these are not found, then that of Kyr Theophanes. But above all of them, the canons of Kyr Joseph are preferred unto all the rest." (Thus canons of later hymnographers are preferred unto the most ancient). The Markian Chapters likewise possess the identical composition as in the most ancient Greek MSS, for example MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 456, but unto them are appended—evidently to obtain the number of 100 chapters—as Chapters 94–100 the chapters concerning the Saturday service with Alleluia, concerning weekday canons, the rubric of censing, prokeimena, and the rubric of their intonation by the reader. Supplementary articles: "Questions from canonical ordinances"—concerning food on great feasts falling during fasts (on the basis of the canons of the Holy Apostles, Theophilus of Alexandria, and others); "Epistle of Nicholas, Patriarch of Constantinople, unto Anastasius, Hegumen of Sinai," in verse; concerning the same subject and concerning the comportment of monks in general: "Of Anastasius, Bishop of Caesarea of Palestine, concerning the Fast of Artzivourion"; "Of the Holy Fathers in Antioch concerning the Great Week"—concerning the identical matter; from the *Apostolic Constitutions* V, 13 concerning feasts and fasts; "Canons of Peter and Paul" (apocryphal) concerning non-working days; "Concerning churches," that when divine worship is impossible within them, it must be performed in houses; concerning orderly singing in church according unto Canon 75 of the Trullan Council; days of fasting relaxation and its degrees; two other articles concerning the same; lives of the four Evangelists (from the *Synopsis* of St. Dorotheus of Tyre); the order of reading the Evangelists during the year; how one ought to believe; "Exhortatory chapters in maxims" (moral counsels); and the Paschal Tables.

### Composition and Plan of Slavic Manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon

The Slavic copies of the Jerusalem Typikon differ significantly in plan and composition from the currently known Greek MSS, expanding their content with new articles, the number of which usually increases with the chronological age of the manuscripts. The most ancient of such MSS of Russian provenance, MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 of the 14th century, possesses the following new articles in

=== LEAF p419 ===
comparison with known Greek copies: the order of three kinds of Compline and Midnight Office, the order of the refectory table (the later "Office of the Panagia"); on the other hand, almost all the minor rubrics of the general liturgical-disciplinary portion of Greek MSS are omitted here, and of *(Orig. p. 441)* this part there is retained here only the order of the All-Night Vigil with an observation concerning how many Vigils occur in the year, and the order of weekday services—Vespers, Matins, and Hours with Inter-Hours; there are not even chapters concerning the Saturday service. Closer unto the Greek MSS is the Slavic MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 329/384 of the 14th century; it contains many of the supplementary articles of the Greek MSS, but retains no more than the first MS from the introductory liturgical-disciplinary part of Greek MSS. It possesses also new articles: not to mention a gathering sewn in front of it in another, probably later hand and of diverse content (prokeimena, Epistles and Gospels for various occasions, prayers, dismissals, the order for the kneading of bread), wherein are given disciplinary excerpts from Nikon of the Black Mountain comprising Chapters 32–47 of our present Typikon; in the primary MS, among the Markian Chapters placed here after the Menologion, there occur also such chapters: concerning a saint who has an entrance (Litiya) (from the Serbian Typikon), concerning the psalmody of the 1st Ode (**"Let us sing unto the Lord..."** [*Gospodevi poyem...*] = Chapter 18 of our present Typikon), concerning the singing of the **"More honorable..."** [*Chestneyshuyu...*] (= Chapter 31 of our present Typikon). Yet closer unto Greek MSS is the Slavic MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 332/385 of the end of the 14th century. Here is given in translation almost the entire introductory part of Greek MSS, and following it, prior unto the Menologion, a section of the Markian Chapters of general content (concerning feasts of the Theotokos on Sunday, etc.) with those chapters new in comparison with Greek MSS that are found in No. 329, and furthermore with the chapter "Concerning how candles are to be kindled" (= Chapter 25 of our present Typikon); then disciplinary excerpts from Nikon of the Black Mountain; following the Menologion and Triodion—common Epistles and Gospels for the saints for various occasions, Sunday readings, dismissals, the prayer for the kneading of bread, and the refrains at the 9th Ode. Close unto it is the Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 678/386 of the 15th century. ### Printed Editions of the Slavonic Jerusalem Typikon

The printed editions of the Slavonic Typikon in Russia necessarily took as their foundation the widespread Slavic manuscript copies; therefore, in the initial *(Orig. p. 442)* stages, as long as the deficiencies of the latter were not recognized, they were of the identical plan and composition. The first edition of the Slavonic Typikon in Russia was produced in 1610, with the blessing of Patriarch Hermogenes and with the counsel of the hierarchs of that time, by the *golovshchik* and *ustavshchik* of the Trinity-Sergius Lavra, Longin (Shishelev), upon the foundation of several manuscripts, though probably exclusively Russian-Slavic ones. It contains a general liturgical-disciplinary part—57 chapters; Markian Chapters—173; the rubric for services unto Slavonic-Russian saints, especially upon their coincidence with the Pentecostarion—25 chapters; temple chapters—46; the Menologion and the Triodion. The disciplinary part here is very extensive, but not everywhere systematic: many extracts from the Studite Typikon, and from the Holy Mountain Typikon according unto Nikon; one may say not a single disciplinary article from the Slavic copies was omitted; yet material entirely extraneous unto the Typikon (prayers from the Trebnik) is absent. On account of certain inaccuracies in the text, principally on account of discrepancies between the exclamations of prayers and their content (prayers addressed unto one Person of the Holy Trinity concluded with an exclamation addressed unto the entire Holy Trinity) and the addition in the prayer for the blessing of water unto the words "by the Holy Spirit" of "and by fire," this edition of the Typikon was condemned unto burning by Patriarch Philaret as heretical (several copies survived in the Moscow Typography Library and elsewhere), and a new edition was produced in 1633, somewhat more systematic and concise: certain articles of Russian origin were excluded, among others, from the Markian Chapters as well. This edition was likewise produced on the basis of Slavic manuscripts. It was reprinted in 1634 without alterations (only an error in pagination was corrected). (From this edition the Old Believer Typikon is printed presently). The subsequent edition under Patriarch Joasaph in 1641 restored much that had been excluded in 1633 and introduced certain corrections into the text. Close unto it is the edition of 1651 under Patriarch Joseph. Patriarch Nikon did not succeed in attending unto the correction of the Typikon. It was carried out under Patriarch Joachim, and the result thereof was the edition of 1682, wherein the Typikon received already its modern composition and volume.
