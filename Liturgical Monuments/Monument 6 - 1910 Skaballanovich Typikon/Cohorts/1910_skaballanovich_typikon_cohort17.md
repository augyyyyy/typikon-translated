(a division, it is thought, that had in view private or monastic use) [^773]. If Euthalius directed his labor upon the Apostle, then, one may think, the Gospel already possessed a division into liturgical lections—although attempts at such a division from the 4th and 5th centuries have not survived.

*(Orig. p. 166)*

##### Scope of the Pericopes

All these accounts concerning the division of the biblical text for liturgical use and the traces of such division demonstrate that in the present period the sacred books were read at divine worship without omissions. Unto the same effect speak also the surviving homilies of the 4th and 5th centuries, which for the most part expound the sacred book in its entirety, verse by verse. St. John Chrysostom says in one homily: "Whoso regularly attendeth church, even if he read not at home, but merely listeneth here unto that which is offered, for him one year sufficeth for the acquisition of rich knowledge; for we read not here today one scripture and tomorrow another, but always the same and continuously (*diapantos*)" [^774]. Since such readings—that is, divine services with the reading of Holy Scripture—occurred for the most part no less than twice a week (on Saturday and Sunday), sometimes thrice [^775] or daily [^776], the pericopes for continuous reading must have been only slightly larger than our present lections (*pericopes* / *zachala*), so that the entire New Testament without omissions could be read through in the course of a year [^777]. The Old Testament, of course, was not read in its entirety (as was the case also in the synagogue [^778]).

##### Festal Readings

If ordinary weekdays and even Sundays in the year did not yet possess fixed pericopes, feasts, Great Lent (*Tessarakoste* / the Forty-Day Fast), and Pentecost in certain localities at least already possessed them. During Great Lent, Genesis was read. Chrysostom says in one of the homilies on the statues, which were delivered during the fasting season: "today I shall examine the reading set before us," and he expoundeth Genesis 1:1 [^779]. The second series of homilies on Genesis (67 in number) was delivered during Great Lent of another year, and the third (9 homilies) in a third year [^780]. The homilies of St. Basil the Great on the Hexaemeron were likewise delivered during the Fast: in them there is an indication that they were spoken in springtime [^781]. The reason why Genesis was read at this time could consist in this: that the ecclesiastical year in certain localities began with the spring [^782]. St. Ambrose in his Paschal greeting writeth: "ye heard how the Book of Job was read, which was completed (*est decursus*) at the solemn office (*munere*) and in solemn season" [^783]. The homilies on the Book of Job attributed unto St. John Chrysostom begin: "The annual sufferer of mankind hath *(Orig. p. 167)* arrived today" [^784]. St. Ambrose mentioneth the reading of the Book of Jonah during Holy Week: "on the following day the Book of Jonah was read according unto custom: and that was the day wherein the Lord gave Himself up for us, when in the church repentance (the fast) is relaxed (*relaxatur*—Holy Thursday or Holy Saturday)" [^785].

=== LEAF p162 ===
The *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia, indicating for each day of Holy Week, beginning with Lazarus Saturday, a definite Gospel reading, and for certain days several such readings, speaketh also "of readings from the prophets" in this week [^786]. Yet in regard unto the reading of the Passion of the Lord in the ancient Church there was diversity. In Jerusalem, according unto Sylvia, on Great and Holy Friday all the Evangelists were read: first all the passages concerning the Passion, then concerning the judgment of Pilate, and finally, at the sixth hour (12 noon by our reckoning) on Golgotha there was read "from the Gospel where He suffered" [^787]. In Africa, on the contrary, according unto the testimony of Blessed Augustine, the Passion of the Lord was read only according unto Matthew; and when Augustine in one year took it into his head to read the Passion according unto John, the people were troubled (thinking that ancient tradition was being violated and preference given unto the custom of another church), and he was obliged to return unto the adopted order [^788].

Equally in both the Eastern and Western Churches the Acts of the Apostles were read during Pentecost. In St. John Chrysostom there is a special homily in explanation of this establishment [^789]. Blessed Augustine saith concerning the Acts that they are read "at the annual solemnity (*anniversaria solemnitate*) following the Passion of the Lord" [^790], and that their reading "beginneth from the Sunday of Pascha" [^791]. Concerning readings for other feasts we find no mentions in the writers of the 4th and 5th centuries, with the exception of indications in the *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia of readings on the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*), the Sunday of Palms, the Sunday of Antipascha, and Pentecost, corresponding unto the festal Gospel pericopes (at Pentecost—from Acts concerning the Descent of the Holy Spirit, and from the Gospel concerning the Ascension [^792]); passages concerning other fixed feasts besides the Meeting of the Lord have not survived in the manuscript. On multi-day feasts, such as Pascha was, there was a special reading for each day. Blessed Augustine saith in words delivered on Pascha: "Concerning the Resurrection of our Lord, in these days it is customary to read from all the books of the Holy Gospel" [^793]. "Concerning the Resurrection of the Lord it was read today also, but from another book of the Gospel, namely according unto Luke; for at first it was read according unto Matthew, yesterday according unto Mark, today according unto Luke; such is the order of the Evangelists. As His Passion is described by all the Evangelists, so these seven or eight days afford room for reading concerning the Resurrection of the Lord according unto all the Evangelists. The Passion, however, since it is read on a single day, is commonly read only according unto Matthew" [^794].

*(Orig. p. 168)*

##### Lectionaries

Scarcely in this period did there exist written designations of festal and fasting pericopes in biblical codices (at least in the codices of Holy Scripture surviving from the 4th and 5th centuries—the Sinaitic, Vatican, and Alexandrian—there are no such designations), and still less anything resembling the later "Evangelistaria," "Lectionaries," and "Festal Gospels": the order of readings was preserved by tradition. From this period we possess reports only concerning the first attempts at Lectionaries. According unto the testimony of Gennadius, a presbyter of Marseilles (*Massiliensis*), who lived at the end of the 5th century, Musaeus,

=== LEAF p163 ===
likewise a presbyter of Marseilles (c. 458), at the urging of St. Venerius (*Venerii*), extracted from Holy Scripture the readings for the festal days of the whole year, as well as responsorial verses of the psalms adapted unto the season and the readings (*responsoria etiam psalmorum capitula temporibus et lectionibus apta*) [^795]. A second report likewise cometh from Gaul: Sidonius Apollinaris, Bishop of Clermont (*Arvernorum*) in the 5th century (mentioned above), in an epitaph unto Claudianus, the brother of Claudianus Mamertus [^796], saith that the latter "prepared (*paravit*) for the annual solemnities (*solemnibus*) readings suitable unto each season" [^797].

##### Ceremonial Aspect of the Readings

More than for the distribution of readings across the ecclesiastical year was accomplished by the 4th and 5th centuries, apparently, for the elaboration of the ceremonial aspect of liturgical reading. At least, from these centuries we find not only the first accounts concerning the preparatory and concluding rites and acclamations for the liturgical readings, but the majority of such rites and acclamations have been preserved in unchanged and even unaugmented form unto this day.

##### The Persons Who Read

First of all, in regard unto the persons who read, a more precise regulation was introduced. Specifically, the reading of the Gospel was almost universally assigned unto the higher ranks of the clergy, beginning with the deacon and not excluding the bishop. Whereas in the 2nd century—in Justin Martyr—the task of the bishop is the homily following the reading [^798], which was performed by a lector—according unto the *Testamentum Domini Nostri Jesu Christi* [^799] and the *Apostolic Constitutions* [^800] the Gospel is read by a deacon and a presbyter. The *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia frequently assigneth the reading of the Gospel unto the bishop [^801]. *(Orig. p. 169)* In other places, principally, it seems, in the Western Church, the reading of the Gospel was regarded as the prerogative of the deacon (as is the case today in the Roman Church and in part among us): Blessed Jerome remindeth the deacon Sabinian that he "in the capacity (*quasi*) of a deacon continually read (*lectitabas*) the Gospel" [^802]. Socrates speaketh of the reading of the Gospel by a deacon (Sabbatius) at every liturgical assembly [^803]. According unto the words of Sozomen, in Alexandria the Gospel was read only by the archdeacon, in other places by deacons, in many places only by presbyters, and in some places on great feasts by bishops, for example, in Constantinople on the first day of Pascha [^804]. In rare localities there was preserved the former right of the lector to read all Holy Scripture, not excluding the Gospel, as may be inferred from the decree of the First Council of Toledo in 400: "penitents shall not be admitted unto the clergy, except only out of necessity and for the benefit of the work, and in such case let them be numbered among the lectors, yet so that (*ita ut*) they read neither Gospels nor Epistles" [^805].

=== LEAF p164 ===
##### The Acclamation "Peace Be Unto All"

The reading of Holy Scripture was preceded by a series of acclamations, and first of all by the acclamation:

**"Peace be unto all"** (*«Мир всем»* / *Pax vobiscum*).

St. John Chrysostom calleth this acclamation "the common preface in all ecclesiastical actions: when the primate entereth into the church, he immediately saith: **'Peace be unto all'**; when he ariseth for the sermon, he beginneth not to speak unto the people before he imparteth peace unto all; when the priests perform the blessing (at Matins and Vespers before the prayer), they preface it with this; when he biddeth greeting to be given—**'Peace be unto all'**; when the Sacrifice is accomplished—**'Peace be unto all'**" [^806]. In the West likewise, judging by Blessed Augustine, this greeting was pronounced by the bishop before the readings. Describing a service whereat a miracle of healing took place, Blessed Augustine saith: "joy was complete, joyful voices sounded forth: 'Thanks be to God, praise be to God' (*Deo gratias, Deo laudes*); no one kept silence; on every side they cried aloud; I greeted the people, and again they exclaimed (*clamabant*) with the same ardent voice; and when at last silence was restored, there took place the solemn reading of the divine scriptures" [^807]. That this "greeting" of the bishop unto the people upon entering the church before the reading consisted in the imparting of peace is evident, besides the cited passage of Chrysostom, also from several other passages of his homilies, which indicate likewise the response of the people unto the greeting: "Upon entering the church, we immediately *(Orig. p. 170)* according unto this law (in conformity with the Savior's commandment concerning reconciliation with one's neighbors before prayer) say: **'Peace be unto you'**, and you reply: **'And with thy spirit'**" [^808]. "What is more perverse and senseless," Blessed Augustine reprehendeth the Donatists, "than for lectors and those who read the Epistles to say 'peace be unto thee' (*pax tecum*), and yet to separate themselves from the peace of those churches unto which these Epistles were written?" [^809] From these passages it is evident at the same time that the practice of the Eastern and Western Churches on this point was identical: in both places the reading was preceded by the imparting of peace; the formula, apparently, was not yet firmly fixed and everywhere uniform. Peace was imparted by the primate of the assembly, that is, for the most part by the bishop. But from the fact that the Council of Carthage of 419 forbiddeth lectors to "greet (*salutent*) the people" [^810], one may conclude that originally this peaceful greeting was pronounced by the lector himself [^811], and only when there was attached unto it a sacramental-intercessory significance was the right unto it transferred unto the bishop or presbyter.

##### Let Us Attend

St. John Chrysostom mentioneth the acclamation **"Let us attend"** (*«Вонмем»* / *Proschomen*) before the reading: "There stands the deacon, the common minister, crying aloud and saying: 'Let us attend'; and this frequently... after him the lector begins: 'The prophecy of Isaiah'; then

=== LEAF p165 ===
he pronounceth aloud (*eis epekoon*): **'Thus saith the Lord'** [^812], while the standing deacon stoppeth the mouths of all by the imposition of silence (evidently by the acclamation: 'Let us attend'), he speaketh this, rendering honor not unto the lector, but unto Him Who speaketh with all through him" [^813]. Concerning this imposition of silence mention is made also by St. Ambrose [^814] and Blessed Augustine [^815]. Of acclamations following the readings, even after the reading of the Gospels, monuments of the 4th and 5th centuries make no mention [^816].

*(Orig. p. 171)*

The fact of the emergence of all these acclamations beareth witness unto the reverence which the reading of Holy Scripture inspired at divine worship [^817].

##### Rites of the Reading

Unto the same reverence testifieth the setting wherein the reading took place. Here first of all one must note the almost universal custom of standing during the reading of Holy Scripture (in contrast unto the sitting of the synagogue), noted for the first time by monuments of the 4th and 5th centuries [^818], yet still contending with the custom of sitting during these readings. The contest was resolved by standing being assigned unto the Gospel reading, and sitting unto the rest. Already the *Apostolic Constitutions* prescribe: "When the Gospel is read, let all presbyters, deacons, and the entire people stand in complete silence" [^819]. But St. John Cassian saith that Egyptian monks during the readings customarily sat upon low benches, and he noteth no difference in this regard for New Testament readings [^820]. Sozomen noteth as something strange (*xenon*) in the Alexandrian Church—something he neither knoweth nor hath heard of in others—that the bishop does not rise during the reading of the Gospel [^821]. Philostorgius (4th–5th cc.) relateth concerning Theophilus, an Arian bishop, that upon arriving in India he "corrected many things that were performed there contrary unto the order (*ouk euagos*), for there they listened unto the Gospel reading sitting, and permitted certain other things not allowed by the Divine law (*hon me theios thesmos epestatei*)" [^822]. In the *Life of St. Anastasius I*, Pope of Rome (398–402), it is related that he established that "each time the Gospel is read, the priests should not sit, but stand bowed (*curvi*)" [^823].

Unto the reproach of the presbyter Vigilantius, who contended against the veneration of martyrs, that the Church permitteth the kindling of lamps in daylight at the tombs of martyrs, Blessed Jerome remarketh: "even apart from the relics of martyrs, *(Orig. p. 172)* throughout all the churches of the East, when the Gospel is about to be read, lights (*luminaria*) are kindled, even when the sun is shining—not, indeed, to banish darkness, but as a token of joy, so that under the figure of material light there may be represented that Light whereof we read in the Psalter: 'A lamp unto my feet is Thy law, and a light unto my paths'" [^824]. Consequently, this custom was not yet universal, but was hardly new: it had been preserved from the time when the Gospel was always read in the evening or at night—at the Liturgy, which in antiquity was celebrated in the evening with the agape, and later (from the 2nd century) in the deep morning, its preparatory portion merging with the dawn

=== LEAF p166 ===
Matins [^825].

##### Reading of Non-Canonical Books

Alongside the canonical books of Holy Scripture, there were still read at divine worship in this period certain non-canonical sacred books. The distinction between the two consisted in this: that the canonical books were regarded as divinely inspired and therefore served as the rule of faith and piety, whereas the non-canonical books were regarded merely as pious compositions and were appointed for reading unto converts and catechumens [^826]. Blessed Jerome saith concerning the books of Judith, Tobit, Maccabees, the Wisdom of Solomon, and Sirach: "as the Church readeth the books of Judith, Tobit, and Maccabees, yet receiveth them not among the canonical scriptures [^827], so also these two volumes [^828] (Wisdom and Sirach) she readeth for the edification of the people (*ad aedificationem plebis*), yet not for establishing the authority of ecclesiastical dogmas" [^829]. Other writers of this period express themselves in similar fashion concerning these books, placing alongside them certain compositions of the Apostolic Fathers and ancient Christian writings as non-canonical books of the New Testament. St. Athanasius, having enumerated the canonical books of the Old and New Testaments, remarketh: "but for greater exactness, writing this also out of necessity, I subjoin that besides these there are others also, not included in the canon, which nevertheless have been appointed by the Fathers to be read unto those newly arriving (*anaginoskesthai tois arti proserchomenois*) and desiring to be instructed in the word of piety: such are the Wisdom of Solomon, the Wisdom of Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the so-called Teaching (*Didache*) of the Apostles, and the Shepherd" [^830]. According unto *(Orig. p. 173)* Rufinus (presbyter of Aquileia, † 410), "there are also other books which by our ancestors were called not canonical, but ecclesiastical (*ecclesiastici*), such as the Wisdom of Solomon and the other Wisdom called of the Son of Sirach; of the same rank are the Book of Tobit and Judith and the Books of Maccabees; and in the New Testament the book called the Shepherd, or Hermas, and that called The Two Ways, or the Judgment of Peter (*Duae Viae sive Judicium Petri* = the 'Teaching of the Twelve Apostles', which beginneth with the words: 'Two ways...'); all these books they willed indeed to be read in the churches (*quae omnia legi quidem in ecclesiis voluerunt*), yet not to be brought forward for confirmation of faith therefrom" [^831]. Concerning the reading of the *Shepherd* in the churches Eusebius likewise makes mention: "it, as we know, hath been publicly read in the churches (*en ekklesiais ismen auto dedemosieumenon*)." In addition, he speaketh of the liturgical reading also of the Epistle of St. Clement, Bishop of Rome:

=== LEAF p167 ===
"it is known that it was publicly read (*epi tou koinou dedemosieumenen*) both in antiquity and now among ourselves" [^832]. When the canon was definitively established, the reading of these books at divine worship had either to cease or to take on a character distinct from the reading of canonical books. In certain churches, apparently, the former occurred. Thus St. Cyril of Jerusalem admonished: "one must have nothing in common with apocrypha (so he calleth all non-canonical books); that alone must one study which we read openly (*meta parresias*) in church... but that which is not read in church, read not even in private" [^833]. Nevertheless Sozomen speaketh (disapprovingly) of the reading in certain Palestinian churches of the apocryphal Apocalypse of Peter (on Great and Holy Friday) [^834]. In the majority of churches, however, not only did the reading of non-canonical books continue, as the above-cited testimonies demonstrate, even after they had been decisively recognized as non-canonical, but after their example there began to be read also the writings of later teachers of the Church, with only a sharp distinction drawn between the readings of divinely inspired scriptures and these latter. Restricting liturgical reading unto canonical scriptures, the Council of Carthage forbiddeth the reading of other books not unconditionally, but "under the name of divine scriptures" [^835]. Blessed Jerome said concerning St. Ephrem the Syrian: "Ephrem, deacon of the Church of Edessa, wrote much in the Syriac tongue and attained such renown that in certain churches his compositions are read publicly after the reading of the scriptures" [^836]. "If a presbyter," decreeth the Council of Vaison (in Gaul) of 529, "on account of any infirmity cannot preach himself, let the homilies of the holy fathers be read by the deacons" [^837]. The biographer placeth into the mouth of Caesarius, Bishop of Arles (6th c.), these words: "if the words of the Lord, of the prophets, and of the Apostles are read by presbyters and deacons, *(Orig. p. 174)* why should not presbyters and deacons read the words of Ambrose, of Augustine, or of my humble self, or of every one of the saints?" [^838]

The custom of reading the acts of the martyrs at divine worship, having begun perhaps already in the 2nd and 3rd centuries [^839], now attained widespread dissemination, although in certain localities it provoked legislative restrictions and prohibitions on account of the corruption of these acts. Eusebius of Caesarea referreth unto "memoirs concerning the martyrs" and unto the "book concerning the martyrs" or "collection of martyrdoms" (*he ton martyron synagoge*) composed by himself [^840]. Blessed Augustine repeatedly mentioneth readings of this kind at divine worship: "in the passion which was read unto us today" [^841]. The Council of Carthage of 397 decreeth: "it shall be lawful also hereafter (*liceat etiam legi*) for the passions of the martyrs to be read, when their annual commemorations (*dies*) are celebrated" [^842]. Only in the Roman Church by the decree of Pope Gelasius at the Council of seventy bishops in 494 was the reading of the acts of the martyrs forbidden, which "out of singular caution, according unto ancient custom, are not read in the holy Roman Church, because the names of those who

=== LEAF p168 ===
wrote them are for the most part unknown, and in them, it is thought, much that is superfluous and incongruous with the matter hath been written by unfaithful simpletons (*idiotis*), such as, for instance, the passions of a certain Quiricus and Julitta, as also of Gregory and others, composed manifestly by heretics" [^843]. Nevertheless this same decree mentioneth the reading in many Roman churches, though not in the Lateran, of the acts of the holy Pope Sylvester.

It was also in custom to read in church and, of course, at divine worship the decrees of Councils. Blessed Augustine saith that in many African churches it was customary annually during the days of the fast, that is, in Great Lent prior unto Pascha, when the strictly fasting people have greater leisure, to read through the acts of the Council of Carthage (*eadem gesta collationis*) in their entirety from beginning to end in order [^844].

Since in the order of the Liturgy given by the *Apostolic Constitutions* [^845], as in all the so-called apostolic liturgies, which in their fundamentals ascend unto the 4th century and earlier [^846], reading is indicated only from the canonical books (the Law, the Prophets, the Gospels, Acts, and the Epistles), all other readings must have taken place at other services, as is the case today. If so, then *(Orig. p. 175)* already in the 4th century did the so-called rubrical readings (*ordo*-mandated lections / *ustavnye chteniya*) at Vespers and Matins take their origin.

##### Preaching in the 4th and 5th Centuries

Preaching at divine worship in the 4th and 5th centuries occupied no less significant a place than in former centuries, owing unto the liveliness of theological controversies and the elevation of the general level of education among the clergy (many church figures of the 4th and 5th centuries had received an education in the finest secular schools of that epoch). Scarcely any period brought forth a longer series of illustrious preachers or furnished more material for our present rubrical readings. In the East there attained renown: Eusebius of Caesarea, Saints Athanasius the Great, Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory of Nyssa, Macarius the Great, Ephrem the Syrian, John Chrysostom, Proclus Patriarch of Constantinople, Cyril of Alexandria, Epiphanius of Cyprus, and Blessed Theodoret; in the West: St. Ambrose, Blessed Augustine, Peter Chrysologus, Leo the Great, and Caesarius Bishop of Arles. The majority of the homilies preserved from this period represent expositions of Holy Scripture; a significant number are devoted unto dogmatic controversies; fewer are of a purely edifying character, and a comparatively small circle is constituted by festal panegyrics. Of sermons of the first kind there entered into our present divine worship in the widest measure the homilies of St. John Chrysostom, and in part of St. Basil the Great; the second kind of sermons of this period, having had a temporary character, entered not at all into our divine worship. Of the third kind there entered the discourses of St. Ephrem; of the fourth, the majority of the few works of this

=== LEAF p169 ===
kind.

Preachers of this period not infrequently note the zeal wherewith their instructions were attended, and at times were compelled to contend even with an exaggerated appraisal of the place and importance of preaching within the composition of divine worship. "Preaching," saith St. John Chrysostom, "is everything unto you. You say: what is there to do in church if there is no sermon? That is an entirely perverse view. What need is there of a preacher? It was properly on your account that he became necessary. Why a sermon? All is clear from Holy Scripture" [^847]. "Often have I sought at that sacred hour (of the Eucharist) for so numerous an assembly as is now gathered here, and found it nowhere; and deeply did I sigh that you listen unto your fellow-servant speaking unto you with such zeal, yet when Christ appeareth upon the mystical table, then the church is empty" [^848]. However, in that same hierarch there occur lamentations also concerning the inattention of the listeners, and concerning their departure from the church prior unto the conclusion of the instruction: "many of those standing and hearing me yawn, scratch their head, and grow weary... while others depart before I finish speaking" [^849]. Caesarius of Arles (early 6th c.), after fruitless exhortations not to leave the sermon, commanded for the duration *(Orig. p. 176)* thereof that the church doors be locked [^850]. In general, in the East preaching at divine worship occupied far more room than in the West, as is evident both from these latter testimonies, and from the catalogue of preachers here and there, and finally, from the manifestly exaggerated yet characteristic testimony of Sozomen, that in Rome "neither the bishop nor anyone else teacheth in church" [^851].

##### Time of Preaching

The principal moment for preaching remained as before the Liturgy, and specifically its preparatory portion, the Liturgy of the Catechumens, as is demonstrated by a whole series of homilies of St. John Chrysostom and others, who frequently remind their hearers of the liturgical acclamations and prayers they have just heard. But sometimes and in certain localities preaching took place also at other services. Thus the homilies on the Hexaemeron of St. Basil were delivered two in a day—at the morning office and at the evening office ("when you were present at the brief morning [*heothen*] discourse, we stumbled upon such concealed depth of thought that we despaired of that which followed, etc.") [^852]. According unto Socrates, "in Caesarea of Cappadocia and in Cyprus on Saturday and Sunday always in the evening at the lighting of the lamps (*meta tes lychnapsias*) the presbyters and bishops expound the Scriptures" [^853]. The homilies of St. John Chrysostom on Genesis were delivered in the evening ("we expound unto you the Scriptures, yet you, turning your eyes away from us, have directed your attention unto the lamps, unto him who was kindling the lamps") [^854]. "Turn your attention unto the continuation of the psalm whereof we spoke at Matins," Blessed Augustine inviteth his hearers [^855]. Since preaching was considered an indispensable constituent of the Liturgy [^856], it must, one must think, have taken place obligatorily on those days when the Liturgy was celebrated, and

=== LEAF p170 ===
as frequently as the latter was celebrated in any locality. But during Great Lent (*Tessarakoste*) and Pentecost, especially during the former—in view of the catechumens—they preached for the most part daily. Thus in the homilies of St. John Chrysostom on Genesis and "On the Statues," as well as in the discourses of Blessed Augustine on the psalms, references unto "yesterday's" discourse constantly occur. In monastic communities, moreover, at least in certain ones, as we shall see, the abba daily instructed the brethren throughout the entire year.

##### Persons Who Preached

As regards the persons who preached, following the example of the Apostles and the practice of the 2nd and 3rd centuries [^857], the true minister of the word was considered to be the bishop. "The proper office (*proprium munus*) of a bishop is to teach the people" [^858]. According unto St. John *(Orig. p. 177)* Chrysostom, the ability to teach in a bishop "ought to be before all else" [^859]. Preaching talent in that era most of all advanced persons unto episcopal sees, as the examples of Eusebius of Caesarea, Saints Gregory the Theologian, John Chrysostom, and Blessed Augustine demonstrate. But in ecclesiastical teaching presbyters also took part far more than in former times. Already in the 3rd century so illustrious a preacher as Origen was merely a presbyter. The *Apostolic Constitutions* prescribe: after the readings, and in particular after the Gospel, "let the presbyters severally, and not all at once, exhort the people, and after them all the bishop, who is like unto a helmsman" [^860]. In conformity with this, in the Church of Jerusalem according unto the description of the *Pilgrimage* attributed unto Sylvia, on the Lord's Day "of all the sitting presbyters whosoe'er desireth preacheth, and after them all the bishop preacheth" [^861]. St. John Chrysostom preached in Antioch precisely in the rank of presbyter in the presence of his bishop Flavian, who sometimes added his own exhortation unto his sermon [^862]. Unto Blessed Augustine, when he was a presbyter, his aged bishop Valerius, who possessed poor command of the Latin tongue of his flock, committed preaching in church; and when the bishop was reproached for this, he appealed unto Eastern custom of this kind [^863]. Consequently, in the West prior unto that time preaching had been the prerogative of the bishop alone. But already Blessed Jerome saith concerning this: "it is a very bad custom that in certain churches the presbyters keep silence and speak not in the presence of the bishops, as though the latter envied them or disdained to listen unto them" [^864]. In cases of necessity or extraordinary circumstances, deacons also were admitted unto preaching. Already in the 3rd century, according unto the testimony of St. Cyprian, during persecutions which at times deprived the Church of presbyters, the right of preaching was granted also unto deacons [^865]. One of the most renowned preachers of the 4th century, St. Ephrem, possessed only the rank of deacon. St. Gregory the Great, Pope of Rome († 604), during illness commissioned deacons to read his homilies [^866]. Above we saw that deacons were commissioned with the reading at divine worship of the homilies of the holy fathers [^867]. Even laymen were admitted unto
