Far more significant is the difference of the Jerusalem typikon from the Syrian, or more precisely—a step forward of the former in comparison with the latter—namely, the adaptation of divine worship unto the feasts of the church year, concerning which both the *Testamentum* and the *Apostolic Constitutions* are silent (not counting Pascha). It is characteristic also that the *Apostolic Constitutions* begin the depiction of the liturgical order with Vespers, whereas the pilgrim begins with Matins. #### The Daily Office According to Other Writers of the 4th and 5th Centuries

The liturgical practice depicted by the two examined monuments—the *Apostolic Constitutions* and the *Pilgrimage* attributed unto Sylvia of Aquitaine—can hardly be restricted unto the confines of those two churches wherein these monuments originated. Even in the 3rd century we observed an astonishing similarity between the most distant churches in their liturgical orders. In the 4th and 5th centuries this similarity was bound to increase. Only now was opened the full possibility of close intercourse between the churches; this was the most flourishing period of Councils and of beginning centralization in ecclesiastical governance. That the first of the examined monuments traces its practice back unto the Apostles in itself speaks for its relative universality; the canonical portion of the *Apostolic Constitutions* reflected the norms of the general, universal law of that era; the same must be expected also from the liturgical sections of the monument. As regards the liturgical practice of the 4th-century Church of Jerusalem depicted in the *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia, to ascribe unto this practice a purely local significance is precluded by the exceptional position which this church has always occupied in the Christian world. Indeed, if we compare with the rich liturgical material provided by these two monuments the meager data concerning 4th- and 5th-century divine worship that can be met with in the writers of these centuries, we shall become convinced of the substantial agreement of the various churches in this aspect of their life; just as the picture of divine worship provided by the monuments will thereby receive some supplementation. From the writers of the 4th century we shall gather first the data concerning the days and hours of divine worship, then concerning its general structure and order, and finally concerning its constituent parts and elements: prayer, psalmody, readings, and instruction.

#### Number and Types of Services

=== LEAF p142 ===
In other churches as well there took place that daily celebration of divine worship which the *Apostolic Constitutions* require, and which the 4th-century *Pilgrimage* attests in the Church of Jerusalem. At least the morning and evening services were celebrated daily, if not in all churches, then in the more significant—episcopal—ones, and were attended punctually. St. John Chrysostom speaks concerning Psalm 140: "The Fathers established that this psalm be read every evening; consequently, one may say that everyone knows this psalm and they sing it at every age... every day they sing it, yet understand not its meaning" [^631]. In his exposition of 1 Timothy 2 St. Chrysostom remarks: "What means 'first of all' (that supplications be made...)? It means at the daily divine worship. And this the initiates of the mysteries know, how it takes place among us every day both evening and morning, how we offer prayers for the whole world, for kings, and for all that are in authority" [^632]. The Council of Agde (*Concilium Agathense*, in southern Gaul) of 506, canon 20, decrees: *(Orig. p. 146)* "The morning and evening hymns shall be sung on all days, and at the conclusion of the morning and evening Masses let the *capitella* (short readings) from the psalms be read, and let the people after the collect (*collecta*) prayer be dismissed by the bishop with a blessing" [^633].

Besides morning and evening, there were everywhere observed also the prayer hours established by the preceding centuries: the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours, the beginning, middle, and end (before dawn) of the night. These were likewise honored, if not by public divine worship, then by elaborate, definite prayer (a prayer rule), at least among ascetics and in monasteries. St. Basil the Great, unto the question: "What times are suitable for prayer?", replies: "...we must not neglect the times of prayer established in the brotherhoods, which (times) were chosen by us of necessity, since each of them in a particular manner calls to mind the blessings bestowed by God. Thus we must not neglect the morning, so that the first movements of the soul and mind may be consecrated unto God... so that we may not set the body in motion for labor until we fulfill that which was spoken: **'Unto Thee will I pray, O Lord; in the morning Thou shalt hear my voice'** [^634]... Then also at the Third Hour we must stand for prayer and gather the brethren, even if it should happen that each be occupied separately with his own work; calling to mind the gift of the Holy Spirit given at the Third Hour unto the Apostles, all must worship with one accord, so that they themselves may become worthy to receive sanctification and ask of the Spirit guidance and instruction unto profit, imitating him who said: **'Create in me a clean heart, O God, and renew a right spirit within me...'** [^635] At the Sixth Hour we deemed it necessary to pray in imitation of the saints who said: **'Evening, and morning, and at noon will I speak...'** [^636] Concerning the fact that the Ninth Hour is necessary

=== LEAF p143 ===
unto us for prayer, this is handed down unto us by the Apostles themselves in the Book of Acts [^637]. And at the close of the day there are needed both thanksgiving for that which has been given us on this day... and confession of that which we failed to fulfill..., for which reason it is said: **'What ye say in your hearts, be penitent upon your beds'** [^638]. Again, at the onset of night we must entreat that our rest may be without stumbling and free from fantasies, reading at this hour the 90th Psalm. And that midnight also is needed unto us for prayer, this was handed down by Paul and Silas, as the narrative of the Acts relates [^639]; and the Psalmist says: **'At midnight I arose to give thanks unto Thee for the judgments of Thy righteousness'** [^640]. And again we must arise for prayer, anticipating the morning, that the day may not overtake us in slumber and upon our bed, according unto that which is said: **'Mine eyes have prevented the morning, that I might meditate on Thy words'** [^641]."

The significance which St. Basil possessed for the monasticism of his own and subsequent times renders *(Orig. p. 147)* it probable that the instructions imparted by him here concerning the honoring of each of the prayer hours were taken into account during the formation of the office for them: all the psalms from which he draws excerpts were incorporated into these services, just as in the prayer of Compline his expression is literally repeated. (Hence, perhaps, the ascription of his name unto the majority of prayers at the Hours and other services).

#### The Origin of the First Hour

According unto the testimony of St. John Cassian, for the first time in his day, and specifically in the monastery of Bethlehem, there was established a special "morning canonical office" (*matutinam canonicam functionem*) "at sunrise"—that is, the First Hour; however, the venerable father remarks, this service was not newly instituted, but was derived through the separation from the nocturnal vigils (*nocturnarum vigiliarum*), celebrated after cockcrow until dawn (Matins), of the final part following Psalm 150—namely, Psalms 50, 62, and 89 (which were assigned unto the First Hour). This service was established, according unto the words of John Cassian, in order to restrain the monks from prolonged sleep during the interval of time between the conclusion of the vigil (Matins) and the Third Hour [^642]. Thus, apparently, one must understand Cassian's somewhat obscure expression concerning this:

"The songs (*hymnos*, that is, psalms, as is evident from what follows) which in this country they adopted (*excepere*) for the morning office (the newly established one, that is, the First Hour), and

=== LEAF p144 ===
which are hitherto likewise sung at the end of nocturnal vigils, which customarily conclude after cockcrow before dawn—that is, Psalm 148 and those following it; whereas Psalms 50, 62, and 89 were assigned (*deputatos*—allotted) for this new service, as we know. In Italy even now in all churches after the conclusion of the morning hymns Psalm 50 is sung, which undoubtedly was borrowed from no other source than this" [^643].

The incentive for establishing the new service according unto Cassian was the abuse of sleep on the part of certain monks after the night office: upon its conclusion they slept not merely until dawn, but until the next service, which was the Third Hour, making up with daytime slumber the hours spent in nocturnal prayer [^644].

#### Weekday Matins According to Basil the Great

As regards the order of daily services in the various churches of the 4th and 5th centuries, brief indications concerning it are provided also in the testimonies cited above, and these indications for the most part coincide with the detailed, although likewise incomplete, data concerning the order of Syrian-Jerusalem divine worship in the *Apostolic Constitutions* and the *Pilgrimage*. A detailed description of the morning service is provided by St. Basil the Great in his letter unto the clergy of Neocaesarea:

"The customs which have now become established in all the churches of God are uniform and harmonious. For among us the people from the night rise early (*ἐκ νυκτὸς γὰρ ὀρθρίζει*) in the house of prayer, in labor, sorrow, and tearful contrition confessing unto God; *(Orig. p. 148)* after which (*τελευταῖον*), having arisen (from their knees) from prayer, they stand for psalmody. And behold, dividing themselves into two parts, they sing alternately unto one another (*ἀντιψάλλουσιν ἀλλήλοις*)... Then in their turn, having entrusted one to begin the singing of the melody (*τοῦ μέλους*), the rest respond (*ὑπηχοῦσι*), and having thus passed the night in the variety of psalmody interspersed with prayers (*μεταξὺ προσευχόμενοι*), at the very break of day, all together (*κοινῇ*), as it were with one mouth and one heart, offer unto the Lord the psalm of confession, making repentance each with his own words" [^645].

At this Matins, beginning, like the Jerusalem office according unto

=== LEAF p145 ===
the *Pilgrimage*, likewise before dawn and uniting consequently the Midnight Office and Matins, the psalms are sung, as at that one, now antiphonally, now responsorially, and alternate with prayers. In it our attention is arrested by the penitential tone, in connection wherewith stands some kind of (public or private?) confession of sins at the beginning of the service (corresponding unto our Six Psalms, which according unto the *Typikon* one must listen to "praying for our sins" [^646]) and a solitary penitential prayer at the end of the service (corresponding unto the present 12 prostrations with silent prayer). This is the first testimony concerning the use at Matins of Psalm 50 (cf. above in Cassian), which according unto the most ancient complete typika (for example, the Hagia Sophia Typikon of Constantinople of the 9th century) constitutes the most important central portion of Matins.

#### The Saturday Vigil According to Cassian

From among the days of the week in liturgical terms, besides Sunday, Saturday was significantly singled out, at least in certain localities. The morning service, which on other days also, as beginning in the depth of night, could be termed a vigil in the proper sense, began on the eve of Saturday from the evening (*ab vespera illuscente sabbato*) and continued "the whole night"; only shortly before Cassian was it shortened by the "elders" (*seniores*) for the winter season to such an extent that it concluded at the fourth crowing of the cock, so that during the remaining two hours before dawn it might be possible to sleep [^647]. The institution of this vigil John Cassian traces back unto the time of "apostolic preaching" and explains by the fact that when "our Lord and Saviour was crucified on the sixth day of the week, the disciples were so struck by His suffering that had just taken place that they spent that whole night in watchful vigil." "For the sake of the labor of the vigil" on the eve of Saturday, Cassian adds, "throughout all the Eastern churches there is not unjustly maintained on Saturday the relaxation of the fast" [^648]. It is remarkable withal that concerning a vigil on the eve of Sunday Cassian speaks not, although he dedicates a special chapter unto the question "wherein Sunday worship differs from the service of other days." Consequently, Sun*(Orig. p. 149)*day Matins (in the localities observed by Cassian) was so insignificantly longer than the weekday office, beginning only slightly earlier than it, as was the case in the Church of Jerusalem according unto the *Pilgrimage*. The Roman Catholic Church has preserved an echo of this practice, when it attaches the vigil not unto the feast itself, but unto the eve thereof. The Saturday vigil according unto Cassian has the following structure:

"first standing they chant (*concinuerunt*) three antiphons; then, sitting upon the ground or upon low stools, they execute responsorially (*respondent*) after a single

=== LEAF p146 ===
singer (*uno modulante*) three psalms, unto which, however, the brethren in turn respond (*qui tamen singuli a singulis fratribus vicissim succedentibus sibi praebentur*) [^649]; and sitting in the same quiet posture they join unto all this three readings" [^650].

In this order our attention is drawn by the absence of hymns and prayers; if the former are not understood under the antiphons, their absence is explained by the fact that here monastic divine worship is kept in view, which at that time generally (as we shall see subsequently) excluded hymnographic material from its composition, regarding it as the property of secular churches. As for prayers, priestly prayers were absent from it evidently because the service was celebrated without a priest, while ordinary prayers were absent because they were exclusively of a penitential character, whereas Saturday was celebrated almost in the same manner as Sunday.

#### Vigils

Concerning vigils St. John Chrysostom speaks frequently, naming them at times even "all-night vigils (*pannychides*, παννυχίδες)" (perhaps rhetorically); yet for the most part he has in view vigils in honor of martyrs or leaves it unknown which he has in view; nor does he report much concerning the composition of the vigils. Thus, for example: "Ye have turned night into day by your sacred all-night vigils" [^651]. Or: "Enter into the church, behold the poor remaining here from midnight until day; behold the sacred vigils uniting day with night; behold the Christ-loving people, fearing neither by day nor by night the tyranny of sleep nor the constraint of poverty." "All-night and continuous standings" Chrysostom contrasts with daytime standings, which are interrupted and imperfect: "In them, imitating the angelic choral standing, we offer unceasing praise unto the Creator. O marvelous gifts of Christ! On high the angelic hosts sing praise; upon earth men by their choir standings in the churches imitate this doxology; above, the Seraphim cry aloud the Thrice-Holy hymn; below, the multitude of men sends it up; a festival common unto earthly and heavenly beings is instituted; one thanksgiving, one rejoicing, one joyful choral dance" [^652]. From this one may deduce that the angelic hymn: **"Glory to God in the highest"** (*«Слава в вышних Богу»*) and the Seraphic hymn: **"Holy, holy..."** (*«Свят, свят...»*) entered into the composition of the vigil.

*(Orig. p. 150)*

#### Sunday Worship According to Cassian

It is possible, however, that in certain localities vigils were celebrated also on the eve of Sundays. In favor of this may speak both the fact that in St. John Chrysostom they appear as an already very customary and frequent phenomenon, and the fact that at the beginning of the 6th century we find them in the practice of not only the Monastery of St. Sabbas. Nevertheless John Cassian, as a peculiarity of Sunday divine worship in the East (apparently, predominantly in Palestinian and Mesopotamian monasteries), states that "on Sunday before the midday meal there takes place only one gathering, wherein, for the honor of the communion of the Lord, with great solemnity and attentiveness executing the psalms and prayers, they combine the Third and Sixth Hours; thus in the sequences of prayer nothing is diminished; and yet in honor of the Resurrection of the Lord there is granted unto the brethren some relaxation and indulgence in comparison with other days" [^653]. Conforming unto this, in the 4th-century Church of Jerusalem (according unto the *Pilgrimage*), wherein there was no office of the Ninth Hour, after the Liturgy there was celebrated a service in place of the Sixth and Ninth Hours [^654].

#### Days of the Liturgy and Communion

The Divine Liturgy in the majority of churches was celebrated, besides Sunday, only on Saturday. So it was in Alexandria, according unto the testimony of its Archbishops St. Athanasius and Timothy (380–385) [^655]; in the neighboring churches and monasteries of Egypt and the Thebaid, according unto the testimony of Socrates [^656] and John Cassian [^657]; in Asia Minor, according unto the testimony of the Council of Laodicea [^658]; in Constantinople, according unto the testimony of Socrates [^659]. According unto Basil the Great: "We communicate four times each week—on Sunday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday, and also on other days of the week if the commemoration of a martyr happens; but it is good and profitable every day to partake of and receive the Holy Body and Blood of Christ" [^660].

From this last passage it is evident that in the 4th century all communicated at every Liturgy. But certain passages from the homilies of Chrysostom demonstrate that many, under the pretext of their unworthiness, not only ceased to communicate at every Liturgy, but restricted themselves unto receiving communion once or twice a *(Orig. p. 151)* year [^661]. Unto such the holy father says, not without irony, that if they be unworthy, they ought not to approach the Holy Mysteries even once a year [^662]. St. Macarius of Egypt says unto a woman who had fallen under the influence of sorcery: "Never neglect to visit the church; never withdraw thyself from communion of the Mysteries of Christ; misfortune befell thee because thou hast not approached the most pure Mysteries of our Saviour for five weeks already" [^663].

More firmly maintained was the custom of daily communion in the West. According unto St. Jerome, in his time this custom existed in Rome and in Spain [^664]. But judging by the fact that various persons addressed letters unto St. Jerome and Augustine concerning the expediency of this custom [^665], it was already close in the West, as in the East, unto waning. Pseudo-Ambrose says: "If the Bread is daily (*quotidianus*), why dost thou receive It after a year, as the Greeks are accustomed to do in the East? Receive daily that which is daily necessary unto thee" [^666].

The cessation of general communion at every Liturgy had this influence upon the elaboration of the liturgical typikon, that it elevated Vespers and Matins unto a height close unto that of the Liturgy, creating the view of them as services equally necessary and obligatory as the latter [^667].

#### Litanies

Speaking of church services of the 4th and 5th centuries, one cannot fail to mention the so-called litanies (*litaneiai*, λιτανεῖαι). These were solemn processions, cross-bearing processions from one temple unto another, or unto the city square, or outside the city, taking place at the translation of relics or other

=== LEAF p149 ===
remains of a martyr, on commemorations of martyrs unto the places of their sufferings or unto their churches from the principal church, as well as on occasions of public calamities. Their origin was laid down already in the 3rd century; but in that era, so arduous for Christians, these processions were restricted unto instances of the translation of martyrs' remains and were conducted for the most part at night [^668]. Only in the 4th century did they receive wide application *(Orig. p. 152)* and become public and more solemn. "A few days before this," says St. Chrysostom, "on account of torrential rains there were litanies and supplications, and our whole city, like a torrent, rushed unto the apostolic shrines; we supplicated, calling upon St. Peter and the blessed Andrew, this pair of apostles, as well as Paul and Timothy" [^669]. Basil the Great speaks of litanies as a new but profitable institution: "In his time," that is, under Gregory the Wonderworker († 270), Bishop of Neocaesarea, he writes unto the Neocaesareans, "there were not even litanies such as ye perform now; and I speak this not by way of accusation against you, for I would desire that all of you live in tears and unceasing repentance" [^670]. Concerning Epiphanius, Bishop of Ticinum (*Ticiensis*, in Italy) in the 4th century, his biographer Ennodius (5th century) relates that he "decreed (*censuit*) that at every pestilential contagion (*aeris errore*) processions should be conducted (*procedendum*)" [^671]. The Emperor Theodosius the Great, before the war with Eugenius, arranged a litany through the city: in haircloth, with priests and people, he made the circuit of all the places of prayer, lying prostrate before the tombs of the martyrs and apostles, entreating of the saints help for himself [^672]. The same was done by the Emperors Theodosius the Younger and Marcian [^673]. The Greek writer Marcus the Deacon (5th century) in his *Life of St. Porphyrius, Bishop of Gaza* († 420), relates concerning a Litiya on the occasion of drought: "Having taken the sign (*signo*) of the Precious Cross which preceded us, we went forth with hymns unto the ancient church situated unto the west of the city. Porphyrius himself followed (walked behind), bearing the Holy Gospel and having around him the pious clergy"; further on the author indicates also the number of prayers at this Litiya before different temples [^674]. Frequently also litanies in honor of martyrs are mentioned. Thus Sidonius Apollinaris, Bishop of Clermont (*Arvernorum*, in Gaul) in the 5th century, speaks of a "pre-dawn procession" unto the tomb of the Martyr Justus [^675]. Few ritual particulars concerning such litanies have been preserved. Sozomen, concerning the procession at the translation of the remains of the Martyr Meletius unto Antioch, remarks that it was performed amid "the alternate singing of psalms" [^676]. St. Augustine says that in the procession at the translation of the remains of St. Stephen the bishop walked in the midst of the people [^677].

Thus the Litiya or litany in the 4th century—that is, from its very origin—receives a dual character: on the one hand, it is a mournful, penitential supplication in times of public calamities or for their averting; on the other hand, it is attached unto the commemoration of the saints. Such a character it retains throughout all subsequent time, both in the Eastern and in the Wes*(Orig. p. 153)*tern Church; such an impress does

=== LEAF p150 ===
it bear even now among us [^678]. At the same time, from its very origin the litany or Litiya appears as something supplementary unto the ancient church offices, more precisely unto the vigil: it is celebrated after the vigil (as in the account of Marcus the Deacon) or before it (as in Sidonius Apollinaris). Such a place with respect unto church services the Litiya occupies also now among us. But in the practice of the 4th-century Church of Jerusalem, as we have seen, it already coalesces more closely with other church services (Vespers and Matins), although it loses not its fundamental character—as an addition unto them.

#### Elements of Divine Worship in the 4th and 5th Centuries

In order that the picture of divine worship in the 4th and 5th centuries may be complete, it is necessary to characterize in greater detail the constituent elements of the divine worship of that era—that is, prayer, psalmody, reading, and instruction—for which purpose it is necessary to assemble from the writers of this period the accounts concerning these elements of divine worship. These testimonies are important also because they familiarize us with the practice of different Churches (St. Basil the Great—the Asia Minor Church, St. Chrysostom—the Antiochian and Constantinopolitan, St. Jerome—the Roman, St. Augustine—the African, etc.).

#### Prayer

As regards prayer during divine worship in the 4th and 5th centuries, among the writers of the 4th and 5th centuries we find nothing that substantially supplements the information concerning it provided by the cited complete orders of divine worship of that era (in the *Apostolic Constitutions* and the *Pilgrimage*). Yet these latter data are so complete that in this respect there is nothing more to be desired. We possess therein both the very text of the prayers of that time, and detailed indications of the place occupied by them in divine worship, and the manner of their celebration. In comparison with later and present prayers they are distinguished, naturally, by greater brevity, but in divine worship they occupied a far more prominent and significant place than today: owing unto the fact that they were not, as now, silent, they must have occupied no less than half of each church service, if not more: according unto the *Pilgrimage* attributed unto Sylvia, the first part of the service, before the arrival of the bishop, consists of alternating psalms, hymns, and prayers, while the second half of the service—with the bishop—as it were consists entirely of prayers. In the divine worship of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, prayers occupy such a dominant position that the author passes over the other elements of divine worship in complete silence.

Unto the holy fathers of the 4th and 5th centuries are attributed many of the prayers in use today. Thus a whole series of prayers at the present Hours, as we shall see, including the prayer **"Thou Who at all times"** (*«Иже на всякое время»*), is attributed unto Basil the Great, *(Orig. p. 154)* as are the prayers of Pentecost (some of which
