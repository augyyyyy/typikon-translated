In Rome, already under Pope Gregory I the Great, there existed a calendar with commemorations for each day of the year, as is evident from the response of this Pope in 598 to the request of Patriarch Eulogius of Alexandria to send him the "Acts of All the Martyrs" collected by Eusebius of Caesarea; replying to the Patriarch that no such collection was found in the Roman archives, the Pope writes: "But we have collected in a single codex the names of almost all the martyrs, with the designation of their passions for all days, and each day celebrate masses in their honor; however, in this volume it is not shown how each one suffered, but only the name, place, and day of passion are designated... We think that you also possess this, most blessed brethren" (Gregory the Great, *Registrum*, VIII, 29). The compiler of the most ancient Roman calendar that has reached us is considered to be Blessed Jerome, on the basis of the testimonies of Cassiodorus († 563): "Read the passions of the martyrs, which you will find, among other places, in the letter of Jerome to Chromatius and Heliodorus" (*De Institutio Divinarum Litterarum*, 32; Migne, *Patrologia Latina*, vol. 70, col. 1147) and of the Venerable Bede († 735 [printed: 753]): "...the martyrology which by name and preface is ascribed to Blessed Jerome, although Jerome was not its author, but its translator, while the author, they say, was Eusebius" (*Liber Retractationis in Actus Apostolorum*, c. 1; Migne, *Patrologia Latina*, vol. 92, col. 997). The correspondence of Blessed Jerome with Bishops Heliodorus and Chromatius is appended to copies of the Hieronymian Calendar; these bishops asked that the calendar (*feriales*) of Eusebius be sent to them from Caesarea for the greater honoring of the martyrs, wherein Bishop Gregory of Cordova had especially distinguished himself, as became known at a certain Council of Milan, who celebrated "on every non-fast day morning masses, and during fasts evening masses, and commemorated at them many names of martyrs"; Blessed Jerome, instead of the calendar of Eusebius, sends the bishops his own, wherein, according to his epistle, he "wrote the festivals for each month and for each day; but since for each day there occurred the names of martyrs from various regions numbering more than 800–900, and for no single day, except January 1, fewer than 500, he writes only the more renowned." The authenticity of this correspondence is doubtful, as the style here is non-Hieronymian; and apart from it, the compilation of the calendar by Blessed Jerome is confirmed by nothing else: neither he himself, nor Gennadius of Marseilles († after 495) in his work *On Illustrious Men* (*De Scriptoribus Ecclesiasticis*), nor Gregory the Great speaks of such a compilation; furthermore, there are commemorations in the calendar later than Blessed Jerome. It was published from a tenth-century manuscript in Migne, *Patrologia Latina*, vol. 39, and by the Bollandists, *Acta Sanctorum*, Novemb., vol. II, 1894, from an eighth-century manuscript (by De Rossi and Duchesne).

If in the second century mass slaughter of Christians was expressed by a figure of no more than 40 (Lyons, 177), then in the third century, and especially under Diocletian, the martyrs numbered in the thousands; the magnitude of the figure therefore arouses no doubt, as does also the frequent repetition of certain names (Felix 118 times, Saturninus 87, etc.), as well as the strangeness of other names (Manira, Jtercola, Barbalabia); one must keep in mind the custom of the Romans not to be constrained in giving nicknames to slaves.
