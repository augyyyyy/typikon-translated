### Divine Worship of the Western Church

The development of Western divine worship took a different course, although at times it exhibited significant convergence with the Eastern. In the daily offices here, the fixed psalms adapted to the time of day soon disappeared, leaving only brief excerpts from them in the form of antiphons and responsories, which, thanks to the solemn manner of their performance (complex melodies and the special alternation of officiants during their chanting), occupied the most prominent position in the service; antiphons accompanied the chanting of the psalms, and responsories accompanied the readings. The psalms themselves, as also the liturgical readings, were selected not to suit the specific moment (the hour of the office or the commemorated event), but were recited in continuous cursus: the entire Psalter was chanted through in regular order at the services, just as the books read in divine worship were read consecutively; consequently, the whole essence of divine worship lay in the quantity of psalms and antiphons, readings and responsories; by this quantity the solemnity of the office was measured. Only for certain offices, namely for the final portion of Matins, the 1st Hour, and Compline, were there fixed psalms adapted to them; and the connection of an office to the celebrated event was expressed only by a single reading (for example, on Sunday one of *(Orig. p. 304)* the readings was the Gospel of the Resurrection). Thus, only the antiphons and responsories bore a relation to the specific liturgical moment. Yet almost the principal difference between Western and Eastern worship, both in this epoch and in the subsequent one (as partly also in the preceding), was the extremely restricted use of hymns of Christian composition. In some places there was even a movement in favor of entirely excluding hymns from the body

=== LEAF p292 ===
of divine service. The Second Council of Braga (*Bracarensis*, in Spain) of 563 requires "that, apart from the psalms and canonical scriptures of the Old and New Testaments, no poetic composition (*nihil poetice compositum*) be sung in church, as the holy canons also command" (canon 12). But the Second Council of Tours [in Gaul] of 567 decrees:
"Although in the canon we possess only the Ambrosian hymns, nevertheless, since certain of the remaining ones are worthy of being sung in form, we willingly agree to accept, in addition to them, those whose authors' names are indicated in the margins" (canon 23).
The 1st [4th] Council of Toledo of 633 (canon 13) demonstrates thoroughly and persistently the legitimacy of hymns in divine service against those who reject them. In the Church of Lyons, hymns are sung only at Compline; likewise in Vienne, hymns are sung only at Compline, varying on different feasts [^1518].

### Western Cathedral-Parochial Divine Worship in the VI–VIII Centuries

As in the East, the entire task of developing divine worship was assumed by monasticism; therefore, information regarding cathedral-parochial worship is very sparse. The daily offices were the same as in the East; but the Midnight Office and Matins formed a single service, frequently termed *vigilia* (vigil) from its commencement in the deep of night; according to the divisions of the night, it was divided into several, for the most part three, *nocturni* (nocturns), followed by Matins in the strict sense, designated *matutinum* or *ad Laudes*. The Hours were not celebrated everywhere, so that on the part of Caesarius, Bishop of Arles (in Gaul, † 542), a special ordinance was required so that the clerics, besides Matins and Vespers, would celebrate in the Cathedral of St. Stephen in Arles the 3rd, 6th, and 9th Hours with their hymns [^1519]. Regarding the order and rite of parochial services, the following testimonies have been preserved. The night service or Matins, at least in certain localities, gradually began to be started later and later. Whereas Gregory of Tours in the VI century says of his contemporaries that they "arise at midnight to render thanksgiving unto the Lord" [^1520], in the VIII century Chrodegang (*Grodegangus*), Bishop of Metz, in his rules for canons permits them "in wintertime, that is, from the Kalends of November until Pascha, to arise at the 8th hour (= 2 o'clock) of the night, in order to sleep a little even after midnight and be more alert for the vigil" [^1521]; and Amalarius in the IX century, explaining the psalm verses at the night vigils that precede the readings, says: "The second verse: **"At midnight I arose"** (*"Polunoshchi vostakh"* / *Media nocte surgebam*); it indicates at what hour of the night one must arise; but since *(Orig. p. 305)* we neglect the time at which our fathers were accustomed to arise, we dare not

=== LEAF p293 ===
lie, and we replace this verse with another: **"For Thou wilt light my lamp"** (*"Yako Ty esi prosveshchiaiai svetil'nik moi"* / *Quoniam tu illuminas lucernam meam*)" [^1522]. There is an indication regarding the structure of the night service in the acts of the Gallic Council of 499 against the Arians in the presence of Gundobad, King of the Burgundians. When the bishops celebrated a vigil at the tomb of St. Justus on his feast day,
"it came to pass that when the reader at night according to custom began the reading of Moses, he came upon the words: **"And I will harden his heart"**; then, when after the singing of the psalms he read from the Prophets, there occurred the words of the Lord to Isaiah: **"Go, and say unto this people: Hearing ye shall hear..."** When the psalms were sung again, and he (the reader) read from the Gospel, he came upon the words with which the Savior rebukes the unbelief of the Jews: **"Woe unto thee, Chorazin..."** When at last there was a reading from the Apostle, the following words were pronounced: **"Or despisest thou the riches of His goodness..."** This was noticed by all the bishops, and all recognized that these readings fell out by the will of God, so that it might be known that the king's heart was hardened" [^1523].
Regarding the number of psalms at the night service, which is not stated in this account, the Second Council of Tours (in Gaul) of 567 says:
"At Matins on feast days let 6 antiphons be expanded (*explicentur*), each with two psalms; throughout the whole of August let there be accelerated Matins (*manicationes*, from *mane*), because at that time there are feasts and masses of saints; in September, 7 antiphons with two psalms each; in October, 8 with 3 psalms; in November, 9 with 3; in December, 10 with 3; in January and February the same until Pascha (consequently, the greatest number of psalms at Matins was 30, and the smallest 12); but whoever recites fewer than 12 psalms, let him fast until evening, eating bread with water, and let him have no other nourishment on that day; and whoever neglects to do this latter, let him eat bread with water all week and fast every day until evening" (canon 18).
At the night service, a special independent position was occupied by its concluding portion, which fell at dawn (*ad Laudes*); it was celebrated with particular solemnity. Gregory of Tours recounts of Gallus of Arverna (Clermont) that, although in other places at dawn they sang only the Benediction (*Benedictio*—the Canticles of the Three Holy Children and of Zacharias,

which begin with the verb "to bless" and are appointed now in the final part of Catholic Matins [^1524]), he concluded Matins having chanted Ps. 50, the Benediction, and performed the Alleluiaticum with a little chapter (*alleluiatico cum capitello*—a brief excerpt from the Old or New Testament)" (all this is appointed even today at *ad Laudes*). — Regarding the office of the 1st Hour in the Western Church of this epoch, it is known that confession of sins took place at it: "Before Ps. 50," says Chrodegang (*Grodegangus*), Bishop of Metz in the VIII century concerning the 1st Hour, "let them make confession one to another, saying: **"I confess unto the Lord and unto thee, brother, that I have sinned in thought, word, and deed; therefore I beseech thee, pray for me"**; and the other brother answers: **"May Almighty God have mercy upon thee, and forgive thee all thy sins, deliver *(Orig. p. 306)* thee from every evil, preserve thee in all good, and bring thee unto life everlasting"**" [^1525] (a similar confession exists in a different formula even today at the Roman Catholic 1st Hour; cf. among us the mutual forgiveness at the end of Compline and the Midnight Office). Following the 1st Hour in certain churches there was a distinct office—the *officium capituli* (chapter office), consisting of the reading of some canonical or pastoral rule (cf. among us the Catechesis of St. Theodore the Studite at the 2nd Hour) and the martyrology, followed by the verse: **"Precious in the sight of the Lord is the death of His saints"** (*"Chestna pred Gospodem smert' prepodobnykh Ego"* / Ps. 115:6; cf. our prokeimenon); on Sundays, Wednesdays, Fridays, and feast days of saints, the first reading was replaced by a homily [^1526]. Among the other offices there is mention of the 6th Hour and Vespers. The aforementioned Council of Tours states: "The patristic ordinances command that at the 6th hour 6 psalms with Alleluia be said, and at the 12th hour 12 psalms likewise with Alleluia" (canon 18). By the 12th hour must be understood, presumably, Vespers, since Compline could only have appeared after Vespers. The Council of Mérida (*Emeritensis*) in Spain of 606 requires that "as in other churches on feast days in the evening, after the bringing in of the light (*post lumen oblatum*), the *vespertinum* (evening psalm) is said before the *sonus* (sound), so let this be observed also by us in our churches" (canon 2). The obscure terms encountered here are well explained by the order of Vespers in the Mozarabic (Spanish) Breviary: "Whether on a feast day or an ordinary day, the hours (the reckoning of liturgical time) always begin with Vespers on the eve [^1527], and the beginning is made through the invocation of Jesus Christ with the preceding Lord's Prayer—through pronouncing in a low voice: **"Kyrie eleison, Christe eleison, Kyrie eleison. Our Father"**. And in a loud voice: **"In the Name of the Lord Jesus Christ, light be with you"** [^1528], that is, light hath been brought in. Those present respond: **"Thanks be to God"** (*Deo gratias*). And the presbyter says: **"The Lord be always with you"**. Response: **"And with thy spirit"**. And the order of Vespers, whether a feast or not, proceeds as follows: first the psalm is said, that is, the *Vespertinum* (evening psalm) [^1529]; upon its conclusion the presbyter says: **"The Lord be with you"**. Response: **"And with thy spirit"**. Immediately the *sonus* is said, if it be a feast, seeing that a weekday hath no *sonus*, with the exception of Sunday, when it is said for the sake of solemnity. Such is the rule. And this *sonus* is: **"O come,**

**let us rejoice unto the Lord..."** (*"Priidite, vozraduemsia Gospodevi..."* / *Venite, exsultemus Domino* [Ps. 94:1 / MT 95:1]) [^1530].
On Sundays the office was more solemn: according to the ancient rite of the churches of Cambrai (*Cameracensis*) and Arras (*Atrebatensis*), the Sunday vigil was to begin before midnight, the ringing for it with all the bells (*signa*), the church was to be illuminated abundantly, the readings and responsories were to be performed protractedly (*tractim*), the ninth reading was evangelical from patristic homilies and was read by the deacon, presbyter, or bishop in its entirety, unless dawn intervened; at the beginning of each of the three nocturns there was censing with 2 censers, which at the *(Orig. p. 307)* first nocturn were brought to the altar, at the second into the choir, and at the third to the people; the morning portion of the vigil was chanted still more slowly and in a higher pitch than the nocturns [^1531].

### Antiphons, Responsories, and Collects

For a complete conception of Western cathedral-parochial divine worship in the VI–VIII centuries, a few additional remarks must be made regarding the manner of performing antiphons and responsories, which occupied such an important place in it, as also concerning the rite of concluding the service, since even greater significance was attached to this conclusion of worship here than in the East. In the epoch under consideration, the term *antiphon* had already fully acquired that meaning which it bears in contemporary Roman Catholic worship, that is, the meaning of a brief verse before a psalm or series of psalms or after it—a verse taken for the most part from that very psalm, expressing its central theme or adapting the psalm to the feast. Today, however, the antiphon is chanted only once before or after the psalm, whereas in the period under consideration antiphons were sometimes chanted with every verse of the psalm. Thus the canons of the Church of St. Martin of Tours, according to the testimony of John the Monk in the Life of St. Odo (Abbot of Cluny, † 943), wishing to prolong the service until dawn, chanted the antiphons twice at every verse of the psalm. The same is evident from the fact that on the greatest feast days at Matins (*in Laudibus*) at the end of the Canticle of Zacharias, and at Vespers at the end of the Canticle of the Theotokos (Magnificat), the antiphon was repeated thrice: once before **"Glory to the Father..."**, a second time at **"Both now and ever..."** (*Sicut erat*), and a third time at the entire concluding verse (such chanting of the antiphon was termed *triumphare—ter fari*, to 'triple') [^1532]. In a manuscript antiphonary of the XI century, to every verse of the Canticle of the Theotokos there is assigned as an antiphon a verse from the Gospel of Matthew concerning the Resurrection of Christ. The chanting of the antiphon was begun (*impositio*), at a sign from the archdeacon, by particularly skilled cantors holding the rank of subdeacon, of whom there were 7 in the Roman Church: the first antiphon being intoned by the foremost of these subdeacons (*primicerius*), the second by the second-ranking (*secundicerius*), and so forth; only at Pascha did a deacon intone the antiphon to the Gospel [^1533]. (Cf. below the Asmatic Cursus / Sung Office and above the assessment of Abba Nilus of Sinai regarding cathedral

=== LEAF p296 ===
divine worship). By *responsory* was meant, for the most part, a verse sung in connection with a reading (cf. our prokeimenon), specifically following the reading. This is a verse taken mostly from a psalm, expressing the fundamental thought of the lesson; today by responsory is properly understood the second half of such a verse, constituting the choir's response to its first half sung by the cantor or priest, which is termed the verse—*versus*, *versiculum*. Amalarius (IX century) notes the difference in the singing of the responsory between the Roman and Gallican churches: the Romans, upon finishing the verse, 'begin the responsory from the beginning,' that is, they answer the singing of the verse by repeating that same entire verse *(Orig. p. 308)* (as among us in chanting the prokeimenon the second time), whereas in Gaul 'from the final verse they make a responsory by means of its two parts, joining them into a single body' (as among us in chanting the prokeimenon the 3rd time); for this reason, for a responsory it was necessary to select a verse both parts of which would possess their own meaning, while their union would yield a new thought [^1534]. The responsory was chanted in a special place of the temple or in the midst of the choir, but not upon the ambo. The verse preceding the responsory was always chanted more softly than the latter (cf. among us the chanting of the verse unto the prokeimenon), out of apprehension that the voice might not suffice for the responsory: 'while at the repetition of the responsory they raise the voice with confidence, no longer fearing the verse' [^1535]. According to canon 17 of the Fourth Council of Toledo of 633, 'some at the end of the responsory do not say **"Glory to the Father..."** on the ground that it sometimes sounds unsuitable; but let there be such a distinction, that on joyful responsories **"Glory..."** should follow, while on mournful ones the beginning should be repeated' (cf. among us the Alleluia of Lenten Vespers without the **"Glory..."**). In the Arabic recension of the canons of the Council of Nicaea it is required to chant the responsories from memory, and not from a book [^1536] (cf. our canonarch). — A very important part of Matins and Vespers was constituted by their conclusion, the final, dismissive prayer, termed the *collecta*. It was pronounced by the highest-ranking cleric present at the service. The Council of Barcelona (*Barcinonensis*) of 540 prohibits 'presbyters in the presence of the bishop to conclude (*non colligant*) the prayers' (canon 5). This conclusion was always identical for different offices, and Agrestius accused Abbot Columbanus at the Council of Mâcon (*Matisconensis*, in Gaul) in 585 of having introduced different collects for different offices. In the Lateran Basilica the Lord's Prayer served as the collect (cf. among us at the end of Vespers and at the end of the Hours according to the Horologia of the VIII–IX centuries), but this was a peculiarity and, as it were, a privilege of this single church, in which an indication was seen of the primacy and dignity of the Roman Church. In the words of the Spanish Abbot Beatus of the VII century, 'upon the conclusion of the office one must not depart from the church before they say: **"In the Name of our Lord Jesus Christ, let us go in peace"**' [^1537].

### Western Monastic Divine Worship

We possess far more information concerning monastic divine worship

=== LEAF p297 ===
in the West during the period under consideration. It differed in different monasteries (just as even now many Roman Catholic monasteries possess special liturgical Typika / rules). Several renowned abbots and bishops of this period left behind their rules for divine worship, which, albeit in varying degrees, influenced the development of the present Roman liturgical rite and exhibit in places interesting correspondences with our own Typikon. To these monastic rules, it would appear, Roman worship is primarily indebted for the elimination from the offices of the fixed psalms (for example, the morning psalms, *(Orig. p. 309)* the evening psalm) and their replacement by continuous divisions of the Psalter. The most important of these monastic rules is: 1) that of St. Benedict of Nursia († 543), founder of the famous monastery of Monte Cassino in southern Italy and of the order bearing his name, and generally the principal organizer of Western monasticism. In his *Rule* (*Regula*) he refers unto the rules of Basil the Great, the *Collationes* of St. Cassian, and at times reveals dependence upon the Pachomian rules [^1538]. 2) Benedict's rival in influence upon Western monasticism was Columbanus († 615), an Irishman [lit. *Scot*], founder of a series of monasteries first in Gaul, then in Italy; his monastic rules are entirely distinctive, rather severe (bearing the imprint of his northern origin), contain no references unto older rules, including that of Benedict, and were adopted in many monasteries prior unto the introduction of the Benedictine Rule. Further noted for their monastic rules were: 3) Caesarius, Bishop of Arles (*Arelatensis*, in Gaul, † 542), and his second successor in the see, 4) Aurelian; unto the former, besides the *Regula ad monachos*, is also attributed the *Regula ad virgines* (intended for the sister of Caesarius and the nuns living with her) with a more complex and detailed liturgical order than in the first *Rule*, which excites doubt concerning their authenticity; Aurelian's rules coincide in places with those of Columbanus; 5) Isidore, Bishop of Seville, † ca. 666; 6) Donatus, Bishop of Besançon (*Vesontionensis*) in northern Gaul [or *Vesontio*], † ca. 624 (*Regula ad virgines*); 7) Fructuosus, Bishop of Braga (*Bracarensis*) in Spain, † after 675; 8) an anonymous author, surnamed *Magister* of the VII century (*Regula Magistri*); and 9) two unknown abbots: the rule of one of them was published with the works of Blessed Augustine, that of the other with the rules of St. Benedict [^1539].

### The Night Office

The rule of psalmody is simplest in the last-named anonymous author:
'One must assemble for three daytime gatherings and for as many at night; at each daytime gathering one must chant 3 psalms, and at all the night gatherings 12, besides that office (*missam*) which is celebrated at sunrise; prayers must also be pronounced, as well as two readings, one from

=== LEAF p298 ===
the Old Testament and one from the New at each of the night gatherings; at the gatherings on Saturday and Sunday the office must be celebrated until cockcrow' (canon 30).
For the night office, close unto this rule in simplicity was the order observed by Columbanus in many (up to 1,000) monasteries contemporary with him:
'There are certain catholics who have one and the same canonical number of 12 psalms for both short and long nights, and from this rule they take for each *(Orig. p. 310)* night watch 4 psalms: for the beginning of the night, for midnight or cockcrow, and for Matins...; but on the especially sacred (*reverentissimis*) nights of Saturday and Sunday this number (of psalms) is tripled, that is, there are 36 of them' (ch. 7).
It is remarkable how in both of these orders the system of 12 psalms is maintained, transferred from Egypt unto the West already by St. Cassian. Somewhat more complex, but closer than others unto these orders, is the night office in St. Benedict: in it the 12-psalm system is likewise maintained, but the 12 psalms are sharply divided into two sections of 6 psalms each (cf. among us the Six Psalms and the Praises / Lauds psalms at Matins); moreover, in St. Benedict the office receives all the accessories of cathedral-parochial worship: antiphons, responsories, and other verses; great significance is likewise attached unto the readings. The night office (*vigiliae*) according to the Rule of St. Benedict begins with the verses **"O God, come to my assistance"** (*"Bozhe, v pomoshch' moiu vonmi"* / *Deus in adjutorium meum intende*) and (is said, *dicitur*) **"O Lord, open Thou my lips"** (*"Gospodi, ustne moi otverzeshi"* / *Domine labia mea aperies*) thrice (today in reverse order and the second verse once; cf. among us); then Ps. 3 (cf. among us) with **"Glory to the Father..."**, and Ps. 94 [LXX 94 / MT 95] **"O come, let us rejoice unto the Lord"** (*Venite, exsultemus Domino* / *"Priidite, vozraduemsia Gospodevi"*; as today), chanted with antiphonal refrains (consequently melodically, as today) or simply (*certe decantandus*, presumably depending on the solemnity of the service), and an Ambrosian hymn (as today; not the *Te Deum*); all this forms the introductory part of the office, just as today. Next follow 6 psalms with antiphons; after they are pronounced (*dictis*) and the abbot's blessing is given, there are three readings from the Old and New Testaments or from commentaries upon them, with responsories between them, during which the brethren remain seated; they rise at the **"Glory..."** of the final responsory, which alone has it (**"Glory..."**; cf. among us at the kathismata). Another 6 psalms are sung, now with Alleluia, and after them a reading of the Apostle from memory (presumably being very brief, = *capitulum*), a verse, and the petition of the litany, that is, **"Kyrie eleison"** (cf. our Augmented Litany and Litiya), with which the vigil concludes. Thus is the night office celebrated from November until Pascha; in summer, however, it is abbreviated: instead of 3 readings there is one from

=== LEAF p299 ===
the Old Testament with one responsory and one from the New Testament. On Sunday the vigil is considerably lengthened, first of all by virtue of the readings: there are 13 of them instead of the 4 or 2 on weekdays: after the first 6 psalms there are 4 readings (not 3 as on weekdays) with responsories, and after the second 6 psalms (sung with antiphons and not with Alleluia), likewise 4 readings (not 1 as on weekdays); furthermore, the vigil receives at the end a new part, as it were a third nocturn, the most solemn: after the second series of readings
'three canticles from the Prophets are sung (cf. our triodion / three-canticle ode), such as the abbot designates, with Alleluia; after the recitation of a verse and the abbot's blessing there are again 4 readings from the New Testament, then the hymn **"We praise Thee, O God"** (*Te Deum laudamus* / *"Tebe Boga khvalim"*), and the abbot reads from the Gospel with fear and trembling, during which all stand, and upon the conclusion of the reading respond: **"Amen"**; unto this the abbot adds: **"Praise becometh Thee"** (*"Tebe podobaiet khvala"*) and, having given the blessing, begins Matins' (chs. 9, 10, 11).
Other rules appoint for the night office fewer or more than 12 psalms, and moreover an unequal quantity of them for different seasons of the year. Thus Isidore appoints for the vigil only 6 psalms and canticles: namely,
'first must be recited (*recitandi*) three canonical (fixed?) psalms, then three masses of psalms *(Orig. p. 311)* (presumably psalms with readings; see below in Caesarius), the fourth (mass) of canticles, the fifth of Matins; on Sundays and feasts of martyrs, for the sake of solemnity, particular (*singulae*) masses are added' (ch. 7).
The first unknown author appoints 6, 5, or 4 psalms: 'The night prayers in November–February have 12 antiphons, 6 psalms, 3 readings; in March, April, September, and October, 10 antiphons, 5 psalms, 3 readings; in May–August, 8 antiphons, 4 psalms, 2 readings' (ch. 1). Caesarius of Arles: from Pascha until October, 18 psalms, 2 readings, a hymn, and a *capitellum* (short reading), and from October until Pascha unto this nocturn there is added yet another, likewise of 18 psalms, 2 readings, and a hymn [^1540]. The Sunday vigil is distinguished by the fact that the readings are from the Gospel: the 1st concerning the Resurrection, the 2nd of the day of the week [Sunday], the 3rd concerning the Resurrection, the 4th of the day. Similarly also Aurelian:
'Daily at nocturns first of all there is recited in direct mode (*directaneus*—by a single choir) **"Have mercy upon me, O God, according to Thy great mercy"** (*"Pomilui mia, Bozhe, po velitsei milosti Tvoei"* / Ps. 50 [LXX 50 / MT 51]); then 18 psalms, three small antiphons, two readings from the Apostle or the Prophets,

=== LEAF p300 ===
(a hymn), and a *capitellum*; upon the completion of the nocturns say Matins; thus from Pascha until October; but from October one must join other nocturns: the Psalm **"Have mercy upon me, O God, have mercy upon me"** (*"Pomilui mia, Bozhe, pomilui mia"* / Ps. 56 [LXX 56 / MT 57]), 18 psalms, 2 readings from the Prophets or Solomon, a hymn... and then, since the nights grow longer, perform 3 masses (readings) from the book; after the first 2 readings, a prayer; after the third, an antiphon, a responsory, an antiphon, and again a reading (*capitellum*?).'
Columbanus, so far as one can understand the obscure text of his rule (ch. 7), for winter vigils (from the autumnal equinox until February) appoints 36 psalms with 12 antiphons, and for summer vigils 24 psalms with 8 antiphons; on Saturdays and Sundays from November until February, 75 psalms with 25 antiphons; from February until May this number is decreased each week by 2 psalms with 1 antiphon, so that at the beginning of May there are 36 psalms (on weekdays then only 24), and such a number remains for the Saturday and Sunday vigil until July; from that point it again increases weekly by 3 psalms, until by November it reaches once more 75 psalms. Similar unto Columbanus is Donatus, except that for weekdays he divides the year into two equal halves by the equinoxes, and for Saturdays and Sundays from February 1 gradually reduces the number of psalms from 60 to 36; from May until October, 36 psalms; this number by November *(Orig. p. 312)* increases unto 75 and remains such until February. Fructuosus is the first to divide the night office into three parts, as it is divided today in the Roman Catholic Church:
'Having arisen before midnight, let them recite (*recitent*) by choirs 12 (*duodenos*) psalms; having rested a little, at midnight let them celebrate the office at which 3 responsories are sung (consequently, probably readings as well) for 3 divisions of psalms, so that after the recitation of 3 psalms there follows one responsory, and thus with 3 responsories 12 psalms are sung; after midnight, if it be wintertime, all sit, and one, sitting in the midst, reads, while the abbot or superior in his discourse explains what is read unto those who do not understand, and in conclusion, having again sung 12 psalms, they go to sleep a little until Matins (*Laudes*)' (ch. 3).
Concerning Sunday vigils Fructuosus says nothing. According to the Master (*Magister*):
'at nocturns the abbot intones the opening verse (*apertionis*, that is, **"O Lord, open Thou my lips"** [*Domine, labia mea aperies*]),
