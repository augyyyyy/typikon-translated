# THE GREAT LITANY

## The Litany

*(Orig. p. 510)*
The song of praise unto God which Psalm 103 presents is not only accompanied and supplemented by the secret prayers of the priest, but is also replaced by the prayer of all the faithful. Such a prayer is the litany following the introductory psalm. The litany is a prayer of an entirely special character. It is calculated to tire the attention as little as possible, and to arouse it constantly. In view of this, a unified prayer is divided into a series of brief, distinct petitions, which are interrupted by the chanting of even *(Orig. p. 511)* more concise prayerful exclamations: **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Kyrie eleison*), **"Grant this, O Lord."** The name of this prayer, "litany," *ἐκτενή* (*ektenē*) [^2078]—an intense, diligent supplication—is, however, in Greek liturgical books applied only unto that which is termed among us the "Augmented Litany" (*sugubaya ekteniya*); litany in general is termed there *συναπτή* (*synaptē*, scil. *εὐχή*)—a connected or composite prayer. The name of litany was applied unto prayers of this kind because they represent especially fervent prayers offered up by all the faithful. With the purpose of drawing all into participation in them, they are intoned not by the priest—a person who by original custom was of advanced age ("presbyter")—but by the deacon; for which reason in ancient liturgical monuments the litany was termed *τὰ διακονικά* (*ta diakonika*), "diaconals" [^2079]. In view of the fact that the deacon is not a celebrant in the proper sense, the litany is composed not in properly prayerful expressions, but in expressions inviting unto prayer and indicating its objects. Yet from time to time (at the end of the Great Litany, in the final petition, and at the beginning of the Augmented and Petitional Litanies) this invitation unto prayer rises unto genuine prayer (**"Help us, save us..."**, **"Have mercy on us, O God..."**).

## The Great Litany: Its Character and Content

The first litany at the most important daily services is the Great Litany (*ἡ συναπτὴ μεγάλη* / *hē synaptē megalē*), which in antiquity was also termed *τὰ εἰρηνικά* (*ta eirēnika*), "peaceful" [petitions], that is, supplications (*αἰτήματα* / *aitēmata*; see Introductory Chapter, p. 345). From the three other kinds of litanies it is distinguished by the fullness of its content: not to speak of the Little Litany, which represents a simple abbreviation of the great one—whereas

=== LEAF p612 ===
the Augmented Litany prays exclusively for persons, and the Petitional Litany for needs without distinction of persons, the Great Litany unites both supplications, so that the Augmented and Petitional are only a further unfolding of its content, for which reason it is placed at the very beginning of the service. Furthermore, from the other litanies it is distinguished by the sublimity and, as it were, mysteriousness of its content. Its supplication it begins not with any particular and everyday needs, even spiritual ones, but with that peace from above (*τῆς ἄνωθεν* / *tēs anōthen*), which the Apostle terms "surpassing all understanding" [^2080]. From these truly celestial heights the Great Litany in its 14 petitions (with the exclamation, 15) gradually descends unto circles closer and closer unto us: unto the world, unto the holy churches [^2081], their *(Orig. p. 512)* primates and ministers [^2082], unto the civil authority [^2083], our city (or monastery) and country and their needs, unto those who stand in greatest need of the help of God (**"For travelers by sea..."**—an enumeration in ascending order of the gravity of distress) [^2084], and only at the very end unto ourselves. The supplication concludes with a summons to turn for intercession in our needs, for which we have prayed, unto the saints, and especially unto the Mother of God, unto Whom are applied the same seven sublime titles that are applied unto Her in the liturgical exclamation **"Especially for our all-holy..."** (why, see below), to turn in sweet and tranquil hope for the fulfillment of the prayer. The conclusion of the litany is a doxology, wherein the glory of God is set forth both as the supreme ground for the fulfillment of our petition (as the glory of God is in general the foundation and goal of the world) [^2085] and which, as the supreme, angelic kind of prayer (see Introductory Chapter, p. 27), containing moreover the naming of the All-Holy Trinity (ibid., p. 17), the priest himself pronounces.

## History of the Great Litany: The Litany in First Clement

Already from the first century there has been preserved a prayer not only close in content unto the present Great Litany, but also termed *ἐκτενῆ τὴν δέησιν* (*ektenē tēn deēsin*). With such an "intense supplication" St. Clement, Bishop of Rome, counsels the Corinthian Christians to pray in his epistle written ca. 90–100, and adduces this supplication, taken, presumably, from the practice of the Roman Church:

"We ask (*ἀξιοῦμεν* / *axioumen*), O Lord, that Thou be our helper and protector; those of us in affliction *defend* [^2086], the humble *have mercy on*, the fallen raise up, unto those in need appear... [the infirm] *heal*, the wanderers of Thy people turn back, feed the hungry, ransom our prisoners, *raise up the sick*, comfort the fainthearted, that all

=== LEAF p613 ===
the nations may know that Thou art God alone, and Jesus Christ Thy Servant, and we Thy people and the sheep of Thy pasture. Thou hast made manifest the everlasting constitution (*σύστασιν* / *systasin*) of the world through the works wrought (*διὰ τῶν ἐνεργουμένων* / *dia tōn energoumenōn*); Thou, O Lord, didst create the universe, faithful throughout all generations, righteous in judgments, marvelous in strength and majesty (cf. the exclamation), wise in creation and prudent in establishing that which hath been made, good in things visible and... unto those who trust in Thee; have mercy and compassion, forgive us our iniquities and unrighteousness and transgressions and shortcomings; reckon not every sin of Thy servants and handmaids, but cleanse us with the purifi*(Orig. p. 513)*cation of Thy truth and guide our steps to walk in holiness of heart and to do that which is good and well-pleasing before Thee and before our rulers (*ἀρχόντων* / *archontōn*). Yea, Lord, make Thy face to shine upon us for good in peace, that we may be shielded by Thy mighty hand and delivered from sin by Thy high arm, and deliver us from them that hate us unjustly. *Grant concord and peace unto us and unto all that dwell upon the earth*, as Thou didst give unto our fathers when they called upon Thee in faith and truth, to be obedient... and unto Thine all-virtuous (*παναρέτῳ* / *panaretō*) name. *Unto our rulers and governors (τοῖς τε ἄρχουσι καὶ ἡγουμένοις / tois te archousi kai hēgoumenois) upon earth Thou, O Lord, hast given the power of sovereignty through Thine awesome and ineffable might, that we, knowing the glory and honour given them by Thee, may submit ourselves unto them, in no way resisting Thy will; unto them, O Lord, grant health, peace, concord, and stability (εὐστάθειαν / eustatheian)*, that they may administer (*διέπειν* / *diepein*) the governance given them by Thee without stumbling; for Thou, O Lord, heavenly King of the ages, givest unto the sons of men glory and honour and power over that which is upon earth; do Thou, O Lord, direct their counsel according unto that which is good and well-pleasing in Thy sight, that administering in peace and gentleness the authority given them by Thee, they may find Thee merciful. Thou Who alone art able to do these things and far greater good with us, unto Thee do we make confession through the High Priest and Guardian of our souls, Jesus Christ, *through Whom be glory and majesty unto Thee, both now and unto all generations, and unto ages of ages. Amen*" [^2087].

## The Primary Source of the Great Litany

It is possible that this is a Eucharistic prayer;

=== LEAF p614 ===
the intercessory prayers of ancient liturgies recall it. Out of these latter prayers, which certain liturgies possessed prior unto the hallowing of the Gifts, and others after it, litanies arose. In later liturgies, both Eastern and Western, the intercessory Eucharistic prayer was pronounced solely by the priest. But the most ancient liturgies, presumably, drew the deacon into it as well. The deacon's exclamations at this prayer, which invited the people unto participation therein and announced for this purpose briefly the content of one or another of the most important parts of this prayer, gave rise unto the litanies. The manner and degree of this participation of the deacon, and following him the people, in the intercessory Eucharistic prayer were diverse in different liturgies. In the most ancient liturgies, if one regards as their representatives the liturgies of the Eastern culturally stationary Christian borderlands (Abyssinians, Copts, Persians, Syrians), this participation was very extensive. The deacon in his exclamations gave lengthy paraphrases of the priestly prayer (in an invitatory form), and the people responded unto these invitations unto prayer with entire prayers, rather than with brief exclamations alone, such as **"Lord, have mercy."**
*(Orig. p. 514)*
## The Litany in the Abyssinian Liturgy

Thus, in the Ethiopian (Abyssinian) Liturgy, after the part corresponding unto our Proskomedia and the initial exclamations of the priest,
"the deacon says: Stand up for prayer. Priest: Peace be unto all. People: And with thy spirit. D.: Stand up for prayer. Priest: Peace be unto all of you. P.: Lord, have mercy upon us. With thy spirit. The priest—a prayer analogous unto the following exclamation of the deacon and interrupted by an invitation of the priest himself: Pray.
Deacon: Ask and pray, that the Lord may have mercy upon us and spare us, and accept the prayer and supplication which is made for us by His saints, that, ever showing goodwill toward us, He may make us worthy of receiving and partaking of the blessed Mystery, and forgive us our sins. And all the people shall say thrice: Lord, have mercy. The priest—a prayer for those who have offered gifts. D.: Pray for those who have offered gifts. Priest—a prayer of similar content. After the Gospel, the deacon: Stand up for prayer. Priest: 'Peace"

be unto all of you,' and he reads a prayer, whose petitions for various classes of the faithful or needs the deacon interrupts with exclamations: Pray for this Holy Church, one, Catholic and Apostolic, Orthodox in the Lord. People: Lord our God, grant us peace; Christ our King, have mercy upon us. D.: Pray for the archpastors, our Patriarch Abba N., the lord Archbishop of the great city of Alexandria, and our Metropolitan Abba N., and all Orthodox bishops, priests, and deacons. Pray for this Holy Church and our assembly therein. P.: Bless our assembly and preserve it in peace. After the Creed, the priest [recites] 'the prayer for perfect peace,' interrupted by the exclamation of the deacon: Pray for perfect peace and the mutual kiss [^2088] of the Apostles. Priest—a prayer with the commemoration of the saints and the faithful. People: Have mercy, O Lord, upon the souls of Thy servants who have eaten Thy Body and drunk Thy Blood and received rest in Thy faith" [^2089].

## The Litany in the Coptic Liturgy

As a further stage in the development of the litany one may recognize that form which the deacon's exclamations possess in the Coptic Liturgy attributed unto St. Cyril of Alexandria. Here, at the intercessory prayer before the hallowing of the Gifts, when the priest begins petitions for one or another class of the faithful or their needs, the deacon makes exclamations for them, after which the priest continues the prayer, preceding, interrupting, or concluding it with his **"Lord, have mercy."** The deacon's exclamations are such as these:
Pray for the peace of the one, Holy, Catholic, and

=== LEAF p616 ===
Apostolic Church, for the salvation of peoples and the security of every place, and for the remission of our sins. Pray for our fathers and brethren. Pray for our fathers and brethren who travel. Pray for the temperature of the air and the fruits of the earth. Pray that Christ our God... (for the emperor). Pray for the fathers... (the departed archbishops). Pray for those who of their own means have made sacrifices and offerings. Pray for the life and prosperity of the Patriarch and our venerable father Archbishop Abba N., that Christ our God may preserve his life for many years and peaceful times and spare us. Pray for the rest of the Orthodox who are in all the earth, that Christ our God may be gracious unto them and have mercy on them and spare us. Pray for the security of this place and all places of our Orthodox hermit and monastic fathers and those who dwell in them, and for the security of the whole world, that Christ our God may preserve them from every evil and spare us. Pray for those standing here who participate with us in prayers, that Christ our God may preserve them and have mercy on them and spare us. Pray for all who have charged us to remember them in our prayers and petitions, that Christ our God may deliver them from every evil. Pray unto God with fear. Pray for this holy priestly assembly and for the entire order of Orthodox priests, that Christ our God may establish them in the Orthodox faith even unto their last breath. Pray for this assembly of ours and for every assembly of Orthodox peoples, that Christ our God may bless them and perfect them in peace and forgive us our sins [^2090].

## The Litany in the Syrian Liturgy

At a similar stage of development stand the deacon's exclamations in the Syrian Liturgy of the Apostle James, common unto the Melkites (Orthodox) and the Jacobites—and consequently having appeared prior unto the Monophysite heresy—and in the ancient Mozarabic (Southern Spanish) Liturgy.
In the former, after the part corresponding unto our Proskomedia and the exclamation of the priest **"Glory to the Father..."**, the deacon says: "For the tranquillity and peace of the whole world of believers in Christ from the ends even unto the ends of the universe, for the weak and

=== LEAF p617 ===
the oppressed and suffering souls, for our fathers, brethren, and teachers, for the sins, transgressions, and offenses of us all, and for the faithful departed who have gone forth from us, with the offering of incense let us pray, O Lord." *(Orig. p. 516)*
The priest—a prayer of different, general content. A similar proclamation of the deacon occurs somewhat later. After the hallowing of the Gifts, the deacon says: Bless, master (*despota*). Let us pray and beseech the Lord our God at the present great and holy moment for our fathers and rulers... (that is, the Patriarch and bishops), let us pray unto the Lord. The priest—a prayer for them and the whole world. People: Amen. Deacon: Again and again we commemorate our faithful brethren, true Christians... (who have now asked to pray and are burdened with temptations and afflictions). The priest—a prayer with the response of the people: Amen. The deacon—a prayer for the sovereigns: Again and again we commemorate all faithful sovereigns, true Christians, who have erected and established the churches and monasteries of God in the four quarters of the earth, and the entire Christian commonwealth and the clergy and the faithful people, that they may prosper in virtues, let us pray unto the Lord. The priest—a prayer; the people—Amen. The deacon—commemoration of the saints: Again and again we commemorate... (the All-Holy Theotokos and the choirs of saints, with the naming of John the Baptist and Archdeacon Stephen)... for all of them let us pray unto the Lord. The priest—a prayer. The people—Amen. The deacon—commemoration of teachers: Again we commemorate before Thee, O Lord God, the teachers, interpreters of the undefiled faith... (namely, the departed among them), let us pray unto the Lord. The priest—a prayer. The people: Amen. The deacon—commemoration of the faithful departed: Again we commemorate... (with the ending): Therefore let us cry out and say: *Kyrie eleison* thrice. The priest—a prayer for the departed. People: Grant them rest, have mercy, and remit, O God, the sins... of us all... The priest—a prayer for the remission of sins and an unashamed end, with a doxology at the end. People: As it (Thy name) was and is unto generations of generations and unto the ages to come. Amen [^2091].

## The Litany in the Mozarabic Liturgy

In the Mozarabic Liturgy, something analogous unto the litany exists only on Holy Saturday (Holy Week in general preserves the greatest number of traces of ancient practice). Here, after each of the ten

Old Testament readings (= paremias) a prayer is appointed according unto the following order:
After the 1st reading (Gen. 1–2), "the deacon says: For (*pro*) the feast of Pascha. Let us bend our knees (*flectamus genua*). Arise (*levate*)" [^2092]. With the title "Prayer" (*Oratio*) follows a brief prayer (of the priest), after which is the *Responsorium* (the response of the people): Amen; then the conclusion of the priestly prayer, like our exclamation, and again Amen. After the 2nd reading, the deacon says: For those who, being detained by diverse necessities, are unable to be present at Pascha. Let us bend our knees. Arise, etc. 3. For the priests and ministers. 4. For the unity of the Catholic faith. 5. For virgins (*virginibus*—according unto the priestly prayer: "as the glorious portion of Christ, in which the Catholic Church rejoiceth most of all"). 6. For those who *(Orig. p. 517)* do alms. 7. For travelers and those that sail by sea. 8. For the sick. 9. For penitents. 10. For the peace of the people and rulers [^2093].

## The Litany in the Liturgy of the Evangelist Mark

At a similar stage of development stand the deacon's petitions in the Coptic Liturgy of the Evangelist Mark, wherein after each of such petitions, having the form "Pray for such and such," follows a brief prayer of the priest. The petitions are the following:
"Pray for the living, for the sick, for the absent.—For the temperature of the air and the fruits of the earth, for the proper rising of the river waters (of the Nile), for favorable rains and crops.—For the health of men and beasts, for the good estate of the world and city,—for Christ-loving rulers. For captives, for the departed and those who make offering, for the afflicted, for the catechumens.—For the peace of the Holy, one, Catholic and Apostolic Orthodox Church.—For our Patriarch Pope [^2094], Father N., lord Archbishop of the great city of Alexandria. For this holy church and for our assemblies" [^2095].
The order of petitions here is the reverse of our present order—from bodily, particular, and most urgent needs unto spiritual and universal ones.

Yet Greek manuscripts of this liturgy correct this order, placing in the first place the petition for the peace of the Holy Church [^2096]. This liturgy, like the other Coptic ones, has the deacon's petitions after the hallowing of the Gifts.
Significantly more do the deacon's petitions in the most ancient Greek manuscript of the Liturgy of the Evangelist Mark, in the 11th-century Rossano Codex (in Calabria), recall our present litanies. Here the liturgy begins:
**"Peace be unto all,"** **"And to thy spirit."** Deacon: Pray (*προσεύξασθε* / *proseuxasthe*). People: **"Lord, have mercy"**—thrice. The priest—a prayer (of general content—thanksgiving for the help of God and a petition for it and for preservation from evil and sin), the end of which ("Through Whom and with Whom unto Thee be glory and dominion in the Holy Spirit") [is pronounced] aloud. People: Amen. Priest: **"Peace be unto all."** P.: **"And to thy spirit."** D.: Pray for the emperor. P.: **"Lord, have mercy"** thrice. The priest—a prayer. P.: Amen. Priest: **"Peace be unto all."** P.: **"And to thy spirit."** D.: Pray for the Pope and the bishop. P.: **"Lord, have mercy"** thrice. The priest—a prayer. Amen. **"Peace be unto all."** **"And to thy spirit."** D.: Stand up for prayer. P.: **"Lord, have mercy"** thrice. The Prayer of the Entrance. Amen. After the Entrance: D.: Unto prayer. Priest: **"Peace be unto all."** D.: Unto prayer (*Ἐπὶ προσευχήν* / *Epi proseuchēn*). P.: **"Lord, have mercy."** Priest—the prayer (of the Trisagion) with an exclamation. *(Orig. p. 518)* P.: Amen. After the Gospel, the deacon [intoneth] a litany (?), the priest—a prayer for diverse (bodily) needs. After the Creed, the deacon says: Stand up for prayer (*στάθητε* / *stathete*). Priest: **"Peace be unto all."** D.: Pray for those who bring offerings. Priest—a prayer for them [^2097].

## The Litany in the Nestorian Liturgy

Still closer unto our litanies are the litanies (expressly so called there) in the later recensions (yet generally very ancient) of the Mesopotamian-Persian Nestorian liturgies, whose most ancient recensions possess nothing at all corresponding unto our litanies (as among other liturgies is true only of the Roman and the Liturgy of the Apostle Peter [^2098]). Thus, the liturgy of the Malabar Christians (the Indian Nestorians) has two litanies: one after the Trisagion before the reading, the other after the hallowing of the Gifts; the first corresponds unto our Great and Augmented Litanies, the second unto the Petitional. The first:

=== LEAF p620 ===
"Deacon: Let us all stand uprightly and with joy and vigilance let us ask and say: Our Lord, have mercy on us. People: Our Lord, have mercy on us (the same response unto each of the twelve proclamations of the deacon). 2. Father of mercies and God of all comfort, we beseech Thee. 3. Our Savior, and the Giver of our salvation, and the Leader of all things, we beseech Thee. 4. For the peace and unity of the whole world and the good estate of all the Churches, we beseech Thee. 5. For the temperate balance of the air and season, the abundance of the fruits [of the earth], and of every ornament, we beseech Thee. 6. For our holy fathers, our Patriarch, the pastor of the whole Catholic Church, and the Bishop, that they may enjoy sound health, we beseech Thee. 7. Merciful God, Who in His love governeth all things, we beseech Thee. 8. Rich in mercy and abundant in goodwill, we beseech Thee. 9. Good Being and Giver of all gifts, we beseech Thee. 10. Glorious in heaven and exalted upon earth, we beseech Thee. 12. Immortal Nature and Dweller in the brightest light, we beseech Thee: save all, O Christ, Lord our God, by Thy grace, and multiply in us peace and love, and have mercy on us."
Thereupon follow the petitions of the deacon without response of the people, seventeen in number, beginning at times with the words "Let us pray," at times "Let us remember," "Let us make commemoration"; "Let us recall," "Pray ye," "For such and such," unto all of which together the people respond: Amen. These petitions, of which the first is "Let us pray, peace be with us," contain a prayer for hearing and mercy, for the Church, its everlasting peace, for bishops, the Patriarch, presbyters, deacons, the entire assembly; further, the "commemoration" of the "Most Blessed Virgin Mary, Mother of Christ and the Savior," with a prayer that the Spirit Who dwelt in Her may hallow us also; the commemoration of prophets, apostles, martyrs, confessors, with a prayer to imitate them; the commemoration of the "fathers" Nestorius, Diodore, Theodore, Ephrem, Abraham, Narses, and all others, with a prayer for the preservation of their teaching in the Church; further, the commemoration of the departed; a prayer for the country and state, for those who have fallen away from the faith, *(Orig. p. 519)* for the ailing, sick, and demon-possessed, for poor orphans, widows, the unfortunate and persecuted; and an invitation unto especially fervent prayer ("cry out from the whole heart...") for our sanctification; and in conclusion, a glorification of the mercy of God (corresponding unto our exclamation, yet pronounced by the deacon) [^2099].
