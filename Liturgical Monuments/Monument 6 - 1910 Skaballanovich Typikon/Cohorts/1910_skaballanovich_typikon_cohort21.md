### Egyptian Cenobia According unto Blessed Jerome

As we see, the *Rules* attest a significant development of the principles laid down by St. Pachomius as the foundation of his coenobitic life, and struggle against that natural decline of ascetical life in monasteries which inevitably setteth in with their external expansion. Approximately the same order of life and the same phenomena were observed in other Egyptian cenobia, insofar as we may judge from: a) the accounts concerning them in the works of Blessed Jerome, and b) the detailed description thereof in St. John Cassian.

"The first condition," saith Blessed Jerome, who observed the Egyptian coenobites in the 380s, "among them is to obey the seniors. They are divided into tens and hundreds, so that over nine men the tenth is the leader; while the centenary hath under him ten deans (*decani*). They dwell separately, yet in interconnected cells. Until the ninth hour, as is appointed, no one goeth unto another, save perhaps unto the aforementioned deans; so that if anyone be troubled by thoughts, he receiveth counsel from them. After the ninth hour they come together, chant psalms, and read the Scriptures according unto custom. Upon the conclusion of the prayers, when all are seated, he whom they call father, standing in their midst, begins an instruction. During his discourse such silence reigneth that no one dareth look upon another, no one dareth even cough. Praise unto the speaker is expressed in the weeping of the hearers. Tears roll softly down their cheeks, and grief doth not break forth even into a groan. But when he begins to proclaim things to come, concerning the Kingdom of Christ, concerning future beatitude and glory, thou wilt see all with

=== LEAF p202 ===
a subdued sigh and eyes lifted up toward heaven, saying within themselves: **"Who will give me wings like a dove, and I will fly away and be at rest?"** (*Кто дастъ ми крилѣ, яко голубинѣ, и полещу и почию*, Ps. 54:7 [LXX; 55:6 MT]). Thereupon the assembly is concluded, and each decade with its senior goeth unto table, whereat by weekly turns they also serve. During the meal there is no noise whatsoever; no one speaketh. They feed upon bread and vegetables seasoned with salt alone. Wine is drunk only by the aged, for whom the meal is often shared with youths, in order to sustain the advanced age of the former and not retard the beginning fast of the latter. Then they rise together and, having chanted a hymn, disperse unto their dwellings; here until evening each talketh with his companions, saying: 'Have ye seen such-and-such an one? How much grace is in him, how much quietness? What a humble gait!' If they behold one who is weak, they comfort him; if one burning with love for God, they spur that brother onward unto ascetical labor. And since at night, over and above the common prayers, each still keepeth vigil in his sleeping chamber, they go around all the cells and, applying their ear, diligently ascertain what is taking place. If they notice anyone being slothful, they do not rebuke him; but pretending as though they know not, they visit him more frequently, and at first rather entreat than compel him to pray. The daily labors are so arranged: whatever is done under a dean is reported unto the steward, who himself also every month with great trembling rendereth account unto the common father. He likewise tasteth the food, how it hath been prepared; and since it is not permitted for anyone to say: 'I have neither undergarment nor outer cloak, nor a bed woven of reeds,' he arrangeth all things so that no one may lack what is needful for him. If anyone falleth *(Orig. p. 215)* sick, he is transferred unto a spacious chamber, and the elders minister unto him with such zeal that he doth not even think of urban comforts or a mother's care. Sundays are devoted solely unto prayer and reading; indeed, the coenobites do the very same at all times upon concluding their labors. Daily something is read from Scripture. Fasting is uniform throughout the entire year, save for the Holy Forty Days, during which alone it is appointed to live more strictly. From Pentecost onward, suppers are exchanged for midday dinners, so as thereby to preserve church tradition and not burden the stomach with a twofold taking of food." [^1079]

### Egyptian Cenobia According unto St. John Cassian

St. John Cassian († 435), for some time a monk of the Bethlehem monastery, undertook from thence a journey unto Egypt to become acquainted with the life of the hermits and monasteries there, spending the years 390–400 in those regions. After the pattern of the Egyptian monasteries he founded in Marseilles both a male and a female monastery, and in 417, at the request of Castor, Bishop of Apt, who had established a similar monastery in his own diocese, he described in his work *De Institutis Coenobiorum* (*On the Institutes of Cenobia*) the life of Egyptian cenobia, supplementing this information subsequently in the *Conferences of the Fathers* (*Collationes Patrum*). The structure of coenobitic life observed by St. Cassian in Egypt, however close it may be unto the Pachomian *Rules* in the recension of Blessed Jerome and unto the latter's observations, readily revealeth a further stage of development, a more precise definition of certain details, especially in regard unto prayer and divine worship. At the same time, Cassian makes us acquainted also with the practice of other contemporary monasteries, especially Western ones.

It is characteristic that St. Cassian begins his description of cenobia with monastic garments: such significance was already ascribed unto them. This description, beside detailed indications of the form of each garment—testifying that this form was already firmly established (close unto the present day)—speaketh also of the symbolic meaning of the vesture. The latter is noted also by Sozomen. [^1080] Concerning reception into a monastery, St. Cassian supplementeth the data of the Pachomian *Rules* with the following particulars: he who desireth to enter the monastery is kept for ten days before the gates, where he, "prostrating himself upon his knees before all passing brethren, being intentionally rebuffed and despised by all as one seeking to enter the monastery not out of piety, but out of necessity, being moreover assailed with insults and reproaches, affordeth proof of his perseverance." He who hath been accepted stands "in the midst of the assembly of the brethren, putteth off his own garments, and by the hands of the abba is clothed in monastic attire." [^1081] Penances, it would seem, were imposed more frequently and more strictly; the customary penance was: "when all the brethren are assembled for prayer, the offender, lying prostrate upon the earth, entreateth forgiveness and remaineth in his position until the service is concluded and *(Orig. p. 216)* the abba biddeth him rise from the floor." [^1082] The weekly turn of service (*hebdomada*), saith Cassian, not only in Egypt but throughout the entire East in monasteries, endeth upon Sunday evening following the psalmody appointed before retiring unto sleep. [^1083] Concerning the refectory, St. Cassian noteth the distinction of Egyptian cenobia from others in that here the strictest silence was observed at table, whereas in other places there had been adopted the custom—which St. Cassian deriveth from Cappadocia (from St. Basil the Great?)—of reading at table "something sacred." [^1084]

### Divine Worship in Egyptian Cenobia According unto Cassian

=== LEAF p204 ===
Yet, as observed, prayer and divine worship receive in the description of St. Cassian the most elaborate practice by comparison with the *Rules* of St. Pachomius, being accorded also the greatest amount of space in his work (two books out of four). In this regard St. Cassian noteth first of all a great diversity and mutability in the practice (*diversos typos ac regulas*) of diverse monasteries—doubtless pre-eminently Western ones—unto which he, by way of reproach unto them, contrasteth the uniformity and antiquity of the Egyptian practice of psalmody and prayer. Pausing first upon the "night prayers and psalmody" (unto which the entire second book of *De Institutis Coenobiorum* is devoted), St. Cassian saith that "among some it is appointed to recite (*dici*) twenty or thirty psalms, and that antiphonally and unto diverse melodies (*et hos ipsos antiphonarum protelatos melodiis et adjunctione quarundam modulationum*); others have attempted to surpass even this number; among some there are eighteen psalms; thus in diverse places we see a diverse rule (*canonem*) established. Unto others also, in regard unto the daytime offices of prayer (*orationum officiis*)—that is, the third, sixth, and ninth hours—it seemed that in performing within them obsequies unto the Lord (*in quibus haec Domino redduntur obsequia*), the number of psalms and prayers ought to correspond unto the number of the hour, whereas others appoint six psalms unto each daytime assembly (*conventibus*). Therefore I consider it necessary to bring forward the ancient patristic ordinance which unto this day is observed by the servants of God throughout all Egypt." [^1085] "Throughout all Egypt and the Thebaid... a single, lawful rule (*modum*) of prayer is kept at evening assemblies or nocturnal vigils." [^1086] "Both at evening and at nocturnal solemnities (*solemnitatibus*) they chant twelve psalms, yet so that they are followed each time by two readings, one from the Old and one from the New Testament. This order, established in antiquity, hath remained inviolable through so many centuries across all the monasteries of those regions for this reason: that, as the elders affirm, it was established not by human contrivance, but was brought from on high unto the fathers by an Angel." [^1087] "Once the Egyptian fathers *(Orig. p. 217)* assembled to determine what measure of psalmody in the daily worship ought to be observed by the entire body of the brotherhood... In this gathering each, according unto his own zeal and mindful not of the infirmity of others, proposed to enact as law what he deemed easily practicable; each thought to establish a vast number of psalms, some forty, some sixty, while others, not content even with this number,

=== LEAF p205 ===
deemed that even more than that should be appointed. While there was thus among them a holy disagreement of pious disputation concerning the liturgical rule (*religionis regula*), the hour of the evening office overtook them in the midst of this most sacred investigation. In this service, when they desired to perform the daily order of prayers, someone arose, stepped forth into the midst, and began to chant (*cantaturum*) psalms before the Lord. All, sitting—as is the custom in the lands of Egypt unto this day—listened... And he, having chanted eleven psalms in an even recitation (*parili pronunciatione cantasset*) without interruption, verse after verse, dividing them only with intermediate prayers, and concluding with the twelfth with the refrain Alleluia (*sub alleluia responsione*), suddenly vanished from before the eyes of all, and thereby brought an end unto the service (*caeremoniis*), as well as unto the dispute." [^1088] "Therefore the venerable council of the fathers... decreed to observe this number in assemblies both evening and nocturnal, annexing unto them two readings, one from the Old and the other from the New Testament. This they added as their own tradition, as it were of supererogation (*extraordinarias*), only for those who desire it. On Saturday and Sunday, however, both readings are taken from the New Testament, one from the Apostle and the other from the Gospel, which they do likewise throughout all the days of Pentecost." [^1089] "The aforesaid prayers they begin and perform in such wise that, having finished a psalm, they do not immediately bend the knees, as do some among us in this country... But before bending the knees, they pray for a brief space, and spend more time upon this standing (than upon the kneeling). After this, having fallen prostrate for a very brief moment, as though only to render adoration unto the Divine Goodness, they rise very swiftly and, lifting up their hands on high, again plunge more intensely into prayer, in the same manner as they prayed while standing previously. [^1090] And that which we behold in this country—namely, that a psalm should be chanted by one alone, and at its end all standing should chant aloud (*concinant cum clamore*): **"Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit"**—nowhere have we seen throughout the entire East; but there, amidst universal silence, by him who chanteth the psalm, upon its conclusion, prayer is begun. With this same glorification of the Trinity an antiphon is there customarily concluded." [^1091] Prayer "they prescribe to conclude with a swift end (*celeri fine concludi*), lest by lingering upon it an influx of saliva or phlegm should interrupt

=== LEAF p206 ===
the course (*excessum*) of our prayer... For this reason they account it more profitable there to make prayers brief, yet as frequent as possible. [^1092] For this reason also the very psalms which *(Orig. p. 218)* they chant (*decantant*) in the assembly, they perform not without intermission, but divide them into two or three sections (*intercisionibus*), according unto the number of verses, with intermediate prayers... And this likewise is strictly observed among them: that with the response of Alleluia only those psalms are recited (*dicatur*) which have Alleluia in their title. The chanting of the twelve specified psalms they divide among themselves in such wise that if there be two brethren, they chant six psalms each; if three, four each; if four, three each. Therefore, however great a multitude of brethren be gathered in church, never are more than four brethren appointed to chant in the assembly. [^1093] This instituted number of twelve psalms they perform in a restful posture of body, so that when those customary offices are celebrated in assemblies (*congregationum solemnitates*), with the exception of him who riseth up to recite the psalms in the midst, all sit upon low benches (*sedilibus humilibus*)..., for by fasts and day-and-night labors they so exhaust their body that, were it not for this relief, they would not be able to hear even this number of psalms while standing... When the canonical psalmody appointed by rule (*orationum canonicarum functiones*) is ended, each, returning unto his cell—wherein it is permitted to dwell alone or with one other...—again zealously performeth the debt of prayer, as it were his own private sacrifice; while to sleep no one any longer permitteth himself, until with the appearance of daylight, following upon nocturnal work and meditation, daily labor succeedeth." [^1094]

St. Cassian noteth also the exactitude with which the hours of nocturnal services were observed in Egyptian cenobia: "Although daily habit causeth one to awaken at an appointed hour, he unto whom is entrusted the summoning unto liturgical assemblies or the care of the offices, nevertheless diligently and frequently observeth the course of the stars, and not otherwise than having accurately determined the time appointed for the assembly calleth the brethren unto the work of prayer." [^1095]

As regards the daytime offices—that is, the third, sixth, and ninth hours—they were not celebrated in the Egyptian cenobia of St. Cassian's time.

"These services, which the semantron (*klepalo*) compelleth (*admonitione compulsoris*) us to celebrate at appointed hours with

certain intervals, are performed among them spontaneously (*spontanee celebrantur*) without interruption throughout the entire course of the day together with handiwork. For being constantly occupied by day with handiwork in their cells, yet in such wise that they never omit meditation upon the psalms or other Scriptures, and at every moment mingling therewith prayers and supplications, they spend the whole time of the day, as it turns out, in prayer, whereas we perform them at appointed hours. Therefore, with the exception of evening and nocturnal assemblies (*congregationibus*), there is among them by day no common service (*publica solemnitas*), save on Saturday and Sunday, whereon at the third hour they assemble for the reception of Holy Communion." [^1096]

*(Orig. p. 219)*
Thus St. Cassian affordeth a complete picture of Egyptian coenobitic worship of his time, the entire order thereof. In what measure it differed from the divine worship of the time of St. Pachomius and his successors, it is difficult to judge in view of the incompleteness wherewith the latter is described in the *Rule* of St. Pachomius (from the Angel) and his *Rules*. Proximity in time (several decades separate that practice from this one) doth not yet guarantee complete identity between the two, because, as St. Cassian himself giveth to understand, that was a time of rapid and energetic creation of specifically monastic divine worship. It is characteristic that in St. Pachomius the essence of worship is placed in "prayers," whereas in St. Cassian it is placed in "psalms." Remarkable also is the strong hesitation that occurred regarding the number of psalms for a service: the tendency toward the greatest possible number of them (sixty and more)—clearly presupposing the eremitic practice of chanting the entire Psalter at one sitting—confronteth the Pachomian principle of moderation here, as in all things (in fasting and labor), and the number twelve requireth a special new heavenly sanction. Remarkable, furthermore, is how everything elaborated hitherto by the secular churches is eliminated from monastic worship: not only litanies and prayers of fixed models—which was natural, since for them a member of the hierarchy was considered necessary [^1097]—but also psalms adapted unto the hour of worship: for Vespers Psalm 140, for Matins Psalm 62; [^1098] at divine worship the Psalter was chanted simply in sequence, and unto this the entire service was reduced. [^1099] Thereby the foundation was laid for the later *kathismata*.

### The Attitude of Egyptian Monasticism toward Church Hymnody

With such a character of divine worship, there could not possibly correspond unto it that already abundant hymnographic material which in the IV–V centuries continuously and vigorously grew across all churches, especially such

=== LEAF p208 ===
centers of cultured Christianity as Jerusalem, Alexandria, and Constantinople. The following incidents illustrate the attitude of contemporary monasticism toward this element of divine worship. According unto a *Patericon* of one recension and the narrative of Nikon of the Black Mountain, Abba Pambo († after 386), a Nitrian hermit, sent his disciple unto Alexandria; having no shelter there, the latter spent the night in the narthex of the Church of St. Mark and, listening unto the church services, became fascinated with the troparia. When he returned unto the elder, the latter perceived confusion in him and inquired as unto the cause. The disciple saith: 'Carelessly, father, do we spend our life in the desert, knowing neither canons [^1100] nor troparia.' *(Orig. p. 220)* The elder answered him: 'Woe unto us, child! The days are near when monks will abandon the solid food spoken by the Holy Spirit (Scripture, and among other things the psalms) and will take to songs and musical tones (*ᾄσματα καὶ ἤχους*). What compunction or tears can come from troparia? What compunction will a monk have when, standing in church or cell, he lifteth up his voice like an ox? If we stand before God, we ought to stand in great compunction, not in mockery. Monks did not depart from the world to mock before God, to melodize (*μελῳδοῦσι*) songs, to modulate (*ῥυθμίζουσι*) tones, to wave their hands, and stamp their feet; but we ought with much fear and trembling, with tears and sighs, with reverence and compunction, in a meek and humble voice to offer prayers unto God. But I say unto thee, child, that days will come when Christians will corrupt the books of the Holy Gospels and the holy Apostles, composing troparia and Hellenic discourses.' [^1101] A similar judgment is placed upon the lips of another, somewhat later, Egyptian hermit. When the Persians devastated Cappadocia at the end of the IV and beginning of the V century, a monk from thence named Paul departed unto Constantinople, then unto Alexandria, and finally settled in the Nitrian desert with a certain hermit. But soon he appeared before the hegumen of the monastery and requested of him a separate cell, since he could not dwell with the elder, who observed no office (*ἀκολουθίαν*) and no order, neither monastic nor secular. Having mentioned the elder's deviations from Cappadocian customs regarding feasts and fasts, the monk addeth: 'Yet what is worst of all, he doth not permit the chanting of canons and troparia, which constituteth the customary psalmody among all.' The hegumen, persuading the monk to return unto the elder, remarketh: 'As for the chanting of troparia and canons and the employment of musical melodies (*καὶ ἤхους μελίζειν*), this is suitable for secular priests and worldly people, to attract the folk into churches; but for monks who dwell far from worldly clamor, such things are not profitable, but often harmful; for as an angler catcheth fish with a hook and worm, so the devil by the bait of troparia and singing casteth one into the pit of vainglory, human inventions, thirst for pleasure, and finally fornication; in truth, far away from the monk who desireth to be saved let all melodious chanting be!' [^1102] However negative the attitude of monasticism toward secular worship appeareth in these narratives, nevertheless they contain

=== LEAF p209 ===
both a clear indication that such an attitude obtained solely in Egyptian monasteries situated under the direct influence of the hermits, and a glimmer of awareness that such a state of affairs could not be enduring.

Indeed, no sooner did monasticism step beyond the borders of Egypt than this attitude substantially changed, and monastic divine worship acquired a form closer unto that of the world. Such a reconciliation took place first, it would seem, in Palestine.

### Palestinian Monasteries. St. Hilarion

Unto Palestine ascetical life was transplanted from Egypt by a disciple of St. Anthony the Great, St. Hilarion († 381). A native of the environs of Gaza in southern Palestine, which was in constant communication with Egypt, Hilarion, while studying under a grammarian in Alexandria, beheld there Anthony who was already renowned, [^1103] dwelt several years with him, yet at fifteen years of age (in 306) returned unto his homeland, having received from St. Anthony as a blessing his leather girdle, [^1104] and settled in the desert not far from Gaza. His dwelling was a small hut resembling rather a tomb than a house [^1105]—of such dimensions that standing one had to bow one's head, and lying down to bend one's legs; [^1106] he nourished himself at first upon the roots of certain shrubs and wild herbs, partaking of food after three or four days, but later began to use bread and cooked vegetables, even with oil; never did he eat prior unto sunset. [^1107] In ascetical feats St. Hilarion surpassed even Anthony the Great: he possessed not two cloaks, but one, and the like. His prayer consisted chiefly of the chanting of psalms and the reading of Scripture, which he knew by heart. [^1108] After twenty-two years of solitude, the healing wrought by St. Hilarion upon the children of the Praetorian Prefect of the East began to draw crowds unto him, and around his cell there arose (ca. 328) a monastery of anchoritic type, entirely reminiscent of the Antonian monasteries; the cells stretched out like the tents of nomadic tribes across the entire expanse of the desert bordering upon Egypt. These St. Hilarion visited periodically accompanied by other monks (at times up unto two thousand), in order to bless the monks and impart unto them instructions for their life (*τύπον τῆς πολιτείας*). [^1109] After thirty years, a foreboding of Julian's persecution moved St. Hilarion to withdraw first into Egypt and then unto Cyprus, where he was ordained unto the priesthood by his disciple St. Epiphanius of Cyprus, and there died; his body was translated unto his monastery. During St. Hilarion's absence his monastery suffered devastation at the hands of the pagans, [^1110] from which it could not recover, and in the V century it is mentioned no more. [^1111] This was the sole purely anchoritic monastery in Palestine, and its fate demonstrated that Palestine was destined for a different type of monasticism than Egypt.

### The Lavras of St. Chariton

=== LEAF p210 ===
The foundation of the new type of monastery was laid by St. Chariton († in the second half of the IV century) the Confessor (doubtless in the last persecutions), former *(Orig. p. 222)* primate (*τά πρῶτα φέρων*) of the Church of Iconium. He founded a "lavra" (*λαύρα*, properly a narrow passage, alley) in the natural caves of a desolate mountain near Jerusalem, where hermits had lived even before him; in this monastery he arranged first of all (with money left behind by the robbers from whom he had been miraculously delivered) a church, consecrated by Macarius, Patriarch of Jerusalem. The structure of life in this "lavra," which received the name of Pharan, must have represented something intermediate between the liberty of anchoritic settlements and the detailed regulation of life in cenobia. [^1112] Desiring solitude, St. Chariton nevertheless soon left his lavra, having first given unto his disciples rules ("commanding all that is proper unto the monastic rank") regarding food, psalmody, and other aspects of life. Among these rules are known: counsel unto the monks to eat only once a day in the evening—bread, salt, and water; [^1113] the requirement of unceasing residence in the monastery, and hospitality; the governance of the monastery St. Chariton committed unto one of the monks with the consent of all. Yet at the new place of residence of St. Chariton (one day's journey from Pharan in the direction of Jericho near the Mountain of Temptation) there soon arose another lavra, which received the name of the Jerichoan Lavra (Douka). At the next place of St. Chariton's solitude, two hours south of Bethlehem, arose a third lavra, named the Souka or Old Lavra, which existed until the end of the VIII century. Before his death St. Chariton gave unto the assembled brethren "sacred commandments" (a typikon?). [^1114]

### The Lavra of St. Euthymius and the Coenobium of St. Theoctistus

The Palestinian monasteries, multiplying so rapidly, were not so isolated from one another as the Egyptian: in 428 we find at the head of them all a single "archimandrite," [^1115] Passarion the Great, appointed by the Patriarch of Jerusalem. Yet a closer unification of Palestinian monasteries became possible when among them arose a monastery which occupied naturally and spontaneously the foremost place among them. That was the Lavra of St. Euthymius the Great († 473). An Armenian by birth, broadly educated, St. Euthymius already in his native land in the rank of presbyter had oversight over several monasteries, but from the cares connected therewith departed in the twenty-ninth year of his life in 406 unto Palestine, entering at first the Lavra of Pharan.
