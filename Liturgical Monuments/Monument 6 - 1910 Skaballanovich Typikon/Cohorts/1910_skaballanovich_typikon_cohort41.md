manuscripts of the *Hypotyposis* (*Hypotyposis*, §§ 28, 29; *Diatyposis*, §§ 14–18). This chapter of the *Hypotyposis*, although it entered into the Studite-Alexis Typikon in a softened form, provoked great disputes in Russia during the 12th–13th centuries in view of its discrepancy with the ancient canons and the Jerusalem Typikon (see below). In both monuments, comportment at the refectory table is defined in agreement: "When the brethren assemble to dine (*Diatyposis*: 'at the sound of the bell,' *kodonos*), in their mouths must be a verse" (a prayerful verse, according unto later monuments: **"Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on us"**); according unto the *Diatyposis*, likewise: "and when they rise from the table, until such time as, having departed into the narthex, they offer thanksgiving for that which they have eaten"; they sit at the tables nine men each (*Diatyposis*), according unto the direction of the overseer watching that there be no vacant places; they sit at the refectory in hoods (*koukoulia*; *Hypotyposis*); there is also a reading, "which (*Diatyposis*) is directed by the ecclesiarch, who takes care that whatever they did not manage to read in church should be read at the refectory and nothing should be omitted"; "the signal for the conclusion of the reading is the clatter of spoons cast simultaneously into the bowls at the final dish; likewise for the pouring of wine and the serving of courses a signal is given by means of a strike" (*Diatyposis*: by a bell; *Hypotyposis*, § 28; *Diatyposis*, § 14). ### The Disciplinary Portion of the Holy Mountain Typikon

The Great Synaxarion of the Athonite Iviron Lavra of the 11th century (see above, p. 372, n. 4), the exemplar of Holy Mountain Typika, in its disciplinary portion represents an abridgment and slight modification of the *Diatyposis*; from it are retained here the sections concerning the hour of services, food, proportion in services, non-acquisitiveness, the order of leaving the monastery, imprisonment, clothing, and non-working days. The hours of service are appointed for Matins: throughout the year, weekday Matins is at the 8th hour (2:00 AM; an hour later than in the *Diatyposis*); on Sundays at the beginning of the 7th hour, and festal Matins at the beginning of the 5th hour (slightly later than in the *Diatyposis*); during the Twelve Holy Days [from the Nativity of Christ unto Theophany], Cheesefare Week, and the second days of the Great Feasts of the Lord, toward the beginning of the 9th hour (around 2:00 AM); in Great Lent, toward the beginning of the 6th hour (around 11:00 PM). Concerning fasts, the dispensation of the *Diatyposis* for cheese during Pentecost and for feasts occurring during multi-day fasts is abrogated, and this dispensation is preserved for feasts falling upon ordinary Wednesdays and Fridays. Unto those occupied with heavy labors during a fast, food is given at the 6th hour (not at the 3rd). Monks are forbidden to acquire money (concerning furniture nothing is said). Of the penances, imprisonment is preserved with the observation: "punishment by blows or other

means appropriate in the world is condemned by our fathers, although in case of extreme necessity it is permissible to resort unto them also." In the event of a desire to leave the monastery, "the hegu-*(Orig. p. 423)*-men shall entrust the departing brother unto another hegumen, or undertake whatever other measures." Feast days are divided into non-working days for all, including laborers, and non-working days only for the brethren; unto the former, besides the Twelve Great Feasts, belong September 13 and 26; November 8 and 14; December 6, 9, and 26; January 1; April 23; May 8 and 24 (Symeon the Wonderworker); June 24 and 29; August 29; unto the latter: September 20; October 18, 23, and 26; November 1 and 13; December 4, 13, and 27; January 17, 20, and 25; February 17 and 24; March 9; June 8; July 1, 8, 20, and 27; August 31; the small number of 'secular' feasts (for the laborers) the *Synaxarion* explains by the small number of laborers in the monastery (only four) and their high wages [^1806].

### The Disciplinary Portion of the Studite-Alexis Typikon

Upon the foundation of the *Hypotyposis*, the disciplinary portion was elaborated in the Studite-Alexis Typikon (11th–12th centuries), which was for so long the regulator of monastic life in Russia. But the primary source here underwent greater modifications than, for example, in the Iviron *Synaxarion*, and was significantly expanded: out of the 281 folios of the most ancient manuscript of this Typikon, the disciplinary portion occupies more than one third: 85 folios. Later manuscripts leave this portion of the Typikon unaltered. Of the officials of the *Hypotyposis*, taxiarchs and overseers are not mentioned; their duties, apparently, are transferred unto epistemonarchs, whose title is translated in Slavic as *khitretsyanachalnika* [^1807]; there were two of them (fol. 215). The first person after the hegumen was the oeconomus (*ikonom*); but in church, if the oeconomus lacked holy orders, the senior among the priests was considered first after the hegumen (fol. 266v). Next follow such 'servitors' (*sluzhbnitsi*): the *kutnik*—chief superintendent over the refectory (that which under St. Theodore was the cellarer and refectorian); "those who are senior on both sides of the church," that is, the two *khitretsyanachalnika*, although they (according unto another passage of the Typikon) are placed in church immediately after the hegumen and oeconomus (fol. 266); "the paramonarios—sacristan (*ssudokhranilnik*); the granary key-bearer; he who attends unto the sick (infirmarian); he who awakens (the awakener?) [^1808]; the church domestikos (= canonarch); the keeper of books; the cellarer (supervisor of clothing?), and the senior hospitaller." The hegumen discharges (and consequently appoints) all monastic officials (fols. 225v, 226). Obedience unto the hegumen must be the same as unto a military commander. Before speaking with him, or catching sight of him, they bow "greatly" (*"Monastic commandments of those living in a cenobium, that is to say, living together,"* fol. 216v). One of the chief duties of the hegumen is to instruct the monks more frequently, both all together and each individually (*"Concerning this, that monks should be frequently catechized,"* fol. 223). Financial affairs

=== LEAF p403 ===
of the monastery the hegumen is advised to conduct with the knowledge and mediation of other monastic officials; money is to be kept in a chest sealed by him, the oeconomus, and the *kutnik* (fol. 226v). The order for the election of a hegumen is indicated: after the death of a hegumen, the brethren, "having performed common prayer and fasting for three days, fervently entreat the Godhead to appoint one who is worthy"; "thereafter, with the Holy Gospel lying before them, they elect whom they know"; the elected candidate must be not younger than 33 years of age, from among the tonsured monks of the monastery, and not those who en-*(Orig. p. 425)*-tered from another monastery, so as not to grieve the local brethren by the necessity "to be pastured by an outsider," and lest the hegumen destroy "the rule of our divine fathers Elias and Theodore"; it is also necessary to elect one who is "by no means partaker of marital intercourse" (neither a widower nor one who has abandoned a wife), lest "love for kindred" distract him from the monastery; it is recommended to elect from among the "consecrated" [priests] or "great schema-monks"; if the election is not unanimous, they elect three candidates and present them unto the Patriarch (*"Concerning hegumens,"* fols. 238v–241). Regarding the spirit of monastic governance itself, the founder wishes, as we have seen, that the principles of St. Theodore be observed here, which is why at the conclusion of the Typikon there is appended a translation (unfinished) of the Penitential of St. Theodore under the title *"Concerning the remnants of church canons"* (fol. 280). The Typikon devotes much space unto admission into the monastery. The establishment of the monastery is fixed at 40–50 or more monks, "besides those who are in the villages" (*"Concerning the number of monks,"* fol. 231v). Unknown persons must be tested for a year before admission; those from other monasteries are to be received only with good recommendations; priests and deacons from other monasteries are admitted unto celebrating only after three months upon learning all the customs of the monastery. It is forbidden to demand from incoming applicants a contribution unto the monastery (*"Concerning priests and deacons,"* fols. 232–234v). Those who make large contributions dare not claim any privileges in the monastery (*"Concerning offerings,"* fol. 234); to receive someone into the monastery upon such conditions is worse than contracting a loan. In case of a deficiency of funds in the monastery, it is better to pawn something or borrow from the patriarchate or its monastery (*"Concerning renunciation,"* fols. 235v–237). In connection with this, the Typikon likewise forbids burying anyone in the monastery, "even if he should give something on that account"; at this, the founder-patriarch observes that even for himself he prepared a tomb in the monastery not by his own will, but at the request of the monks; he himself had the desire to be buried in the Studite monastery, where as a youth he received tonsure and labored until the patriarchate itself (*"Concerning this, that no one should be buried in the monastery,"* fol. 237v). The founder wishes to make the monastery, after the model of the great ancient ones, as isolated from the world as possible: leaving the monastery is permitted only with the hegumen's permission; visitors are received initially only in the hospice, and are reported unto the hegumen for further directions. To oversee this, at the outer 'hospice' gates of the monastery there is one monk, and at the inner gates three, "from men aged, chaste, and

=== LEAF p404 ===
borne witness by all"; they may alternate in attending divine services, and at their post it is recommended to read the Psalter and other soul-profiting books. Women are unconditionally forbidden entry into the monastery; permission is granted only unto the empress with a single maidservant; while for the ladies of her court, "as many of the women as follow after her," it is permitted to enter the monastery only through the "bath-house" (the bath or refuse) gates into the small church; and it is forbidden for all to meet the empress, but only the hegumen, oeconomus, protopresbyter, paramonarios, and other elders (*"Concerning hospices,"* fols. 229–231). During Great Lent, however, from Vespers of Cheesefare Sunday until Friday of the sixth week, the monastery was locked unconditionally, except in case of deliveries or dispatching someone, for which purpose *(Orig. p. 426)* they open the rear gates (*"How it is proper to lock the monastery during holy Great Lent,"* fol. 215v) [^1809]. With the aim of greater isolation from the world, the monastery was required to maintain within itself (as was practiced also by ancient monasteries of high ascetic discipline) such necessary institutions as an infirmary with a physician and pharmacy, and a special hospice for elderly monks, placed under the charge of eight elders, where the means for its maintenance by the monastery in food, firewood, oil, and candles are defined in detail; into this hospice Christ-loving laymen were also received at their own expense, where it is likewise determined in detail for what payment is taken from them—not forgetting payment for soap in the bath-house and "the washing of their garments by one of the neighboring women" (*"Concerning the infirm and the aged,"* fols. 241–244). The means of sustenance the monastery was obliged to acquire for itself chiefly through the labors of the monks; therefore no one in the monastery is exempt from manual labors, not even the hegumen. In particular, kneading dough was also performed by all in rotation, for which purpose the brethren are divided into three 'ranks' (*chiny*): the first is called ("styled") the rank of the hegumen, the second of the oeconomus, and the third of the *kutnik*; each of these supervisors had assistants: the hegumen the *kutnik*, the oeconomus the sub-oeconomus, and the *kutnik* the sub-*kutnik*; alongside them, one of the brethren (a specialist?) was placed at the head of each rank; his duty included calling to the kneading. Following the Six Psalms at the beginning of the kathismata, this brother, having naturally prepared everything in the bakehouse, entered the church and, having made a bow in the center, approached first on the right side of the temple, then on the left, each brother of his rank, and with a bow said: **"Lord, bless, unto the bakehouse"** (*Gospodi blagoslovi v peklnitsyu*); those summoned in this manner assembled before the Royal Doors and, together with that brother, at the conclusion of the first antiphon, approached the hegumen and bowed unto him to the ground; the caller said: **"Bless. Pray for us, holy father"**; the latter replied: **"God save you"**; in the bakehouse, part of them kneaded the dough, while part chanted "the Six Psalms, one canon, and the remainder of Matins"; upon conclusion of the work they return unto the church and chant with the rest, "and the first manages according to the readiness of the dough"; prosphora were kneaded by a single baker (*"Concerning kneading,"* fols. 227v–228). "Just as kneading the dough is established for the brethren themselves, so also burdens brought into the monastery from without, such as firewood or wine and the like, they

The Typikon exhor-*(Orig. p. 427)*-ts the monks to perform all commissions given them, especially oversight of labors outside the monastery (where work must have been executed chiefly by hired hands under the supervision of monks), with constant thought of the unslumbering eye and to fear, as the sin of Ananias and Sapphira or as sacrilege, any dishonesty (*"Concerning those sent upon obediences,"* fol. 219). In free time they may not assemble for conversations, nor enter the cell of another even under the pretext of study. Those wishing to study assemble for this in the 'refectory' after Matins. The hegumen takes care that each monk know the Psalter by heart and chant it in church according to the "living book of the soul," and not search for a book here and there (*"That it is not fitting for monks to congregate with one another,"* fols. 220–221). In connection with the external life of the monastery, the Typikon speaks *"Concerning monastic vesture"* (fols. 223v–224) in greater detail than the present Typikon (see ch. 39 of the Typikon), and concerning the bath-house: they go to it by tickets ("seals") from the hegumen, apparently once a month (the place is abraded in the manuscript), and the sick according unto the direction of the physician (fol. 225). Extensive sections of the Typikon under the headings: *"Chapters of the church rule, similar unto the first rule-book (that is, the rule concerning food) of various chapters extending into church service"* (fols. 245v–249), *"Concerning this, with what great attentiveness monks must stand at divine doxologies"* (fols. 249–250), *"Concerning the standing of monks in church"* (fol. 253), interspersed with purely liturgical paragraphs (concerning how to chant the triadika [Trinity hymns], concerning the number of kathismata at Matins, concerning the chanting of kathismata on Saturday, concerning the chanting of **"God of gods"** [Ps. 49:1], that is, the second antiphon of the 7th kathisma, and **"Praise the Lord"** [Ps. 148], that is, the third antiphon of the 20th kathisma, with special alleluiaria, concerning the refrains unto the Psalms of Praise and **"Lord, I have cried"**), these sections are dedicated unto the comportment of monks in church. Upon the arrival of the hour, the awakener (*ubuzhayay*) rises and goes first of all unto the cell of the hegumen, bows unto the ground, and says outside the door: **"Bless and pray for me, holy father"**; the latter replies from within: **"God save thee"**; then the awakener, still by the cell, proclaims very loudly: **"Bless, holy fathers"** (*Blagoslovite svyatii*), and repeats this likewise loudly, passing by all the cells. ### The Disciplinary Portion of Ancient Founder Typika

Not a little space is allotted unto the disciplinary side of monastic life by all the other extant founder (*ktitoral*) Typika as well. The majority of them even have exclusively such content, referring for liturgical practice unto other Typika (see above, pp. 362–363). But these Typika exerted no influence upon our present Typikon: not one of them is older than the Studite-Alexis Typikon, which was preserved in an immutable form in Russia until the introduction of the Jerusalem Typikon. Nevertheless, a comparison of these Typika with the Studite-Alexis Typikon can demonstrate the distinctive character of the latter and explain why precisely unto this Typikon fell such a broad and enduring sphere of operation. In none of the currently known founder Typika does the administrative section touch unto such a degree upon all aspects of monastic life as in the Studite-Alexis Typikon. They occupy themselves more with the auxiliary institutions of the monastery—hospitals, hospices—than with the internal life of the monastery. At times, noticeable deviations from the counsels of asceticism are also made. Thus, for example, the organization

=== LEAF p408 ===
of the Pantokrator Monastery in Constantinople resembles rather the staff of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople than the great lavras of antiquity; here are the same ecclesiastical ranks, including even four women who clean the temple and correspond unto the 'myrrh-bearers' of Hagia Sophia and *(Orig. p. 430)* of the Church of the Resurrection [^1815]; menial labors fall upon four permanent persons, and not upon the whole brotherhood in rotation [^1816]. Remarkable is the peculiarity in the election of the hegumen presented by certain of these Typika: they elected three candidates and determined the final choice by lot, wherein parchments with the names of those elected were placed upon the holy table and prayer was offered that God might reveal the one who was worthy; a priest, an illiterate monk, or a boy drew the parchment from the holy table [^1817]. Remarkable likewise is the requirement of certain of these Typika, for example the Evergetis Typikon, that the Typikon should be read at the beginning of each month at the refectory.

### The Typikon of Empress Irene in Disciplinary Regard

Among these Typika, the Typikon of Empress Irene deserves attention as the first (and almost unique) experience of a female monastic rule. The difference from male rules consists in: a lighter fasting regimen: on Wednesday and Friday usually oil; daily not two, but two or three dishes; yet of wine not two or three cups, but one, with permission also for an addition at the discretion of the abbess; all sleep in one or two rooms, so that "all might be visible unto all"; meeting with male relatives is permitted only at the monastery gates in the presence of the abbess or a most honorable nun; no exception is made even for sick nuns, who are carried unto the gates upon stretchers. Priests must be monks and eunuchs. They likewise serve as oeconomoi of the monastery in view of the inconvenience for nuns of external relations outside the monastery. The officials are the same as under St. Theodore with minor additions and modifications: there is a 'granary mistress,' a 'wine-pourer,' two 'docheiariai' (one for financial accounting, the other supervising clothing), and two 'ergodotriai'—labor-givers, that is, supervisors of handiwork [^1818].
*(Orig. p. 431)*

### The Disciplinary Portion in the Jerusalem Typikon According to Georgian and Ancient Greek Manuscripts

In contrast unto the Studite Typika, the ancient Jerusalem Typika possess a very small disciplinary portion. In the most ancient manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon, for example, the Georgian Shiomghvime MS of the 13th century and the Greek MS of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum, Sevastyanov collection No. 35/491 of the 13th century, the disciplinary sections are inserted here and there among the liturgical material into the first general part of the Typikon, preceding the Menologion and

Thus the disciplinary portion proper in the mentioned two copies of the Jerusalem Typikon possesses the following uncomplicated composition. At the end of the exposition of the weekday service, such observations are made: "It is necessary to know that the monk appointed to chant the prokeimenon, following its intonation, bows unto the holy doors, then turns toward the west and stands with hands crossed upon his chest until the brethren recite the last verse; thereafter he bows and goes unto his place. He likewise intones **\"Let every breath\"** and the kontakion after the 6th ode of the canon. The Typikon returns again unto discipline following the exposition of the service of the Hours and Mid-Hours: after the First Hour and its Mid-Hour, "those wishing to be saved do not leave the cell until the time of the church bell tolling (*chrousmatos*), but some occupy themselves with handiwork, others with reading, and others with psalmody with the knowledge of the superior; while the servers (*diakonetai*) attend unto (*scholazousi*) their service. It should be known that when we are present at church offices, the ecclesiarch directs (*tassei*) the right choir, while the epistemonarch directs the left." Next is cited the chapter concerning the awakener in the recension of the present Typikon (cf. the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis*) [^1821]. Finally, and this is already for the third time, the most ancient manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon touch upon monastic discipline at the end of their first general part, before the Menologion; here an observation is made that at Compline "at Psalm 50 the candle-lighter (according unto the Georgian MS, the canonarch) gives water unto the brethren"—this in view of the fact that after Compline, as the last prayer of the day, partaking of any food is already forbidden on account of the morrow's Liturgy (on days of Vigils it is forbidden to drink water after partaking of the blessed bread and wine at Vespers); furthermore, it is required right here to conclude Compline before dark (*epiouses hemeras*, "while daylight yet remains"); after Compline "it is not permitted for monks to engage in conversations (*syntychias*) with one another, but each departs unto his cell, to occupy himself a little with the Trisagion which we have received (evidently, the cell rule, as is explicitly stated in the Georgian MS) and with reading, so that with attentiveness they may rise for Nocturns and the remaining office and church assembly (*synaxei*)" [^1822].
