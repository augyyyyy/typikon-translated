=== LEAF p71 ===
Clement of Alexandria, rising up in his *Paedagogus* against immoderation at the agapes, first of all elucidates their profound internal meaning and significance. It is "a beautiful, saving institution of the Logos... The agapes are in truth celestial food, a banquet of the Word (*hestiasis logikē*, ἑστίασις λογικὴ)... And if thou lovest the Lord thy God and thy neighbor, then this festive and blessed celebration, having the properties of the heavenly, taketh place already as it were in the heavens... The agapes are a pure undertaking worthy of God, and their purpose is mutual assistance. The joys of the suppers of love, owing unto the participation in them of many, act upon love in a revivifying manner; they are a foretaste of eternal joy... That one must participate in the agapes in precisely this manner is revealed from the fact that herein we partake of the food (*brōsis*, βρῶσις) of Christ" [^361]. These passages, seemingly, speak in favor of the fact that the Eucharist continued to be joined with the agape [^362].

At the same time, the learned Alexandrian hath left us important information concerning the external aspect of the agapes as well. He provideth nearly a complete list of the dishes customary at the agapes. "Certain persons dare," saith Clement, "with insolent tongue to bestow the name of agapes upon certain petty feasts (*deipnaria*, δειπνάρια), reeking of roasted fat (*knissēs*, κνίσσης) and broths (*zōmōn*, ζωμῶν), reducing them unto wine, pots, and soups." "The most grievous of all falls is when unfading love (*agapē*, ἀγάπη), this inhabitant and child of heaven, is cast down from heaven unto the earth into soups." In contrast unto such a composition of the meal at the agape, Clement requireth for it the simplest dishes: "onions [or bulbs] (*bolboi*, βολβοὶ—perhaps also in a generic sense: garden plants with bulbous heads), olives (*elaiai*, ἐλαῖαι), various greens (*lachanōn enia*, λαχάνων ἔνια), milk, cheese, and likewise fruits (*ta te hōraia*, τὰ τε ὡραῖα), various boiled fruit decoctions (*hepsēmada*, ἐψήμαδα) [^363]—without broths; if, however, it be necessary, boiled meat (*kreōs*, κρέως) may be added." Under meat *(Orig. p. 76)* Clement understandeth fish (otherwise he would contradict his own previous rebukes), since he immediately citeth the Gospel narrative of how the disciples offered fish unto the risen Savior [^364]. "Furthermore, those dining with the Logos cannot deprive themselves of genuine dessert (*tragēmatōn*, τραγημάτων). In a word, of dishes at the agape there ought to be the most necessary, which may be consumed at once without fire, as well as those which are most readily at hand (*epei kai hetoimotera*, ἐπεὶ καὶ ἑτοιμότερα), and then also cheaper." Recalling the passage in Proverbs: "Better is a dish of greens (*lachanon*, λάχανον) with love, than a fatted calf with hatred" [^365], Clement qualifieth this by noting that he doth not consider this simplicity of dishes to be anything essential in the agape, but rather—love: "greens (*lachanon*, λάχανον) [^366] are not the agape, but meals (*deipna*, δεῖπνα) received with love" [^367]. In one passage Clement seemingly calleth the agapes *potos* [πότος] (in an unblameworthy sense) and speaketh of "drinking together" (*sumpinomen*, συμπίνομεν) at them with "hymning and glorifying God" [^368].

In rich and effeminate Alexandria, as is evident, they attempted to surround the agapes with special splendor and turn them into veritable luxurious banquets. Such figures as Clement of Alexandria energetically

=== LEAF p72 ===
contended against these tendencies and openly declared agapes with meat dishes to be ordinary suppers. They demanded the observance of the former strict practice regarding meals at the agapes—a practice indications whereof we encountered in the 2nd century, that is, the consumption at the agapes of exclusively plant-based, and in extreme cases fish, food. Here, consequently, is the germ of the later fasting diet in the Christian Church. At the same time, Clement's inventory of dishes at the agape explaineth unto us why at present at Vespers during the blessing of the loaves (Litiya/artoklasia), which is undoubtedly a ritual remnant of the agape, there is blessed not bread alone, but wine, wheat, and oil, although the latter two products are not consumed in church and generally remain unused. In antiquity these products went toward the meal of the agape.

### The Agape according to Tertullian

Unto the relative luxury of the agapes Tertullian likewise pointeth, whose testimony can speak of the practice of the Roman and Carthaginian Churches, as Clement's doth of Alexandria. Tertullian, moreover, giveth a valuable indication concerning the order and rite of the agape. "Our supper (*coena*)," saith Tertullian, "fully justifieth its name *agapē* [ἀγάπη], which is employed by the Greeks to signify love (*dilectio*). Whatever our suppers may cost, our outlay is incurred in the name of piety on behalf of the indigent. They recline at table (*discumbitur*) not before they have tasted (*praegustetur*) of prayer unto God; they eat as much as is needed to satisfy hunger; and they drink as much as is profitable for the modest; *(Orig. p. 77)* they satisfy themselves as those who remember that even during the night (*per noctem*) they must pray unto God; they converse (*fabulantur*) knowing that the Lord heareth all. After the washing of hands (*post aquam manualem*) and the lighting of lamps (*et lumina*), each person is called forth into the midst to sing (*canere*) unto God either from Holy Scripture or from his own mind [or genius] (*ingenio*). From this it is already evident how each hath drunk. The supper endeth, as it began, with prayer. Then they depart as though they had conducted not a supper, but a lesson of moral discipline (*disciplinam*)" [^369]. Tertullian, as we see, could still boast before the pagans of complete good order at the agapes, which the Apostle Paul had so greatly desired and which in Alexandria and other cities had begun to decline once more. The agape presenteth in Tertullian a moving picture of eating unto the glory of God, with constant thought of God. However, Tertullian, as an apologist, might somewhat idealize. At the same time, the agape in Tertullian already receiveth a rather complex rite, with details that are not mentioned in the 2nd century. It is preceded, seemingly, by a prayer of no small duration. After the meal followed the washing of hands, which in this epoch was a customary rite before prayer [^370] (continuing to be preserved among us even now in the order of the Liturgy); consequently, the prayer portion of the agape began properly after the eating thereof. Inasmuch as the agape was celebrated in the evening, this prayer

=== LEAF p73 ===
portion thereof fell upon twilight and night, for which reason the necessity for lamps arose. The example of Old Testament divine worship, as we have seen above [^371], could impart a special sacred (sacramental) significance unto the lighting of lamps at the agape, which other monuments of the 3rd century likewise note [^372].

The spiritual, prayerful side of the agape, according unto Tertullian's testimony, bore a hymnographic character, wherein nearly each of those present took part in singing by turn. The songs employed were as much biblical as of Christian composition, seemingly even improvised. (Even now Vespers is among us a more hymnographic service than Matins). Tertullian saith nothing concerning the Eucharist either in this description of the agape or in his preceding description of the morning service, naturally because this was not required by the aims of his apology.

### The Agape according to Minucius Felix and Origen

Among other writers of that period, *Minucius Felix*, a younger contemporary of Tertullian, mentioneth the agapes: "Our assemblies," saith a Christian unto a pagan in his work *Octavius*, "are distinguished not only by chastity, but also by solemnity; at them we do not surrender ourselves unto surfeiting with food, we do not sweeten the feast with wine; mirth itself we temper *(Orig. p. 78)* with gravity, chaste discourse, and yet more chaste bodily demeanor" [^373].

*Origen* mentioneth only memorial agapes. "When their memory (of the departed) is celebrated, we summon the pious together with the priests, and equally with the clergy we entertain the faithful; herein we feed the poor and indigent, widows and orphans, so that our festivity may serve for the remembrance and repose of the soul whose memory is celebrated, and for us before the face of God may be a sweet-smelling savor" [^374]. The last words point unto the sacrificial character of these memorial agapes. Consequently, in Alexandria in the middle of the 3rd century the agape is still accompanied by the Eucharist. Not so in Carthage.

### The Agape according to Cyprian

In *St. Cyprian of Carthage* we find for the first time an indication of the separation of the Eucharist from the agape and its transferral unto the morning. Demonstrating the insufficiency for the Eucharist of water alone without wine against the custom of certain heretics (Ebionites, Marcionites, Encratites, etc.), St. Cyprian saith: "Some, perchance, beguile themselves with the thought that, although in the morning water alone is offered, yet in the evening, when we come unto the supper, we offer the mixed cup. But at the time of supper we cannot call together all the people, so as to celebrate the genuine reality of the mystery (*sacramenti veritatem*) in the presence of the whole brotherhood. True it is that the Lord offered the mixed cup not in the morning, but after supper. Is it therefore

=== LEAF p74 ===
the case that we also ought to celebrate the Lord's supper (*dominicum*—others translate: 'the Lord's day') after supper, and offer the mingled cup unto those who are present at the assembly? But it behoved Christ to offer His sacrifice at evening, so that by the very time He might demonstrate the setting and evening of the world... We, however, celebrate the Resurrection of the Lord in the morning" [^375]. The reason for celebrating the Eucharist in the morning is thus purely external—physical inconvenience and the impossibility for the majority of the people of evening assemblies.

The agape is described by St. Cyprian, seemingly, in the following passage: "And as it is now a feast day and a season of leisure, let us pass all the remainder of the day, when the sun is tending toward the west, in joy; but suffer not thyself at table for even a single minute to remain without thanksgiving unto God. Let it resound with sober psalmody; and since thou hast a good memory and a tuneful voice, begin this singing thyself according unto custom. Ye wish to provide for your best friends the best entertainment; but if we listen unto spiritual songs and attend unto them spiritually, we shall find a pious gratification for the bodily ear" [^376]. As in Tertullian and Clement, the agape according unto this testimony is filled with singing (the germ of the hymnographic sequence). Perchance reading and prayer had departed from it altogether along with the Eucharist.

*(Orig. p. 79)*
A similar picture of the agapes is afforded likewise by the *Sibylline Oracles*, the citation whereof in the *Apostolic Constitutions* [^377] giveth right to assign them unto the 3rd century: "With bountiful agapes and broadly bestowing hands, with sweet psalms and God-pleasing aspirations unto Thee, the Immortal, we cry aloud and glorify Thee, the unfailing God and Father of all" [^378]. Here is an indication of the sacrificial and charitable character of the agapes, of the abundance of dishes at them (cf. Clement of Alexandria), of the high inspiration which they instilled, and of the predominance of hymnody, even as it were an allusion unto the hymn "O Gladsome Radiance" (*Phōs Hilaron*).

### The Agape according to the Testamentum Domini

Of the mentioned canonical monuments of the 3rd century, the agape is described by the *Testamentum Domini*, the *Canons of Hippolytus*, and the *Egyptian Canons*; the latter in both of their recensions—the Coptic (edited by De Lagarde) and the Ethiopic (edited by Ludolf)—not completely identically.

The *Testamentum* speaketh less than the others concerning the agape and presenteth the simplest picture thereof [^379]. In it regarding the agape there are given several particular directives with obscure terminology, presupposing its customary practice to be well known.

"At a dinner or meal they receive" (it is understood, presumably: "the fraction," the bread sanctified by prayer and fraction—in such manner as is described in detail in the *Canons of

=== LEAF p75 ===
Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*, see below) "those" (understood: first) "who are closest unto the pastor—likewise also regarding the blessing" (according unto Rahmani: *ad benedictionem*; presumably the cup with wine) [^380]. "The catechumen, however, receiveth not. Whosoever is an intimate or kinsman of a teacher of secular subjects" (pagan?), "is not admitted unto praise, and it is unlawful to eat with him merely on account of kinship and intimacy, lest he surrender unto the wolf that which is unutterable, and bring condemnation upon himself."

There follow next instructions for those who are invited with the bishop "into the house of a faithful man" (unto an invited agape), regarding moderation in food, drink, and in general concerning blameless behavior befitting "saints," so as not to grieve the host; it is advised to eat in such manner that there be remnants for those unto whom the host may wish to send them; not to take food before the elders; not to contend in conversations, but to eat in silence; one may speak only in response unto a question from the bishop; the speech of the latter must not be accompanied by tokens of applause [^381].

This rite of the agape the *Testamentum* giveth after the description of the Paschal service; but in expounding the rite, the author presupposeth its celebration at any time, and only most immediately hath Pascha in view; moreover, this description of the agape is preceded and concluded by directives regarding the Paschal service, *(Orig. p. 80)* which we shall utilize later. The relationship of the Eucharist unto the agape is unclear from the *Testamentum*. Yet judging from the fact that the *Testamentum* provideth a distinct order of the Liturgy with a separate portion for the catechumens and setteth forth this order after the morning service separately from the agapes, the monument clearly presupposeth a celebration of the Eucharist entirely separate from the agape.

### The Agape according to the Canons of Hippolytus and the Egyptian Canons

While being very brief and general in its description of the ordinary agape, the *Testamentum* dwelleth with affection upon the description of the Paschal agape, in no way giving it to be understood that such an agape—as we shall see, with the solemn lighting of lamps, with the singing of psalms and hymns alternating with prayers, with lengthy readings and homily, with the solemn dismissal of the faithful homeward and the continuation of the service throughout the entire night for certain persons—is celebrated at any other time than Pascha [^382]. The *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*, on the contrary, saying nothing specifically concerning the Paschal agape, transfer much of the composition and ceremonies of the Paschal agape of the *Testamentum* unto the ordinary, or at least Sunday, agape. Furthermore, they depict more distinctly and clearly the central ceremony of the agape—the fraction of bread. According unto these monuments, the agape is likewise a service entirely distinct from the Eucharist. At least,

=== LEAF p76 ===
it can be celebrated even without the Eucharist: this the monuments give to be understood by presupposing that the celebration of the agape is possible by the laity alone without the clergy [^383]. The agape is celebrated necessarily with the Eucharist only on Sunday and at memorials [^384]. In the latter case it is noted: "If there be an *anamnēsis* [ἀνάμνησις, memorial] for the departed, before they sit down (unto the meal of the agape), they receive the Mysteries; yet not on the first day" [^385] (not on Sunday, or on the first day after death?); thus the Eucharist in such a case was celebrated prior unto the agape; concerning the Sunday agape the very same thing seemingly is stated in the expression: "Obligatory" (an Arabic word translated by the editors into Latin as *necessaria*) "for the poor (for whom an agape is arranged by someone on the Lord's day) is the *eucharistia* [εὐχαριστία], which taketh place at the beginning of the liturgy" (an Arabic word translated by the editors into Latin as *missa*). As the essential rite of the agape there is considered the fraction of bread, which is performed even without the clergy, and consequently without the Eucharist. If a bishop be present at the agape, he offereth prayer over the bread, blesseth it (*signet*), and distributeth it (*distribuet*) among the participants [^386]; in the absence of a bishop a presbyter doth the same [^387]; "in the absence of a presbyter a deacon taketh his place in that which concerneth the prayer and fraction of bread, which he distributeth unto the guests; but unto a layman it is not fitting to bless the bread, but let him only *(Orig. p. 81)* break it, doing nothing besides this" [^388]. The bread having received such a blessing (or perchance fraction alone—the latter is unclear) is called the bread of blessing, *eulogia* [εὐλογία], or bread of exorcism, *exorkismou* [ἐξορκισμοῦ] [^389], as being purified (*purgatus*) by prayer. Unto this bread, as its very name already showeth, there is ascribed a special sanctity and miraculous efficacy, evidently a power against evil spirits (here is the primary source of the note in Chapter 2 of our Typikon concerning the virtue of the blessed loaves); in particular, the bread of exorcism is eaten at the beginning of the agape, "that God may preserve the agapes from the dread of the evil one, and that they may be safely exalted in peace" [^390]. Unto the catechumens, who are not admitted unto the agapes, there is nevertheless sent, by direction of the bishop [^391], the bread of exorcism [^392] (as also the cup) [^393], "that they may be joined unto the Church" [^394]. This primary rite of the agape—the fraction of bread with the blessing thereof and of the cup, and the partaking of them—is preceded in the *Canons of Hippolytus* by those ceremonies wherewith the *Testamentum* surroundeth only the Paschal agape: "If there be an agape or a supper be arranged by someone for the poor on the Lord's day (*kyriakē*, κυριακῇ), at the time of the lighting of the lamp, in the presence of the bishop the deacon ariseth for the lighting. The bishop then prayeth over them (the poor, participants of the agape) and over him who invited them (the host of the agape). And he dismisseth them, that they may go their ways before the falling of darkness. They read psalms before they disperse" [^395]. In the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* there are given prescriptions similar unto the *Testamentum* concerning good order at the table of the agape, and its order concludeth with instructions regarding that

=== LEAF p77 ===
which the widows (deaconesses) ought to do after the agape, recalling the instructions in the *Testamentum* for widows and virgins at the Paschal vigil, yet recalling them by their complete antithesis. There the widows remain after the Paschal agape for all-night prayer; here, according unto the *Canons of Hippolytus*: "If anyone desire to prepare for the widows [^396] a meal, let him take care that they have their meal and be dismissed before the sun goeth down [^397]. If, however, there be many of them, let him beware lest there be disorder, and let him not hinder them from dispersing before evening. Let each of them be given sufficient food and drink. But let them depart before night falleth" [^398]. Here those very widows who alone with the clergy and virgins celebrate in the *Testamentum* the Paschal *(Orig. p. 82)* all-night vigil after the agape are solicitously sent away unto their homes at the onset of evening, wherein the reason for such a requirement is likewise stated: disorders that arose, evidently, owing unto the former practice. There are yet in these monuments several new remarks regarding the agapes, in comparison with the *Testamentum*, characteristic of their history. Thus the monuments clearly give to be understood that intemperance and noisy behavior at the agape not infrequently "grieved" the benefactor—the host thereof [^399]—and for the prevention of these disorders the *Canons of Hippolytus* advise requesting the host of the agape to be present at it with his entire family [^400].

If the agape was celebrated together with the Eucharist, the latter preceded it. This all the canonical monuments under consideration clearly give to be understood by the fact that they require the reception of the Eucharist prior unto other food. The *Canons of Hippolytus*, however, require this especially on fast days [^401]. The *Testamentum*, on the other hand, speaketh of this, as we shall see, in connection with the Paschal service: "Let the faithful man ever strive to partake of the Eucharist prior unto the taking of food; then he shall unknowingly escape affliction" [^402]. This latter promise for the eating of the Eucharist prior unto food the *Egyptian Canons* give in a more concrete form: "Whosoever receiveth (the Eucharist) with faith, even if something deadly be given unto him, it shall not harm him" [^403]. The tone of these requirements showeth that the custom of such reception of the Eucharist was still recent and demanded special insistence for its firm establishment.

### Firstfruits as Material for the Agapes

Toward the provision of the agapes went firstfruits, which the faithful brought unto the clergy from their fruits and revenues. Such an ancient monument as the *Testamentum* placeth these firstfruits in dependence upon the good will of each person: "Whosoever desireth to bring fruits or the firstfruits of the harvest, let him bring them unto the bishop"; the bishop then blesses that which is brought and reads over it a special prayer; herein a remark is made: "Vegetables are not blessed, but the fruits of trees, and flowers—roses and lilies" [^404].

=== LEAF p78 ===
The *Egyptian Canons* and the *Canons of Hippolytus* already counsel bringing firstfruits, but principally for the blessing thereof by the bishop—the former less resolutely: "Let them endeavor to bring at all times unto the bishop the firstfruits," and the *Canons of Hippolytus*: "Whosoever hath firstfruits..., let him bring them" [^405]. As in the *Testamentum*, over the brought firstfruits there is read by the bishop (*Canons of Hippolytus*) or priest a special prayer, wherein thanksgiving is offered unto God for the fruits of the earth, and the blessing of God is invoked upon them and upon the bringer [^406]. In all three monuments no remark is made *(Orig. p. 83)* regarding the destination of these offerings; yet that they went, among other things, toward the agape, we shall see presently from the *Didascalia*. The catalog of offerings is fuller in the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* than in the *Testamentum*. According unto the *Canons of Hippolytus*, besides tree fruits there are brought "the firstfruits of the garden (contrary unto the *Testamentum*) and of vineyards, oil, honey, wool, and the firstfruits of wages for labor" [^407]. According unto the *Egyptian Canons* it is yet more definite: "grapes, fig, apple, olive, pear, pomegranate, peach, cherry, almond"; herein it is remarked: "There are not blessed: lotus [^408], onion, garlic, melon, watermelon, cucumber, and other vegetables; if flowers be brought, rose and lily are accepted; of the rest permit not to accept; but for all that is eaten let them render thanksgiving and eat, glorifying God" [^409]. Behold the primary source of the present blessing of grapes and apples on the day of the Transfiguration. These firstfruits the *Didascalia* already converteth into direct tributes, increasing their amount, or at least accurately defining this amount: "Bring (O Church) tributes and tithes and firstfruits unto Christ, the true High Priest, and unto His Servants, as the tithe of salvation... When (the bishop) shall receive them, he will divide them equitably, because the bishop well knoweth those in need... The pastor's portion must be set apart and given unto him according unto custom, even if he be not present at the agape and at the gifts" [^410]. Consequently, a portion of the offerings went toward the provision of the (charitable) agape, while the remainder was divided between the clergy and the poor. The *Didascalia* accurately defineth what portion of the offerings each cleric ought to receive: a widow receiveth one portion, a deacon two, and the presiding hierarch two double portions. Concerning these distributions of offerings the *Apostolic Constitutions* likewise speak in almost the identical expressions as the *Didascalia*, adding that the cantor and the doorkeeper likewise receive one portion. These monuments, consequently, require firstfruits no longer for the blessing thereof, but for the maintenance of the clergy and in part (*Didascalia*) for the agapes, thereby establishing the decline of the institution of agapes.

### Matins according to Tertullian

All the more, therefore, in the 3rd century was a purely "verbal," "noetic" [spiritual] ministry unto God destined to advance forward at the expense of the agape. And inasmuch as the evening of the day was occupied by the divine worship of the agape, a ministry of this kind and character

=== LEAF p79 ===
became attached unto the morning and developed into a rather prolonged morning divine service. With the decline of the agape, the morning divine service was bound to acquire an ever greater significance and predominance over the evening service. But inasmuch as the first half of the 3rd century was, so to say, the era of the greatest flourishing of the agape (writers of this first half of the 3rd century, and even canonical monuments of the end of the century, furnish the richest ritual for it), in the 3rd century the morning divine service was only just beginning to acquire such a position for itself, and only toward the end of this *(Orig. p. 84)* century did it already occupy in certain churches at least the first place after the Liturgy.

From the monuments of the 3rd century we possess only two accounts concerning the morning divine service. In the first account, belonging unto Tertullian, it is remarkable first of all that the writer, compelled for apologetic purposes to touch upon the divine worship of Christians, not only speaketh first of the morning service, but likewise assigneth unto it no less space than unto the agape. Nothing other than the morning service can be meant by the following words of Tertullian, immediately preceding his testimony concerning the agapes cited above:

"We form," saith Tertullian, "gatherings and assemblies (*coetus et congregationes*), so as to encircle God with common prayers, as it were with an army assembled into one place by hand (*manu facta*). And this force is acceptable unto God. We pray for the emperor, his servants (*ministris*), his authorities (*potestatibus*), for the postponement of the end of this age (*pro mora finis*) [^411]. We assemble for the reading (*commemoratione*) of the divine scriptures, if the circumstances of the times require admonition or reminder. With these sacred words we nourish our faith, animate our hope, support our confidence, even as we reinforce discipline by the inculcation of precepts (*disciplinam praeceptorum nihilominus inculcationibus densamus*); here also are divine exhortations (*exhortationes*), reprimands (*castigationes*), and censure (*censura*): this judgment proceedeth (*nam et judicatur*) with great solemnity (*pondere*), as among those assured of the presence of God; this is a supreme anticipation (*summum praejudicium*) of the future judgment, so that if anyone hath transgressed, he is banished from communication with us in prayer, assemblies (*conventus*), divine worship, and in all sacred intercourse (*commercii*)" [^412].

Not devoid of significance in this testimony is already the dual designation of the worship assemblies of Christians as *coetus* and *congregationes*. The latter, presumably, were more populous and solemn, and it is possible that by them Tertullian understandeth the agapes described by him

=== LEAF p80 ===
further on, and by *coetus* in such a case the morning worship assemblies. The latter, according unto Tertullian, bear predominantly a prayerful-homiletic character in distinction from the agapes described further on with their sacramental-hymnographic character: whereas at the agapes the chief element was the meal and singing [^413], the morning divine service was composed of prayers, the reading of Holy Scripture, and homily. The constituent petitions of the prayer represent the germ of the present litanies, in particular the Great Litany (Litany of Peace). In the highest degree remarkable is the mention of a certain "judgment" during divine worship—perchance the excommunication of unworthy members after pub-*(Orig. p. 85)*-lic penance; this peculiar detail of divine worship somewhat recalleth Pliny's *sacramento se obstringere* [^414].

### Matins according to the Testamentum Domini

Among other monuments of the 3rd century, information concerning the morning divine service is found only in the *Testamentum of Our Lord Jesus Christ*. Here there is already given a complete and rather complex rite of Matins. Concerning the morning divine service under the title of "laudation at dawn" (*collaudatio aurorae*), the monument speaketh immediately after the Liturgy; whereas concerning the agape the monument speaketh, as we have seen, only in the order of the Paschal service. Consequently, by the epoch of the *Testamentum* (the end of the 3rd century) Matins had already assumed its present honorable position in the circle of daily services. It was the sole public service besides the Liturgy. All other worship gatherings at special, prayer hours of the day—the number of which, as we shall see, significantly increased in the 3rd century in comparison with the 2nd century—bore a private, non-obligatory character. Such, presumably, the agape had become as well.

#### Episcopal Matins of the Testamentum Domini

"At first dawn," prescribeth the *Testamentum*, "the bishop gathereth the people to celebrate the service until the very rising of the sun. For the first laudation at dawn, he, surrounded by the standing presbyters, deacons, and the rest (of the clergy), saith: **"Glory be to the Lord."** The people: **"It is meet and right."**"

Then in the monument there is placed with the heading "Glorification of the Dawn" (or laudation at dawn) a prayer for reading by the bishop, beginning with the words: **"It is meet and right for us to praise Thee, and to exalt Thee, and to confess unto Thee, O ineffable God, Who hast created all things."** In it morning praise is offered unto God, all-wise and most merciful,
