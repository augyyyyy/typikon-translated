containing a lectionary compiled, in all likelihood, c. 650, and a martyrology of 925–1000. The lectionary is an important monument of the ancient Spanish rite, distinct both from the Roman and from the South-Spanish Mozarabic: there is the practice of catechumenate; on the Saturdays of the Forty Days [Great Lent] there is no fasting; the Sundays after Theophany and after Pentecost are not indicated; the Apostle James is absent (in Spain!); the Annunciation is on December 28, in accordance with canon 1 of the Tenth Council of Toledo (656); the 'Crushing' (*allisio*) of the Holy Innocents is on January 8 (not December 27 or 29); the Feast of the Holy Cross is on May 3 (not September 14). The martyrology, although later than the lectionary, bears the imprint of great antiquity. Beside certain names the title is not 'saint' (*sanctus*) but *domnus* (4 of them Toledan); of feasts of the Theotokos there are only the Annunciation and the Dormition (*assumptio*) on August 15 [^1549].
 
#### d) The Constantinopolitan Martyrology of the Eighth Century

Of the same local type is the most ancient Constantinopolitan 'Menologion of the Festal Gospels of the Whole Year' (*Μηνολόγιον τῶν εὐαγγελίων ἑορταστικῶν*) in a Greek codex of Gospel *(Orig. p. 323)* lections. In favor of its Constantinopolitan provenance speaks the mention of local sites, local commemorations (May 11 [Foundation of Constantinople] and October 26 [Great Earthquake]), and 29 patriarchs from Metrophanes (306–326) unto Paul the New (either Paul III, 686–693, or Paul IV, 780–784). This last commemoration indicates likewise the date of compilation; that it took place immediately following the first Iconoclastic persecution is shown by the absence of commemorations of the champions of the holy icons of this era as being very recent, even Patriarch Germanus († 740), John of Damascus, and others. The menologion contains commemorations for 143 days, for the most part 2, rarely 2–3; in February there are 6 commemorations, in March and April 4 each (Lent). After Constantinopolitan saints, the most numerous are those of Asia Minor; the most renowned patriarchs and saints of the entire East are present, even Anastasius the Persian († 625, January 22); popes are absent; of Western saints there are only Laurence, Gervasius, and Protasius. The commemorations almost all fall on our present dates (only the Holy Innocents on December 31) and diverge from Roman dates. For the first time appear SS. Constantine and Helen on May 21; Justinian and Theodora likewise occur on November 14 (the day of Justinian's death), but without the title 'saint'. There are no Constantinopolitan bishops prior unto Metrophanes. For the Annunciation, the Dormition, and St. John Chrysostom (November 11) a *pannychis* (*παννυχίς*) is appointed; on the eves of the Nativity of Christ and Theophany—a *paramone* (*παραμονή* [vigil / eve]) [^1550].

In the 6th–8th centuries, similarly unto the Greek and Roman, the calendars of the Nestorians, Armenians, Copts, and Abyssinians, who had separated from the Eastern Church, likewise assumed nearly their present form.
 
### The Nestorian Church Year

The Nestorian church year possesses only a few immovable feasts; consequently, the church year is determined by Pascha unto a greater degree than among us. The beginning of the year among the Nestorians is reckoned from the Nativity of Christ, properly speaking from the 4 preparatory Sundays preceding it (as among the Roman

=== LEAF p312 ===
Catholics), which are called Annunciation Sundays (the Annunciation is celebrated during these Sundays, whereas under March 25 there is merely a calendar notice of the event), and the New Year is celebrated on the first of these 4 Sundays. The feast of the Nativity of Christ is the most solemn and constitutes with Theophany a special festal period of 12 days (as among us). Commemorations of saints among the Nestorians, in order to avoid coinciding with Sundays and feasts, are assigned unto the Fridays of almost the entire year: the first Friday after the Nativity of Christ is dedicated unto the Holy and Blessed Lady Mary (cf. among us the Synaxis of the Most Holy Theotokos); the 1st Friday after Theophany—unto John the Forerunner (preceded by a three-day fast); the 2nd—unto the Apostles Peter and Paul; the 3rd—unto the 4 Evangelists; the 4th—unto the Protomartyr Stephen; the 5th—unto the three Greek Doctors: Diodore, Theodore, and Nestorius; the 6th—unto the three Syriac Doctors: Ephrem, Narsai, and Abraham; the 7th—unto the patron saint of the church; the 8th—unto Adam *(Orig. p. 324)* and all the departed. After the Sundays of Epiphany, of which there may be 5 to 8 (if fewer than 8, then on Fridays two commemorations are combined), follow the Sundays of Great Lent, three weeks before which the Nestorians observe the Fast of the Ninevites (on Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday of our Week of the Publican and Pharisee, which for this reason is made fast-free among us). After Pentecost follow the 7 Weeks of the Apostles; on the Friday of the 3rd of these weeks occurs the 'Feast of God' or 'Thou Shalt Wash Me' (named after a special ceremony) in honor of the Holy Trinity (while Pentecost is solely in honor of the Descent of the Holy Spirit; cf. among Roman Catholics the Feast of the Holy Trinity on the 1st Sunday after Pentecost). Following the 7 'Weeks of the Apostles' come the 7 'Weeks of Summer', after them the 7 'Weeks of the Prophet Elias', 1 to 3 (the number depending on the Paschal cycle) 'Weeks of St. Moses', and 4 Weeks of the 'Dedication of the Church' or 'Entrance into the Temple', so named because on the first Fridays of each cycle one of these three commemorations is celebrated. The year thus always comprises 42 weeks or 364 days; the days lacking to complete the full solar year are supplemented by an intercalary week every 7 years. The immovable feasts are: the Transfiguration of the Lord on August 6; the 'Passing of the Virgin' on August 15; the 'Nativity of the Lady Mary' on September 8; the 'Finding of the Cross' on September 13; the Holy Martyr George on April 24 (if it coincides with Bright Week, the Friday of Renewal Week, or Sundays, it is transferred unto the 1st Friday after the Dormition). Thus of our Twelve Great Feasts only the Entry into the Temple is absent (as well as the Conception). Every Wednesday is dedicated unto the Most Holy Virgin, Friday unto the Sufferings of Christ, and Saturday unto the Veneration of the Cross. The services of the diverse days of the year possess very little in common with one another: not only doth each Sunday have a proper service, but almost every day of the week as well; in total there are approximately 220 services [^1551].
 
### The Armenian Church Year

The principal peculiarity of the Armenian menologion in comparison with the Greek is that the majority of commemorations in it are movable. Specifically, the Transfiguration is celebrated on the 7th Sunday after Pentecost and together with

=== LEAF p313 ===
the Sunday of Arachavorats (corresponding unto our Sunday of the Publican and Pharisee) forms the Paschal cycle of the year (Pascha falling, as among us, between March 22 and April 25), consisting of 24 weeks; for the remaining 28 weeks the reckoning is by the feasts and fasts that occur in this period: first come the Sundays after Transfiguration, then the Sundays after the Assumption [Dormition], after the Exaltation, then after the Fast of Hisnakats (the 'Pentecost' Fast, beginning on the Sunday nearest November 18), and after the Nativity of Christ or Theophany. The Assumption and the Exaltation are celebrated on the Sundays nearest unto our dates for these feasts. The remaining feasts are immovable: the Nativity of Christ and Theophany [Baptism] on January 6; the Meeting of the Lord [Hypapante] on February 14; the Annunciation on April 7; the Nativity of the Most Holy Theotokos, the Entry into the Temple, and the Conception, as among us; feasts in honor of the Savior, the Cross (apart from the Exaltation: the Finding and the Apparition), the Church (the Dedication of the Cathedral of Etchmiadzin), and the Theotokos (apart from our Twelve Great Feasts: the Conception, the Deposition of the Robe, and of the Cincture) are accounted dominical (*derunakan*), possessing *(Orig. p. 325)* a forefeast or vigil and an 8-day afterfeast; the afterfeast is almost equal in solemnity unto the feast itself and unto Sunday, so that there are 140 'dominical' days in the year (on all of which marriages are prohibited). The feasts of the saints are all movable, because they must not be celebrated on dominical days or during fasts; during the course of the week, therefore, saints may be celebrated only on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Saturday; during multi-day fasts—only on Saturday; throughout the entire period of Pentecost saints are not celebrated at all, with the exception of the commemoration of the Beheading of the Forerunner on Holy Saturday (at the Liturgy only; the Nativity of John the Baptist is celebrated in January) and the commemoration of Elias on Trinity Sunday [^1552]. The most important commemorations of saints—St. Elias, the Apostle James, and Gregory the Illuminator of Armenia—are preceded by fasts. There occur such commemorations as: the Creation of the World on Great Monday; the Destruction of Sodom on Great Wednesday; and the Old Testament Ark and the New Testament Church on the eve of the Transfiguration [^1553].
 
### The Copto-Abyssinian Church Year

The Coptic and Abyssinian menologia, closely related to one another, diverge considerably from the Greek and Latin both in their present form and in antiquity [^1554]. Their Monophysite character is discernible in that they contain, and that several times a year, the commemoration of Severus, Patriarch of Antioch, as well as of other Monophysite teachers. Their most sharp and conspicuous distinction from the Orthodox and Western calendars is a different system of chronology, by virtue of which all Julian and Gregorian calendar dates fall upon different days of the Coptic and Abyssinian months. The latest of the Orthodox saints in the Copto-Abyssinian calendars are Symeon the Stylite († 596) and Thomas, Patriarch of Constantinople (601–611). Yet of the ancient saints of the Greek Church many are absent from the Coptic calendars: absent, for example, in the most ancient of them are Ignatius the God-Bearer, John Chrysostom, and Ephrem the Syrian (whereas in

=== LEAF p314 ===
the later ones they are present). The Twelve Great Feasts are all present as among us, with only the Entry into the Temple lacking (among the Copts). In the most ancient Coptic calendar the 'Feast of the Theotokos' occurs on the 21st day of the following months: January, March, May, July, and October; in another calendar—on the 21st day of every month; the Nativity of the Theotokos on April 26 and September 7; certain Apostles (for instance, both Jameses) likewise occur twice a year; the Archangel Michael 8 times a year; the Martyr or 'Apostle' Thecla 5 times. This peculiarity of the Coptic calendar is developed still further by the Abyssinian, which repeats the principal commemorations every month: thus on the 12th day (= the 8th according unto our reckoning) of each month occurs the commemoration of the Archangel Michael; on the 19th—of the Archangel Gabriel and the other incorporeal powers; on the 21st—of the Mother of God *(Orig. p. 326)*; on the 28th—of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; on the 27th—the Crucifixion ('Salvation of the World'); on the 29th—the Nativity of Christ. There are commemorations of the Angels Raphael, Uriel, Raguel, and of the following Old Testament personages: Adam and Eve, Noah, Samson, Solomon, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Susanna 'the Pure', Pilate and his wife; the 4 Apocalyptic Living Creatures and the 24 Elders. The following events are likewise celebrated: the Translation of Enoch unto heaven, the Entrance of Noah into the Ark and his Departure therefrom, the Standing Still of the Sun by Joshua the Son of Nun, the Annunciation unto Joachim concerning the Birth of the Virgin, the Purification of Anna on the 80th day after the Birth of the Most Holy Virgin, the Journey of Jesus Christ into Egypt, the Flight of Jesus Christ from Mehs to Qosqam in Egypt, the Miracle in Cana of Galilee, the Imprisonment of John the Baptist, the Translation of the Head of John the Baptist, the Synaxis of the Apostles with the Savior, and the Second Repose of Lazarus [^1555].
 
### The Cycle of the Principal ("Twelve Great") Feasts

A review of the menologia of the 6th–8th centuries hath demonstrated that the church year both of the East and of the West had already formed down unto the minutest details of its present aspect; it remained for it subsequently only to receive into itself later saints. The natural consequence of the multiplication of commemorations—with which, in all likelihood, all the days of the year were now everywhere occupied (as in the most ancient of the menologia of this era, the Pseudo-Hieronymian; while in the Constantinopolitan of the 8th–9th centuries half the days of the year are occupied, because there not only commemorations but also Gospel lections are indicated)—was the necessity of establishing a certain gradation in the celebration of these commemorations, the need to render certain of them, a select few, more solemn. When these commemorations were so few in the course of the year, as in the 4th–5th centuries, it was possible to celebrate them all with identical solemnity, which was indeed done at that time: there was no marked distinction between the manner of honoring a martyr and the greatest events from the life of the Savior. Nonetheless, already unto the Fathers of the 4th–5th centuries are attributed by later writers attempts to single out from among all commemorations the feasts in honor of the Savior into a category of special solemnity. In the homily on the Ascension attributed unto St. Epiphanius of Cyprus, 4 great feasts are enumerated (the Nativity of Christ, Theophany, the Resurrection, and the Ascension) [^1556]. St. Proclus, Patriarch of Constantinople († 447), enumerates 5 of them (adding the Descent of the Holy Spirit)

=== LEAF p315 ===
[^1557]. In the words placed into the mouth of St. John Chrysostom by George Hamartolos (9th century), there are 7 such feasts (adding: the 'Saving Passion' and the 'Seventh Great, Unceasing, Awaited Day of the Universal Resurrection of the Dead') [^1558]. John, Bishop of Euboea (8th century), enumerates 11 principal feasts: all *(Orig. p. 327)* the present Twelve Great Feasts, excluding the Entry into the Temple, the Dormition, and Palm Sunday, with the addition of the Conception; in another passage he adds unto these feasts the Dormition as well [^1559]. Thus this era was already close unto distinguishing the feasts in honor of the Savior and the Mother of God into a category of the highest rank, and was even approaching the concept of the sacred number 12 for them. Nevertheless, even now these feasts were not yet separated from the others so sharply as they were subsequently. This is evident from the Festal Gospel (written in gilded letters) of the Sinai Library, wherein, alongside all our present 12 feasts (excluding Palm Sunday), there are Gospels appointed for a whole series of other days in the year: September 1, December 24, January 6, February 7 (St. Peter, in all likelihood the patron saint of the monastery), March 9, April 23, May 8, May 10 ('Dedication of the Theotokos of our Monastery'), June 29, August 29, and all Sundays; in a certain festal Apostolos of the 8th century several of these commemorations are absent (apart from special ones: September 8, March 9, and June 29 are lacking), but there are added: October 26, November 1 and 8, December 6 and 26, and June 24 [^1560].

The expansion and elevation of certain feasts was fostered likewise by the civil authority. Thus Emperor Justinian in 534 renewed the prohibition of judicial proceedings for December 25 and January 6 [^1561]. In 542 the same emperor, in all likelihood, celebrated for the first time in Constantinople the Feast of the Meeting of the Lord [Hypapante] and made it mandatory for the entire empire, in thanksgiving for the deliverance of Constantinople from a three-month pestilence and Antioch from an earthquake [^1562]. Unto Emperor Maurice (582–602) is attributed the introduction of the feasts of the Nativity of the Theotokos, the Annunciation, and the Meeting of the Lord (for the latter two, in all probability, their renewal) [^1563]. Of the Twelve Great Feasts, there originated in this era the Transfiguration (the earliest authentic homilies for this feast being those of St. Andrew of Crete), the Nativity of the Theotokos (likewise), and the Entry into the Temple (present in a Festal Gospel of 715).
 
### The Nativity Fast

In this same era the Nativity Fast received its origin in the East and its universal dissemination in the West. Its kernel, as we have seen, could have been the 4th of the 'fasts of the four seasons' [Ember fasts]. Yet from the 5th century there are reports for the West regarding a fast specifically preparatory unto the Nativity of Christ. Thus concerning Perpetuus, Bishop of Tours († 491), Gregory of Tours relates that from St. Martin's Day on November 11 until the Nativity of Christ he fasted on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Saturdays [^1564]. The Second Council of Tours (567) prescribes that monks fast *(Orig. p. 328)* on all days in December until the Nativity of Christ (canon 27). The Council of Mâcon (*Concilium Matisconense*, in Gaul) in 581

=== LEAF p316 ===
prescribes fasting on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Saturdays from November 11 until the Nativity of Christ, and the offering throughout all this time of the Sacrifices (*sacrificia*) according unto the order of the Forty Days [canon 9]. Later the fast was adopted in Rome and Italy. Augustine and Leo I make no mention of it, yet the series of homilies of Gregory the Great begins with discourses for the second Sunday of Advent (such being the name given unto the Nativity Fast, as a preparation for the coming, *adventus*, of the Savior). The Sacramentary of Pope Gelasius, assigned unto the 6th century, provides 5 masses (that is, for 5 weeks) of Advent; the ancient Spanish Silos Lectionary has the same number, whereas the Milanese and Mozarabic rites possess 6 weeks of Advent (42 days, corresponding unto our 40). However, the 7th-century Lectionary of Luxeuil (*Lectionarium Luxoviense*) and the so-called *Missale Gothicum* (*Gotico-Gallicanum*) begin with the vigil of the Nativity, and consequently have no Advent. Pope Gregory VII in the 11th century reduced the number of weeks of Advent unto 4 (in all likelihood, corresponding unto the 4,000 years according unto Roman chronology prior unto the Nativity of Christ). In the East the first indication of the Nativity Fast (from November 15) occurs in a Coptic calendar of the 8th century [^1565]. In the 9th century, as we shall see, the obligatoriness and duration of this fast were still disputed here.

### Ecclesiastical Chant

With the development of the church year in the 6th–8th centuries is closely linked the activity of the Eastern hymnographers of that era. On the one hand, it was evoked and expanded by this development; on the other hand, it contributed unto it, elevating ecclesiastical commemorations by hymns extolling them. Therefore this activity, no less than the history of menologia, may serve as an index of the growth and enrichment of the church year during this period.
 
### The Troparion and Sticheron (Venerable Auxentius)

Properly speaking, only from the 6th century did hymnographic creation in the East achieve immortality, since not a single hymn prior unto this time hath come down unto us, at least under its author's name. Evident it is that only now did such creative labor enter upon the true path. Remarkable it is that precisely in this era hymnography completely languished in the West, and local councils there, as we have seen, attempted definitively to bar its access unto church services. If we compare the Eastern hymns of that era with the Western, the cause of this difference will become manifest: Western compositions continued to content themselves with general, indefinite themes; Eastern hymns responded vividly unto the rapid development of the church year, striving to exhaust each ecclesiastical commemoration. Therefore Eastern hymnography could not cease so long as this development continued. A special invigoration of it is observable precisely toward the beginning of the 6th century in Constantinople, and for this invigoration it was indebted unto the above-mentioned *(Orig. p. 329)* St. Auxentius. From him date the most ancient forms of ecclesiastical song that have come down unto us: the troparion and the stichera. This

=== LEAF p317 ===
Bithynian ascetic († 470), according unto the words of his biographer, for the people gathering at his cave 'composed troparia of two or three clauses, exceedingly pleasant and profitable, though simple, and caused them to chant them in a voice most simple and artless. When one verse had been repeated several times in alternating chant, they began at the command of the blessed one to chant the second, then the third, and the others in order.' The biographer cites specimens of these troparia of Auxentius: one of these specimens hath as its theme the angelic doxology of the Lord; another, God's creation of the world; the third, redemption; the fourth and fifth are of a penitential character; the sixth is a glorification of the saints (**"Rejoice in the Lord, O ye righteous, and pray for us. Glory to Thee, O Christ, God of the saints!"**). The biographer likewise observes that some remained at this common singing until the 3rd hour (of the morning), others until the 6th hour (9:00 a.m. and 12:00 noon), and that with special solemnity the venerable father himself pronounced the Canticle of the Three Holy Children with the refrain of the people: **"Praise and exalt Him supremely unto all ages"**, concluding this canticle with his own homily [^1566]. In this narrative is found an indication of the original manner of chanting troparia: each strophe was chanted twice by both choirs; an echo of this practice remains in our present chanting of the entire troparion twice or thrice, and on great feasts in the identical execution of the majority of stichera, irmoses, and troparia. In this same manner psalms were chanted in the 4th–5th centuries.
 
### The Kontakion

Yet very soon such singing was abandoned both for psalms and for the troparion. If for psalms it was abandoned under the influence of the desire to chant as great a number of them as possible (ascetics chanted the entire Psalter within twenty-four hours), for troparia it was abandoned owing unto the multiplication of these hymns. Then the thought arose, in place of the solemn congregational chanting of each verse in a troparion, to chant in this manner only its final verse as a refrain unto an entire series of troparia. With the change in the manner of chanting the troparion (*τρέπω* = I turn; *τρόπος* = among other things, melody, tune), the designation likewise had to change: such hymns came to be called *kontakion* (*κοντάκιον*), a corruption of *κονδάκιον* (a scroll [^1567], from *κόνταξ*—a rod or stick), in all likelihood because they drew after themselves an entire series of other hymns; these latter, supplementary unto the kontakion and of longer length, were called *oikos* (*οἶκος*—house, temple, household, or the 12th part of the zodiac [^1568]).

### The Authors of Kanons

The first creator of kanons was, in all likelihood, St. Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem († 644), a native of Damascus. He composed triodia [three-ode kanons] of a penitential character for all the days of the Forty Days [Great Lent] and for the Resurrection of Christ, which are no longer in use today (having been supplanted, in all probability, by Studite compositions); thus the original form of the kanon was the triodion, perhaps even the diodion [two-ode kanon], just as at Matins originally not all biblical canticles were chanted, but only several (as hath survived unto this day in Armenian and Roman Catholic worship). Apart from triodia, St. Sophronius wrote Anacreontic verses and epigrams for many feasts (evidently not intended for liturgical use). Under his name are inscribed in the most ancient manuscripts the Royal Hours of Great Friday, the Eve of the Nativity of Christ, and the Order of the Great Blessing of Water at Theophany. St. Andrew († c. 712) of the Lavra of St. Sabbas, subsequently Archbishop of Crete, was in all likelihood the first to write complete kanons [nine-ode kanons], yet he began with triodia, which he composed for all the days of Holy Week (now chanted at its Complines, having been supplanted at Matins by the triodia of Cosmas), with the exception of Holy Saturday; for the more solemn feasts he wrote complete kanons—those presently in use being: the Great Kanon, that for the Raising of Lazarus, the Sunday of the Myrrh-bearers, Mid-Pentecost, the Nativity of the Theotokos, *(Orig. p. 332)* the Conception of Anna, June 24 [Nativity of the Forerunner], and December 20 unto St. Ignatius the God-Bearer; those not presently in use: for the Exaltation, the Meeting of the Lord, June 29, July 23 (unto the Martyr Trophimus and his companions), and August 1 (unto the Maccabees). St. Germanus, Patriarch of Constantinople, who died in exile for the veneration of the holy icons (the son of an executed senator, previously Bishop of Cyzicus), composed kanons presently in use: for the Forefeast of the Exaltation, May 30 unto St. Isaacius, and June 17 unto the Martyr Hermias. ### The Metrical Structure of Ecclesiastical Song

Greek ecclesiastical chant in all the indicated genres (troparion, kontakion, kanon, sticheron) of this era, as of subsequent periods, is constructed according unto a special poetic system: not the ancient metric system based upon the quantity (length and brevity) of syllables, but upon the so-called rhythmic system, based upon the number of syllables in a verse and the placement of accents upon them; this because the distinction between letters according unto their length and brevity had by that time already vanished from spoken speech.
