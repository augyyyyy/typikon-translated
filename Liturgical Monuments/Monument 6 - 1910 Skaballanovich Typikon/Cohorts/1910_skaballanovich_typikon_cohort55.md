# EXPLANATORY EXPOSITION OF THE TYPIKON

=== LEAF p542 ===
## The Title of the Book

### The Meaning of the Word "Typikon"

In every book, its title is of the utmost importance. A good book title immediately reveals to the reader what new insight he will find within it, and even guides him toward the resolution of the question to which the book is devoted. The book now called the *Typikon* previously bore other titles—for example, the designation still in common use today: the *Ustav* [Rule]. Its present title developed over centuries, and this fact alone demonstrates that it must be deliberate and deeply significant. Being an adjective derived from *typos* (τύπος)—feature, form, model, pattern, norm—*typikos* (τυπικός) signifies "fashioned according to a model"[^1835]; yet it is not identical with *antitypos* (ἀντίτυπος), an imprint or copy. Therefore, in English the word *typikos* corresponds somewhat to the word "exemplary," if the latter is used in the sense of "most fully conforming to its archetype." *Typikon* (Τυπικόν), with which *biblion* (βιβλίον, book) is understood, can signify a "book of models"—models, understandably, of divine worship. Only in the Orthodox Church does the book setting forth the order of divine service bear such an original title. It is untranslatable into the Slavonic language, for which reason it was retained without translation. The word *ustav* [rule/statute] would be an inaccurate rendering of the Greek *typikon* (*ustav* corresponds rather to the Greek *diataxis* [διάταξις]).
*(Orig. p. 447)*

### The Meaning of the Title

Such a title artfully defines the character not only of the book to which it is assigned, but also of the divine worship itself with which this book is concerned. In relation to this divine worship, a book bearing such a title seeks not so much to enact statutory legislation for its minutest details—thereby eliminating all freedom on the part of the officiants—as it seeks to depict a lofty ideal of divine worship, an ideal whose beauty would constantly evoke an involuntary striving toward its realization, even if that realization, in full measure, might not always be possible, just as the realization of every ideal and the following of every lofty model is not always possible. Such, essentially, is the entire law of Christ: not fully realizable in all its heavenly height, yet by His divine majesty inspiring an irresistible yearning in humanity toward its fulfillment, and through this imparting life unto the world.

### The History of the Title

It is not surprising that so profound a title for our book had a long and intricate history. Along the path of its development, the Typikon exchanged the following designations. The one whom tradition considers the author of the rule presently in force among us, the so-called Jerusalem Typikon—St. Sabbas the Sanctified (5th c.)—named his concise rule himself, or those who recorded it named it, with three words at once: *Typos kai paradosis kai nomos* (Τύπος καί παράδοσις και νόμος: model, tradition, and law). To the representative of another form of the church rule, which for a long time enjoyed far wider dissemination than the first—St. Theodore the Studite (9th c.)—tradition attributes a rule bearing the name *Hypotyposis* (Ὑποτύπωσις: outline, sketch). To St. Athanasius the Athonite (10th c.), who transferred this second rule to the Holy Mountain of Athos where it was to undergo a new redaction, is attributed the composition of a *Diatyposis* (Διατύπωσις: "re-formation," "representation")[^1836]. The most ancient of the complete typika that have come down to us, the rule of the Church of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (9th–10th c.), has survived without its title leaves, but in all probability bore the title *Synaxarion* (Συναξάριον), a collection of assemblies—that is, an indicator of the days on which solemn liturgical assemblies take place[^1837]. Competing with this rule in antiquity and completeness, the Sinai manuscript of the rule is entitled *Kanonarion* (Κανονάριον), a collection of canons [rules]. In the 11th century, the term *typikon* (τυπικόν) already appears applied to the church rule, although still accompanied by other explanatory titles. The rule of the Evergetis Monastery in Constantinople in a 12th-century manuscript is inscribed *Synaxarion ētoi Typikon* (Συναξάριον ἤτοι Τυπικόν). The rule in a manuscript of the Turin University Library is entitled *Typikon ētoi diataxis tēs ekklēsiastikēs katastaseōs kai akolouthias* (Τυπικόν ἤτοι διάταξις τῆς ἐκκλησιαστικῆς καταστάσεως καί ἀκολουθίας: *Typikon, that is to say, Order of the Ecclesiastical Constitution and Office*). In the 13th century, in manuscript titles *(Orig. p. 448)* the single word *typikon* (τυπικόν) already appears without explanatory additions. Thus is entitled, for example, a 13th-century manuscript of the Vallicelliana Library in Rome[^1838], and, seemingly, as far as can be deciphered, a manuscript in the Sevastyanov collection of the Moscow Rumyantsev Museum (No. 491/35). In this manner, in all attempts to title our book, preference was most frequently given to words derived from *typos*. Ancient Slavonic translations hesitate in rendering the Greek *typikon* between *ustav* and *tipik*. The majority of manuscripts translate it as *Ustav*; thus the manuscripts of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 (14th c.), No. 329/384 (14th c.), and others. But manuscript No. 27/1458 of the Sevastyanov collection of the Rumyantsev Museum (1372) reads *Tipik*; likewise No. 330/485 of the Moscow Synodal Library of the late 14th century. Printed editions prior to 1641 were entitled *Ustav* or *Tserkovne Oko* (lit. 'Eye of the Church'); in the edition of 1682 this title was corrected to *Typikon*. Among the Old Believers: *Ustav*, and in the

### The Full Inscription of the Typikon

The full title of the Typikon at present on the flyleaf reads: "The Typikon, that is, the Rule" (*Tipikon siyest' ustav*). On leaf 1: "The Typikon, that is to say, the depiction of the ecclesiastical order in Jerusalem of the holy Lavra of our venerable and God-bearing father Sabbas. The same order also obtains in the other honorable monasteries in Jerusalem, and likewise in the other holy churches of God" (*Tipikon sirech' izobrazhenie tserkovnago posledovaniya vo Iyerusalime svyatyya lavry prepodobnago i bogonosnago ottsa nashego Savvy. Tozhde posledovanie byvayet i v prochikh vo Iyerusalime chestnykh obitelekh; podobie i v prochikh svyatykh Bozhiikh tserkvakh*). The title, therefore, links the origin of the present rule with the Lavra of St. Sabbas the Sanctified, situated near Jerusalem. The history of the liturgical rule does not permit us, as we have seen, to determine with precision what in the present Typikon must be attributed to St. Sabbas and the customs of his monastery. Indisputably, such an origin can be assigned only to the custom of the Sunday All-Night Vigil, with the order of which the present Typikon begins. It is possible that originally the present inscription of the Typikon was applied primarily to the "office" (*posledovanie*) of the Sunday Vigil.

The title in nearly this form is repeated in all complete manuscripts of the rule, known at present only from the 13th century onward. But in some of them the final sentence—"The same order also obtains..."—is absent (for example, in a 16th-century Greek manuscript from the library of the Lavra of St. Sabbas itself[^1839]). Without this sentence, the title must have had its original form. The next oldest form of the title must be when, in place of this sentence, there stands the addition: [in the Lavra of St. Sabbas] "and in the majority of the holy monasteries found here" (thus in a Greek manuscript of the Vatican Library of 1346, No. 320/331). In the majority of manuscripts, only the concluding addition is lacking: "and likewise in the other holy churches of God"—evidently the latest addition of all (thus in the manuscripts of the Moscow Synodal Library, Nos. 328/383 and 329/384, Rumyantsev Museum Sevastyanov collection No. 27/1458, and others).
*(Orig. p. 449)*

## THE CONTENTS AND DIVISION OF CHAPTER 1 OF THE TYPIKON

The Typikon begins with the exposition of the Sunday service, because it was formed earlier than the weekday service and served as the foundation and model for the latter. Such a beginning for the Typikon is all the more natural since in the majority of churches divine services are celebrated comparatively rarely on weekdays.

Such a beginning for the Typikon, like its title, was likewise chosen not without historical hesitation. The most ancient monuments of the church rule of the 9th–10th centuries—the *Hypotyposis* attributed to St. Theodore the Studite, the *Diatyposis* bearing the name of St. Athanasius the Athonite, and the *Kanonarion* of the Sinai Library—begin with the Paschal service. The Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th century and others begin with the Menologion. Typika of the Studite type begin with the preparatory Sundays before the Great Fast. Typika of the Jerusalem type were the first to begin the Typikon with the evening service, which is explained by the significance they attached to the celebration of the Lord's Day [Sunday].

The exposition of the Sunday service in the Typikon begins with Saturday evening in view of the fact that the liturgical day among us Christians, as among the Jews, begins in the evening[^1840], because the course of time, according to the teaching of Holy Scripture, began in the evening. Moreover, it is all the more natural to begin the celebration of the Resurrection in the evening because the very Resurrection of Christ took place on "the evening of the Sabbath"[^1841]—that is, during the Saturday night. Naturally, the church rule appoints that this night, beginning from the evening, be spent in prayers.

The sequence of such prayers, or Sunday services, opens with the Ninth Hour and Little Vespers. Chapter 1 of the Typikon sets forth the order of these two services, subjoining to them also the order of the evening meal [trapeza]. Prior to examining these three parts into which Chapter 1 of the Typikon naturally divides, we prefix a preliminary remark on the bell-chime [blagovest] and on the customary beginning of church services, since both are mentioned here in the Typikon for the first time.

## THE BELL-PEAL [BLAGOVEST]

### The Meaning of the Bell-Chime

Much attention in general is devoted to the bell-chime [*blagovest*] in the Typikon, and rightly so. It not only announces the time of divine service, but also prepares Christians for it: the grace-filled effect that it exerts upon the soul is universally recognized. For those absent from the divine services, it in a certain manner even *(Orig. p. 451)* substitutes for the latter. It is, properly speaking, already divine worship itself, celebrated by the sounds of music. Old Testament worship consisted predominantly of music; and behold, as though in commemoration of this, and in token of its indissoluble bond with that worship, the more spiritual and exalted New Testament worship possesses in the bell-peal its own sacred music—of all forms of which it has chosen, in this manner, the most simple, austere, and unartificial.

### The History of the Bell-Chime

Like the entire order of church services, the bell-chime had a long history, and assumed its present form—that of bell-ringing—at a relatively late date, although no later than when the church services themselves assumed their current form. Naturally, under a simpler structure of divine worship, the method of summoning to it was also simpler. The Old Testament Church, however, possessed, one might say, a more musical method of summoning to divine service than ours (which is natural given the entire character of its worship)—namely, by means of silver trumpets[^1842]. Among ancient Christians during the period of persecutions, bishops summoned the faithful to divine service by sending messengers to their homes. St. Ignatius the God-bearer advises in a letter to St. Polycarp of Smyrna: "Let assemblies be held more frequently; summon all to them by name (*ex onomatos*)"[^1843]. In the spurious epistle of Ignatius the God-bearer to the deacon Heron, among the duties of a deacon is placed also the summoning of the faithful to divine service, each one individually[^1844]. Tertullian calls liturgical assemblies *convocationes*[^1845]. In the Church of Jerusalem in the 4th century, the archdeacon during divine service announced the place and time of the subsequent service with such formulas as: "Let us all be ready at such-and-such an hour in such-and-such a place" (for example, "in the Martyrium," "on the Mount of Olives")[^1846]. In the primitive coenobia of Egypt, according to the words of St. John Cassian, they summoned the brethren to divine service by knocking upon the door of their cells: "When

=== LEAF p548 ===
sitting in their cells and diligently engaged in labor and meditation, they hear the sound of the knocker (*sonitum pulsantis*) upon their door and the doors of other cells, which summons them to prayer or to some common task, each one immediately hastens to depart from his cell"[^1847]. In accordance with this, in the *Life of St. Pachomius* the summons to divine service and to the refectory in his monastery is designated by the word "to strike" (*krouesthai* / κρούεσθαι)[^1848], which is why in the 5th century "striking"—*krousma* (κρούσма), *(Orig. p. 452)* *krouma* (κροῦμα)—becomes the technical term for the summons to divine worship[^1849] (as also in the present Typikon).

In the *Lausiac History* of Palladius (5th–6th c.) the instrument for this knocking is also indicated; here, concerning the monk Adulphes (4th c.), it is recorded: "Having fulfilled the customary canon of prayer, he immediately struck with an awakening mallet (*tō exypniastikō sphoriō* / τῷ ἐξυπνιαστικῷ σφορίῳ) upon all the cells, gathering [the brethren] into the churches for the morning doxology"[^1850]. Thus, a mallet for knocking upon the cell door is the most ancient instrument for summoning to divine service. But from the 6th century onward we find mentions also of the semantron [*bilo*]. In the *Life of St. Theodosius the Great* († 529), composed by his contemporary Theodore, Bishop of Petra, concerning the summons to the Hours and the Liturgy it is recorded: "they struck the wood (*ekrouon to xylon* / ἔκρουον τὸ ξύλον)"[^1851]; the same appears in *The Spiritual Meadow* of John Moschos[^1852]. Besides *xylon* (ξύλον)—"wood"—the semantron was called *krousma* (κρούσμα, striking), *semantron* (σύμαντρον, a hand-semantron), *sēmantēr* (συμαντήρ), and *sēmantērion* (συμαντήριон, the latter being, presumably, the mallet for striking the semantron), and *sidēron* (σίδηρον)—an iron semantron. Iron semantra are mentioned for the first time only in the complete typika of the 11th–12th centuries; in some of them and in Balsamon (12th c.) there is mention also of bronze semantra[^1853]. At present, semantra are used predominantly on Athos, Sinai, and in Palestine, and in general in large monasteries (including Russian ones) alongside bells. A wooden semantron is called *sēmantron* (σήμαντρον), and an iron one *sidēron* (σίδηρον); distinctions are also made between the "great" semantron and the "little semantron" [*bil'tse*]; the largest semantra (festal ones) are called "heavy" (*tyazhkaya* / βαρέας)[^1854]. In addition to material and size, semantra are distinguished also by the manner of their use: there are hand-held semantra and suspended semantra. In shape, the semantron is an elongated straight or curved board, sometimes with perforations at the ends. The hand-held semantron is held with the hand or placed by its center upon the left shoulder; the suspended semantron is hung from chains or a rope passed through the center of the board. They strike the semantron with a mallet of the same material, and depending on whether the blow is struck nearer to or farther from the center, sounds of different pitch are produced, forming an entire gamut of tones (especially on a *(Orig. p. 453)* metal semantron), thanks to which the striking of the semantron acquires an entirely musical character[^1855].

Bells appeared originally in the West and are first mentioned by Gregory of Tours († 594) under the name *signa*[^1856].

=== LEAF p549 ===
Dageus, an Irish bishop († 586), being a master of copper and iron smithing, made 300 bells (*campanos*)[^1857]. In the East, bells are mentioned for the first time in the 9th century: according to the Venetian chronicle of John the Deacon, Doge Ursus sent to Constantinople, at the request of Emperor Basil the Macedonian (876–879), 12 bells for a church constructed by the latter[^1858]; Byzantine historians recounting the construction of this church, however, do not mention the bells. Transferred from the West around this time, bells were evidently admitted to liturgical use in the East not without hesitation and not entirely willingly. Although from the 11th and 12th centuries there are several accounts of the use of *kōdōnes* (κώδωνες—bells, presumably of small size[^1859], whence through corruption of the word arose *kandiya*) in the churches of Constantinople and Thessalonica[^1860], yet Balsamon *(Orig. p. 454)* in the 12th–13th century remarks concerning semantra: "Among the Latins there exists another custom of summoning the people into the churches, for they employ a single sign—I mean the campan (*kampanon*), which they call thus from the field (*kampos* / κάμπον), because, according to their words, just as an open field presents no obstacles to one who wishes to travel, so also the high strike of a bronze *kōdōn* resounds everywhere"[^1861]. (Balsamon's etymology for *kampanon* is not uncontroversial; others derive this word from Campania, which supplied the finest bronze for bells). And in the early 15th century, Symeon of Thessalonica speaks only of semantra.

Introduced and disseminated so late in the East, bells were banned by the Turks (permitted in Constantinople only in 1856). In Rus', chronicles mention bells from the 11th century (first in 1066), but seemingly as a great and costly rarity: bells serve as war booty, carried off by princes who conquered Kyiv and Novgorod. In the 14th century (under the year 1394), mention is made for the first time of a bell-tower—in Pskov—and of bell-founders in Moscow (under 1342 and 1346, and in Tver under 1403)[^1862].

### The Mode of the Bell-Chime for Little Vespers

The church rule recognizes the following modes of the bell-chime (and of ringing in general during divine services): festal, Sunday, weekday of three degrees of solemnity, Lenten, and funeral. Of these modes of bell-ringing (concerning which more detailed treatment will be given under the corresponding rubrics of the Typikon)[^1863], for Little Vespers there is appointed the weekday ringing of the lowest solemnity—on the small campan (bell). Such a bell-chime is appointed also for Compline, the Midnight Office, and the Hours, equally on weekdays and on feasts. The bell-chime conforms in solemnity not

=== LEAF p550 ===
only with the day, but also with the service. Because all the specified services, with the exception of Little Vespers and the Midnight Office, are identical in structure on feasts and weekdays, the bell-chime for them is appointed to be always identical and of the lowest solemnity (according to the most ancient typika of the Studite type, on feasts these services—especially the Hours—were omitted altogether). Little Vespers and the Midnight Office, however, are subsumed in the Typikon (as we shall see) under the same type as the Hours.

### The Time of the Bell-Chime

As regards the time of the bell-chime for Little Vespers, it is indicated by the present Typikon indeterminately: "before the setting of the sun," whereas for other services, especially on the most important days of the Triodion and Menologion cycles, this time is indicated more precisely—by the hours. Here an indeterminate indication is given in view of the difference between the length of the day in summer and in winter.
*(Orig. p. 455)*
In ancient Greek and Slavonic manuscripts of the Typikon (14th–16th c.) there is added to this rubric: "that is, about the tenth hour." This addition was excluded by the Nikonian correctors (it was retained in the Old Believer Typikon), because the assignment of fixed hours for church services is inconvenient in view of the unequal length of the day in winter and in summer: the tenth hour (3–4 p.m.) in summer would fall in the heat of midday. The most widely accepted time for Little Vespers, as for Daily Vespers, is in Russia 3–4 p.m. in winter and 5 p.m. in summer. On Athos, the bell-chime for Little Vespers begins about 3 p.m.[^1864]

### The Order of the Bell-Chime

The bell-chime for Little Vespers, as for every service, the Typikon appoints to be performed by the paraecclesiarch (assistant ecclesiarch) or candle-lighter (*kandilaptēs* / κανδηλάπτης), who receives a blessing for the bell-chime from the superior (the abbot). Every task in a monastery is performed with the blessing of the superior, all the more so such a sacred action as the bell-chime—the beginning of divine service. By the blessing is expressed also the superior's consent to the beginning of the service. Oversight of the timeliness of the bell-chime, however, is laid first of all upon the paraecclesiarch—a thought conveyed by the characteristic expression: "he comes to the superior, signifying by his coming the time of the striking."
