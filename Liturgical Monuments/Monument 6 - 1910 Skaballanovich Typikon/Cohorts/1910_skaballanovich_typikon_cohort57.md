Traces of primitive forms of doxology (in which the names of the Holy Trinity were *(Orig. p. 465)* not placed in identical grammatical cases) have been preserved among us in certain exclamations ("with Whom Thou art blessed, together with the All-Holy... ### The Trisagion

The prayer **"Holy God..."** (*Svyaty Bozhe...* / *Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός...*) represents an amplification of the Seraphic hymn in the vision of the Prophet Isaiah [Isa. 6:3]. According to the tradition related in the epistles cited by Zonaras (12th c.)[^1915] of Patriarch Acacius of Constantinople (471–479) and other contemporary bishops to the Chalcedonian presbyter Peter the Fuller (who sought to introduce into this prayer the addition "Who wast crucified for us"), it was heard from the Angels by a boy lifted up into the air during a penitential moleben on the occasion of an earthquake in Constantinople in 438–439 (under Emperor Theodosius the Younger, Pulcheria, and Patriarch Proclus) 20 days prior to Pascha around the 3rd hour of the day, and by imperial edict was immediately introduced into liturgical use. The Monophysite addition "Who wast crucified for us" introduced by the aforementioned Fuller, notwithstanding the extensive polemic against it, was accepted for a time even in Constantinople, for which the city, according to tradition, suffered many calamities (during a litany with the singing of such a Trisagion, an ash rain fell upon the city). This addition is retained today in the Armenian and Coptic [Egyptian] Churches, wherein in its justification it is argued that the entire hymn may be referred to the Son of God alone.[^1916] The Greek text of the prayer: *"Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός, ἅγιος Ἰσχυρός, ἅγιος ἀθάνατος, ἐλέησον ἡμᾶς"*—permits also the translation: "Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal, have mercy on us" (such a translation would be closer to the original, which lacks the vocative case); then the prayer formula would consist of praise and petition, which also corresponds to that detail of the tradition that the boy heard from the Angels only the first part of the hymn, and the people added: "have mercy on us."

### "O Heavenly King" and "Most Holy Trinity"

Concerning the remaining prayers in the usual beginning, regarding the origin of the prayers **"Most Holy Trinity"** (*Presvyataya Troitse*), **"O Heavenly King"** (*Tsaryu Nebesny*), and **"Glory to Thee, our God, glory to Thee"** (*Slava Tebe Bozhe nash, slava Tebe*), nothing can be stated with certainty. The prayer **"O Heavenly King"** is rather a hymn than a

prayer, and serves as a sticheron of Pentecost, from the service of which it was undoubtedly borrowed into the usual beginning, inasmuch as it appears therein later than in that festal office. Judging from the fact that this hymn is unknown to the 9th-century Typikon of Constantinople, it appeared *(Orig. p. 466)* no earlier than the flourishing of hymnographic activity in the 8th–9th centuries. According to the Horologion of the Sinai Library No. 865 of the 12th c. and the Ethiopian Horologion (14th c.), it is appointed as yet only as a troparion at the Third Hour (in the latter also at the Midnight Office). The prayer **"Most Holy Trinity"** the Coptic Horologion contains in the following simpler and therefore, perhaps, most ancient form: "Most Holy Trinity, have mercy on us (thrice). Lord, cleanse our sins; Lord, cleanse our iniquities; Lord, forgive our transgressions; Lord, visit the infirmities of Thy people and heal them, for Thy Name's sake." The Horologion places it not within the order of daily services, but within the order of evening prayers, in their midst, after **"Holy God"**.[^1917] The Ethiopian Horologion, however, places it in various services after the Trisagion, before the **"Our Father"**, as with us, but again in a different recension: "Most Holy Trinity, have compassion upon us; Most Holy Trinity, spare us; Most Holy Trinity, have mercy on us. Lord, forgive our sins; Lord, remit our iniquities and our transgressions; Lord, visit the infirm among the people and heal them, for the sake of Thy Holy Name."[^1918]

### "O come, let us worship"

**"O come, let us worship"** (*Priidite poklonimsya*) likewise did not immediately receive its present form. In the Typikon of the Monastery of St. Nicholas of Casole (in Calabria) of the 12th c. # THE NINTH HOUR

## Character of the Service

Sanctified by the death of the Savior and His descent into Hades, the ninth hour of the day opened unto us a new era of spiritual life; it was the pre-beginning of the Resurrection of Christ and of our own, and therefore with it the Church naturally begins the cycle of the daily services. But in view of the fact that at this hour there took place so sorrowful an event in the earthly life of the Savior as death, the liturgical commemoration of this hour cannot bear a festive character, and to this hour, as to the other sorrowful hours in the life of the Savior, the least festive type of service is assigned.

## The Order of the Hour in General

The Hours (*hōrai* / ὧραι, *horae*) are services specially instituted for the sanctification of specific hours noteworthy in some respect in the history of our salvation, in thanksgiving for the events that transpired in them; but since our salvation was accomplished through the sufferings of Christ, the individual moments of this great work were the most agonizing in the life of the Savior. Therefore the sentiment of thanksgiving for salvation cannot but be mingled with tears of compassion for Christ and sorrow over the sins that brought Him to the Cross. Hence the service of each of the Hours, of which the Orthodox Church, like the Roman Catholic, celebrates four—the First, Third, Sixth, and Ninth—bears a sorrowful, penitential character. This service for all the Hours possesses one and the same order (scheme), completely unlike the order of other services and, specifically, inferior to them in solemnity. The service of the Hour begins (if one excludes from it the usual beginning, as an accessory and contingent part) with Psalms, as do all services except the Liturgy. This is the most natural beginning for a church service, since in the Psalms the Christian Church has always possessed ready-made, specifically prayerful material, and moreover material not only of unsurpassed but of unattainable height, divinely inspired.[^1923] Moreover, it is natural for a Christian to begin prayer in the Old Testament spirit, in order to pass thence to New Testament prayer, experiencing thus in the small compass of one's soul that gradation of religious disposition which believing humanity experienced through the centuries. At each Hour three Psalms are appointed in honor of the Holy Trinity and in correspondence with the three hours of each "watch of the day," each of those divisions of the day which

=== LEAF p565 ===
is sanctified by the service of the Hour. To the series of Psalms at the Hour, as at every service, there is joined *(Orig. p. 468)* as a conclusion to them a brief but rather complex *prayer formula*, half Old Testament and half Christian in content (as constituting a transition to purely Christian prayers), of a laudatory and prayerful character. It consists of the *Minor Doxology*, wherewith from ancient times in the Christian Church the singing of Psalms was concluded;[^1924] after this Christian glorification of God follows the glorification of God customary among the Psalmists, serving also as a refrain to certain Psalms: *“Alleluia”*, left untranslated for the purpose of reminding us of our connection with the Old Testament Church and in order to impart a special mysteriousness and ineffability to our doxology,[^1925] as well as on account of the impossibility of rendering this word into the Slavonic tongue by a single word.[^1926] Repeated thrice in honor of the Holy Trinity, Who has already been glorified also by the Minor Doxology, the *Alleluia* is concluded by joining to it the verse **"Glory to Thee, O God,"** breathing the artless simplicity of antiquity in the glorification of God. The thrice-repeated *Alleluia* with the single—as if in token of the consubstantiality of the Holy Trinity—**"Glory to Thee, O God"**[^1927]—the whole in its en*(Orig. p. 469)*tirety is repeated again thrice in honor of the Holy Trinity, and for greater concentration of attention.[^1928] After this altogether lengthy doxology there follows a comparatively brief petition (as throughout Orthodox worship generally praise predominates over petition): **"Lord, have mercy,"** likewise thrice. This prayer formula replaces at the Hours the litany (*ektenia*) that follows the preparatory Psalms at longer and more important services. After the preparatory Psalms at Lenten Hours (though not always and not at all of them) a *kathisma* is appointed—one, as at Vespers. Having glorified God through the lips of the Psalmist, the service of the Hours begins now to praise God with the Church's own Christian hymns and to pray to Him with its own prayers. But, in accordance with the modest solemnity of the Hour as a church service, there are assigned to it the simplest (and most ancient) of the various genres of hymnography, namely the troparion and kontakion (at Compline and Typika—even a kontakion alone). The *troparion* for Lenten Hours is adapted to the commemoration of the Hour, while for non-Lenten Hours it is adapted to the commemoration of the day (from the Menaion, Sunday Octoechos, or Triodion). Inasmuch as the troparion by its concept is not (like stichera and in contrast to the kontakion) an independent hymn, but only a refrain to a Psalm verse, at the Hours, as at the beginning of Matins (its primary and original place), *(Orig. p. 470)* it is sung not alone, but is framed with *verses*, to which it serves, as it were, as a refrain. These verses are selected likewise in accordance

=== LEAF p566 ===
with the commemorations associated with the Hour, but with them only the Lenten troparion is sung (which is repeated thrice with two verses), since only the Lenten troparion is adapted to the commemorations of the Hour (for this reason in ancient monuments these troparia were termed prokeimena;[^1929] the current troparion of the First Hour, by reason of its brevity, completely possesses the appearance of a prokeimenon). On non-fasting days, however, in place of Psalm verses, the Minor Doxology serves as the refrains to the troparion; for this purpose the doxology is divided here (as always when it is divided) into two halves, each of which represents a complete thought: the first renders honor to the Holy Trinity, while the second directs our thought toward eternity; the first precedes the troparion, and the second precedes the Theotokion. (At Lenten Hours the doxology in its entirety precedes the singing of the troparion and concludes it). Originally, according to ancient Typika (as will be seen), the present Lenten troparia were always performed at the Hours (on the other hand, the Hours according to these Typika were not sung on Sundays, feasts, and even post-festal periods). The Theotokion of the Hour for Lenten and non-Lenten troparia is one and the same, and is adapted to the commemoration of the Hour. If the day has two commemorations, then at the Hours both troparia are read, with "Glory to the Father" inserted between them. If the day has three commemorations, then two troparia are still read at the Hours, but the second place is occupied in turn at the different Hours by the second and third troparia. With the Theotokion ends the first half of the Hour—the laudatory and psalmodic half; consequently, all insertions at the Hour—for example, paremias and instructional readings at Great Lenten Hours, special troparia, paremias, the Epistle, and the Gospel at the Royal Hours—are made after the Theotokion.

The second half of the Hour is predominantly prayerful. Its beginning, like the beginning of all services and of all their more or less significant parts, is psalmodic. But as a beginning here there is taken not a series of Psalms or an entire single Psalm, but only an excerpt of a Psalm. This excerpt bears no particular name in the Typikon, but one might designate it the *"verse of the Hour"*; at the First Hour this verse is **"Order my steps in Thy word..."** [Ps. 118:133], at the Third Hour—**"Blessed is the Lord God..."** [Ps. 67:20], and so forth. In its content the verse of the Hour represents the prayerful petition of the believer at a particular hour of the day, initiating with divinely inspired words the second, prayerful part of the Hour. Instead of the brief prayer of the first part of the Hour, consisting merely of the threefold **"Lord, have mercy,"** this part of the Hour possesses three (corresponding to the three Psalms of the first part) lengthy prayer texts, noticeably demarcated from one another: the Trisagion with the **"Our Father,"** the prayer **"Thou Who at all times,"** and the special prayer of the Hour. Between the first and second of these prayer texts, that is, after the **"Our Father,"** there are appointed at Lenten Hours once again *troparia*,

=== LEAF p567 ===
adapted to the commemorations of the Hour: at the First Hour—one, and at the remaining Hours—three each, including the Theotokion, linked together by the two halves of the *Minor Doxology*, wherein the troparion of the First Hour and the Theotokion of the Sixth Hour change on Wednesday and Friday. These troparia are bor*(Orig. p. 471)*rowed from the services of the Triodion and Menaion cycles, and the majority of them serve also as sessional hymns (*kathismata*) of the Octoechos. On non-fasting days, however, in place of these troparia there is appointed the kontakion of the day (Sunday, Menaion, or Triodion, corresponding to the troparion of the first part of the Hour). The kontakion *introduces* into the purely prayerful content of the second part of the Hour a laudatory element and, together with the troparion, adapts the service of the Hour to the commemoration of the day (the troparion and kontakion, of all hymns, illuminate the celebrated event most concisely and fully). Just as in the first of the three prayer sections of this part of the Hour everything prior to the **"Our Father"** can be regarded as preparation for this prayer, so in the second prayer section the 40-fold **"Lord, have mercy"** is a preparation for the richly meaningful prayer **"Thou Who at all times,"** in which we penitently entreat Christ to accept the prayers of the Hour—and in general everything necessary for our body and soul. This prayer is attributed by a number of ancient Horologia, beginning with Sinai MS No. 865 of the 11th c., to St. Basil the Great. The prayer concludes, as it began, with **"Lord, have mercy"**—threefold, the Minor Doxology to the Holy Trinity, and in addition (in view of the end of the service), the hymn to the Most Holy Theotokos, the most concise and expressive: **"More honorable than the Cherubim."** Then, conscious of the insufficiency of our prayers, we entreat those of the priest with the words: **"In the Name of the Lord, Father, give the blessing."** In response to this the priest at the First and Ninth Hours prays: **"God be bountiful unto us, and bless us, and cause His face to shine upon us, and have mercy on us"** (an allusion to dawn and evening), and at the Third and Sixth Hours: **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers, O Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us,"** as if referring those entreating his prayers to more powerful intercessors. This exclamation serves at the same time as the dismissal for the Hour, which for the most part has no formal dismissal (in antiquity, as will be seen, the service of the Hour ended at this point). Inasmuch as the needs of the believer at different hours of the day vary, after the prayer common to all the Hours (**"Thou Who at all times"**) there follows also a special *prayer* proper to each Hour, which is the third prayer section of the second part of the Hour and together the *concluding prayer of the Hour*. After it, if with the Hour a series of services concludes (as is always the case with the First Hour), or if its service is sharply separated from the next (by being performed in the narthex), a *dismissal* is made, which for the Hour is naturally appointed as the small dismissal, differing from the great dismissal by the brevity both of the preparatory prayer formulas (the prayer to the

=== LEAF p568 ===
Mother of God and Her glorification being omitted) and of the priestly dismissal formula (among the saints only the Most Holy Theotokos and the venerable [monastic] fathers are commemorated—the latter in view of the original assignment of the Hours exclusively for monasteries).[^1930] Unlike other services, the Hours (non-Lenten), as the least solemn service, consist of continuous reading without singing. According to the intention of the Typikon (which, however, is nowhere explicitly expressed), the troparia and kontakia of non-Lenten Hours should likewise be read, since their Theotokia are not adapted in tone (*ēchos*) to the troparion (to Lenten troparia they are adapted). Only in fasts, when the Hours, by virtue of their sorrowful tone, correspond more to the needs of the day *(Orig. p. 472)* and therefore are performed with greater solemnity, are the troparia at them sung (according to ancient Typika, on certain fasting days so-called sung Hours were performed, consisting entirely of singing).

## History of the Office of the Hour in General

The custom of sanctifying with prayer the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours passed to Christians from the Old Testament Church and was observed already by the Apostles.[^1931] In the 2nd century at these hours, presumably, they read the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"**), which was required to be recited thrice daily.[^1932] In the 3rd century, in addition to this prayer, at the sacred hours of the day they sang Psalms with the refrain of a single verse and *Alleluia*,[^1933] and specific commemorations were assigned to the Hours,[^1934] expressed, perhaps, only in corresponding reflections and discourses. In the 4th century in the Church of Jerusalem the service of the Hours possesses the identical composition with Matins and Vespers, namely, it consists of the singing of Psalms and antiphons until the arrival of the bishop, who, having arrived, entered the sanctuary, read a prayer, came out to the people, and blessed everyone individually.[^1935] From this latter order of the Hours there were subsequently formed the so-called sung Hours. Such Hours in the 9th–11th centuries in the same Church of Jerusalem consisted of the singing of a single Psalm, a troparion with Theotokia (the present ones), a prokeimenon, Old Testament readings, the verse of the Hour, and the Trisagion.[^1936] In the 13th century the sung Hours consisted of the singing of a single Psalm, the Great Litany, three antiphons, the Little Litany, a troparion with Theotokion, a reading, the Litany of Supplication, the Trisagion with **"Our Father,"** **"Lord, have mercy,"** the present blessing of the priest, and the prayer of the bowing of heads.[^1937] Both orders, to be sure, are appointed by those monuments wherein these orders are transmitted for Holy Week, wherein in the second monument for ordinary days Hours close to our present ones are appointed (in the first monument the Typikon is preserved only for Holy and

=== LEAF p569 ===
Bright Weeks); but judging from Jerusalem practice of the 4th century, one may suppose that originally, where the so-called sung order of worship prevailed, the Hours in general approached in their structure Vespers and Matins, and already beginning with the 5th–6th centuries possessed an order close to the specimens presented, especially the first. But the sung order of the Hours did not enjoy universal dissemination, and alongside it there was formed another order of the Hours—the monastic order, which reduced, for example, in the 5th century among the Egyptian ascetics, according to the testimony of St. John Cassian, the entire service of the Hour to Psalms, interspersed with secret ("noetic" / mental) prayers, prostrations, and the lifting up of hands, wherein the number of Psalms was diverse in different places: either six at each Hour, or corresponding *(Orig. p. 473)* to the number of the Hour (at the Third—three, and so forth).[^1938] The Psalms were chanted by one of the brethren in an even melody without interruption, presumably in recitative, judging from the fact that John Cassian expresses it now as "were sung," now as "were recited." From the mutual influence of these two types—of which one imparted to the service of the Hour too great a solemnity, not distinguishing it from the other major services, while the other gave a very monotonous order to the service of the Hour—there arose the present type of the Hours, uniting elements of both. By the 8th–9th centuries we find this latter, present type already outlined in its details. Thus, according to the Greek Horologion of the Sinai Library (No. 863) of the 8th–9th c. according to the order of the Lavra of St. Sabbas, the Hours have the following composition. After the preparatory Psalms, whose number for different Hours varies—for the First: 8, the Third: 7, the Sixth: 6, the Ninth: 4—there follows *Alleluia* with two Psalm verses corresponding to the Hour (as today at Lenten Vespers and Matins); then the *troparion* of the Hour (the present one, excluding the First Hour), but before it and after it there is written in the Horologion its concluding phrase, which presumably served as its refrain (thus, for example, for the First Hour: "And direct. [O Christ,] True Light"). After this troparion there is appointed another—varying on different days of the week (for Sunday none is indicated, presumably because the Hours were not chanted then). After the troparia comes our present *verse of the Hour*, but divided into two parts, of which the first is repeated thrice (as today in Lent at the First Hour: **"Order my steps..."**). The service of the Hour concludes with the *Trisagion* (the **"Our Father"** is not mentioned). From this type of the Hours in this Horologion only the First Hour diverges by certain additions at the beginning and at the end.[^1939] Beginning with the 11th century, surviving Horologia provide a service of the Hours almost of the present structure, excluding the portion following the Trisagion. Thus, troparia after the Trisagion are not present in all Horologia up to the 15th century,[^1940] or have troparia not like the present ones, and diverse across different Horologia. Furthermore, the concluding (now preceding **"Thou Who at all times"**) **"Lord, have mercy"**

is not appointed in all ancient Horologia 40 times. Thus, in one Horologion of the Moscow Typography Library of the 15th c., at the First Hour **"Lord, have mercy"** is appointed 30 times, at the Third and Sixth—40, and at the Ninth—12 times (at the end of the Typika and Matins likewise 12 times each).[^1941] In the Ethiopian Horologion at each Hour **"Lord, have mercy"** is appointed 41 times, while at the offices of the third hour of the night and at cockcrow 51 times each, at Vespers and Compline both 51 and 41, and at Midnight Office 51 and 51.[^1942] But in the Studite Typikon of the Moscow Typography Library of the 11th c. it is appointed 40 times, only at the Royal Hours 30 times.[^1943] According to the Typikon of the Roman Barberini Library of 1205, even at the Royal Hours it is 40 times.[^1944] Of the concluding exclamations of the priest: **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers..."** or **"God *(Orig. p. 474)* be bountiful unto us..."**—in the 13th century the first still possessed this form: "O Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us,"[^1945] while the second already possessed its present form,[^1946] but the first was rarely used; in the 14th century, however, both present formulas appear.[^1947] The prayer **"Thou Who at all times"** and the concluding prayer are completely lacking in certain Horologia (for example, one of the Moscow Typography Library of the 15th c.).[^1948] Other ancient Horologia indicate the prayer **"Thou Who at all times"** only for the First Hour among two or three others (for example, the Horologia of the Sinai Library No. 865 of the 12th c. and No. 870 of the 14th c.).
