29. The monument was discovered by the Italian archaeologist Gamurrini in 1884 in a Latin manuscript of ca. the 12th century belonging unto the Confraternity of St. Mary in Arezzo. The initial leaves of the work are lost, which is why neither the title of the work nor the name of the author has been preserved. In the margins of the manuscript, in two places in the corner, appear the letters *PE*, which are regarded as an abbreviation of the title. Indirect evidence in the work points unto the end of the 4th century as the time of the origin of the *Pilgrimage*: the Jerusalem edifices are described in such wise that the Constantinian structures can be recognized in them; mention is made of a recently built church in Edessa, which occurred in 372 under Emperor Valens; of the left bank of the Tigris belonging unto the Persians, which was ceded unto them by Emperor Jovian in 363; of the Thebaid ascetics (among them St. Anthony, † 356, and Pachomius, † 340); on the other hand, St. Jerome's translation of Holy Scripture is unknown unto the pilgrim, as is the decree of Pope Gelasius of 494 against reading apocrypha — the letter of Abgar, the *Acts of the Apostle Thomas*, and the *Acts of the Martyr Thecla*; nor are Justinian's constructions in Sinai and Constantinople mentioned. The pilgrim came unto the Holy Land *de extremis porro terris* (from the furthest ends of the earth); judging by her comparison of the Euphrates unto the Rhône (*Rhodanus*), she was presumably a native of Gaul; she is an educated and well-read woman; she travels as a noble personage — with a retinue; she is provided with a military escort; bishops meet and accompany her. In Palestine she spent three years, residing in Jerusalem and making excursions thence. Having returned unto Constantinople, she described her pilgrimage unto her "sisters" (fleshly sisters or nuns?). The identification of the pilgrim by some with Galla Placidia, daughter of Theodosius the Great, and by others with Silvia, sister of the Constantinopolitan prefect Rufinus (an Aquitanian), is rather arbitrary. The monument is, of course, of inestimable liturgical importance, but one cannot help regretting that it belongs unto a foreigner and a woman: the pilgrim observes only
