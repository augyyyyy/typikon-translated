of St. Basil the Great (not falling upon a Great Twelve Feast or Great and Holy Week), **"It is truly meet"** is replaced by **"All of creation rejoiceth in thee"** (*O Tebe raduetsia*), a hymn which, if less ecstatic, is yet more profound. **"It is truly meet"** is Tone 6, while **"All of creation rejoiceth in thee"** is Tone 8, both possessing a special chant melody, though the former is also customarily chanted in Tone 8. **"It is truly meet"**, in addition to the Liturgy, concludes the chanting of the Canon at Matins and Compline and enters into the dismissal of the Typika (*Izobrazitel'nye*); **"All of creation rejoiceth in thee"** serves likewise as the Theotokion of the Sunday Sessional Hymn.

#### History
In heterodox liturgies, both Eastern and Western, there is nothing corresponding to **"It is truly meet"**, despite the commemoration of the Most Holy Theotokos in the anaphoral prayer of all these liturgies (with the exception of the Nestorian), moreover in a form close to ours: *"especially"* (*izryadno*), *imprimis*.[^2793] But in all ancient Orthodox liturgies there is always a hymn unto the Most Holy Theotokos at this point: in the liturgies of St. Mark the Evangelist, of St. Peter the Apostle, and of St. James the Apostle (10th–14th c.), before the words **"Especially"**, immediately in the first two, and closely preceding in the last, the priest says: *“And remember, O Lord, the archangelic voice proclaiming: Hail, full of grace... blessed is the fruit.”*[^2794]

In one manuscript of the Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom: *“the choir chants: For (ὡς) it is truly and right to glorify thee, O Theotokos... while the choir chants this, the priest says: Hail, full of grace.”*

In certain manuscripts of the Liturgy of St. Basil the Great (13th c. and later), **"All of creation rejoiceth in thee"** is absent, and in its place:

*“then the priest prays, saying thus: Hail, full of grace, the Lord is with thee, etc.”*; in others, both are present.[^2795]

**"It is truly meet"** (*Dostoyno yest'*) was perhaps composed according to the model of **"It is meet"** (*Dostoyno*) occurring earlier at the Liturgy; regarding **"More honorable"** (*Chestneyshuyu*), see *Tolkovy Typikon*, II, pp. 289–290.

At present among the Greeks at the Chrysostom Liturgy:

*“The 1st choir chants harmoniously the Megalynarion (μεγαλυνάριον) of the Theotokos, **‘It is truly meet’**, in Tone 2”*;

at the Liturgy of St. Basil the Great:

*“The 1st choir simply recites (χύμα): **‘All of creation rejoiceth in thee’** down to ‘God before all ages’; the remainder of the troparion, however, slowly and with singing in Tone 1: ‘For He made thy womb a throne’, etc., unto the end.”*[^2796]

### The Communion Verse (*Kinonikon* / *Prichasten*)
The final variable part of the Liturgy is the Communion verse (*Kinonikon*, κοινωνικόν; Slavonic: *Prichasten*)—a verse, for the most part psalm-based, chanted during the communion of the sacred ministers within the altar. This communion, performed as a sign of special sacred mystery behind closed sanctuary doors and drawn curtain, constitutes the holiest moment of the Liturgy after the consecration (*transubstantiation*), demanding of the worshipers a profound inward recollectedness. The latter could not be attained through any prolonged hymn. What was needed here was precisely a single, concise and expressive verse with the refrain *Alleluia*, which marks all the most crucial and joyful moments of divine worship. The melody of the Kinonikon is directed toward this same end—slow, serene, and tenderly compunctious, in which echo the melodies of Great and Holy Week. (Such, at least, is the melody of the Kinonikon in the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra). The chanting of the Kinonikon may be divided between the two choirs—half a verse to each, which for the most part contains an independent clause (in the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra, the 1st choir chants the Kinonikon, and the 2nd chants the *Alleluia*).

*(Orig. p. 773)*

The Communion verse of the Sunday Liturgy—Psalm 148:1: **“Praise the Lord from the heavens, praise Him in the heights”**—has here the same significance as the Praises (*Ainoi* / *Khvalitny*) at the end of Matins: in the consciousness of the inadequacy of our praise, at its conclusion we commit the work of praise unto the heavenly hosts. And the Resurrection of the Savior is pre-eminently such a deed for which the Lord can worthily be praised only in heaven. Moreover, in that earthly heaven which is the altar, during the Communion verse there takes place a sacrifice of praise worthy of the true heaven: the most intimate communion through the Eucharistic Gifts with Christ Himself, made possible through Christ's redemptive death (cf. the Paschal Kinonikon: **“Receive the Body of Christ”**). Aside from the Sunday, the Kinonikon **“Praise the Lord from the heavens”** belongs to two of the greatest feasts: the Nativity of Christ and Theophany at their principal Liturgy—the Liturgy of the Eve—as well as the Feast of the Circumcision, closely associated with the former, and the Church New Year (September 1).

If the Menaion saint has a Communion verse—and saints commemorated with Epistle and Gospel reading possess one—then after the Sunday verse his Communion verse is also chanted (*“and of the saint, if he have one”*), wherein the *Alleluia*

=== LEAF p933 ===

is chanted only after the second Communion verse (by musical tradition).

#### History
The antiquity of the Communion verse is attested by its presence in all heterodox liturgies.

In the Roman Liturgy it is called precisely thus (*communio*) and consists of a verse, for the most part psalm-based, differing not only for different feast days, but changing also on Sundays—for example, on the 3rd Sunday after Pentecost, Psalm 17:3 [18:2]; this verse is read by the priest before the altar table with folded hands; on a solemn High Mass, however, it is chanted by the choir, and the ancient Roman Order (*Ordo Romanus*) says concerning its chanting:

*“As soon as the Pontiff (the Pope) begins the communion, immediately the schola begins the communion antiphon, and they chant until all the people have communicated; the Pontiff gives a signal that they should say the Gloria, and then, having repeated the verse, they cease.”*[^2797]

In the Mozarabic Liturgy, the Communion verse, rarely designated as such (*communio*), sometimes called *“for approaching”* (*ad accedendum*), usually (even on the Nativity of Christ) has the following composition:

*“The choir says: Taste and see that the Lord is good, alleluia (3). Verse: I will bless the Lord at all times, His praise shall continually be in my mouth. Priest: Alleluia (3). Verse: The Lord will redeem the souls of His servants, and none of them that trust in Him shall offend (Ps. 33:9, 2, 23 [34:8, 1, 22]). Priest: Alleluia (3). Glory... Both now... Priest: Alleluia.”*[^2798]

In the Nestorian Liturgy the Communion verse, here called the responsory, consists of two parts, separated by certain exclamations and actions of the sacred ministers; regarding the first part, in the oldest manuscript of this liturgy, attributed to the Apostles Addai and Mari, it is noted:

*“Deacon: Praise. And they recite the responsory”*;

in the contemporary Nestorian Liturgy at this point *(Orig. p. 774)* there are appointed such “antiphons”:

=== LEAF p934 ===

*“Deacon: Render praises unto God. Clergy: Praise unto Him in the church of the saints (Ps. 149:1) evermore, and the mercy of the Lord and His compassion be upon us unto ages of ages, amen”*;

the second part according to the ancient manuscript:

*“meanwhile the responsories are spoken: Brethren, receive the Body of the Son, crieth the Church, and drink His cup with faith in the house of the Kingdom. On feast days: Strengthen, O Lord, (..?) Daily: The Mysteries which we receive (..?)”*.[^2799]

In the Coptic Liturgy, Psalm 50 [51] serves as the Communion verse.[^2800]

Among ancient Orthodox liturgies, the Liturgy of St. Peter the Apostle, which in general is close to the Roman, has as its Communion verse the phrase spoken in the latter by the priest at the commingling of the bread and wine: *“O Lamb of God, that takest away the sin of the world, have mercy on us.”*[^2801]

In the manuscripts of the Alexandrian Liturgy of St. Mark the Evangelist, no mention is made of a Communion verse. In the Liturgy of St. James the Apostle, however, according to all its manuscripts (10th–14th c.), the Communion verse, preceded by the deacon's exclamation *“In the peace of Christ let us sing”*, is:

*“O taste and see that the Lord is good (Ps. 33:9 [34:8]). Alleluia.”*[^2802]

The Kinonikon **“Praise the Lord”** could have arisen from the chanted exclamation of the priest in the Liturgy of St. James after the fraction of the Lamb: *“Praise God in His saints”* (Ps. 150:1), or (according to another manuscript): *“Praise the Lord, all ye nations”* (Ps. 116:1 [117:1]).

In antiquity, apparently, the Communion verse chanted during the communion of the clergy was repeated also during the communion of the laity: in an 11th–12th-century manuscript of the Apostol (the Muravyov manuscript) of the Moscow Theological Academy Library, on Meatfare Sunday:

*“Kinonikon: **‘Rejoice (in the Lord, O ye righteous)’** the small; and when the holy communion begins, **‘Rejoice’** double and **‘Let our mouths be filled’**.”*[^2803]

At present among the Greeks:

*“The leader of the 2nd choir (the Lampadarios) chants (the Kinonikon) slowly and melodically (ἀργῶς καὶ μετὰ μέλους) in the tone in which the Cherubic Hymn was chanted”*, that is, *“in the principal (κύριον) tone of the day.”*[^2804]

### The Order of the Panagia (*Chin o Panagii*)

#### Meaning and Content of the Order
The essence of the Order of the Panagia consists in this: that from the church upon the conclusion of the Liturgy, the prosphora from which the particle in honor of the Mother of God was extracted at the Liturgy is carried forth by all the brethren with sacred songs into the monastic refectory (*trapeza*); there it is placed upon a special vessel, and at the conclusion of the meal, with the glorification of the Holy Trinity and prayer to the Most Holy Theotokos, the prosphora is elevated (raised up) above Their icons, and the brethren partake of it. The meaning of the rite, evidently, is vividly to represent the presence at the meal of God Himself and of the Most Holy Theotokos. Such a significance of the rite is indicated also by the tradition concerning its origin *(Orig. p. 775)* recorded in the Sequenced Psalter (*Sledovannaya Psaltir'*; discussed below). According to St. Symeon of Thessalonica, *“a portion of bread we offer daily unto our One God existing in Trinity in the name of the Mother of God, because through her divine childbearing we have come to know the Holy Trinity, and because she bare for us the Living Bread.”*[^2805] The immediate objective of the rite is so closely to unite the refectory meal with the just-concluded Liturgy that both should appear as a single divine service and the former should communicate its grace unto the latter. Thanks to the Order of the Panagia, indeed, the monastic dinner is transformed into a veritable divine service of the type of the Typika (*Izobrazitel'nye*) and Compline, with Kontakion and Zadostoynik in place of a Canon (and on Pascha even with an entire Ode of the Canon).

**"Panagia"** (παναγία—*“All-Holy”*), or *“Most Holy”* (*Sequenced Psalter*, fol. 135v), is a title customarily applied to the Mother of God, but in the Order of the Panagia—or, as it is more fully titled, *“On the Elevation of the Panagia”* (*Sequenced Psalter*, fol. 136)—this appellation is applied to the prosphora from which at the Liturgy the particle was extracted in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos. This prosphora is, therefore, the most sacred after that from which the Lamb was extracted, and which, as the most sacred of all, is consumed not in the refectory after taking food as is the panagia, but in the church itself prior to any food (*antidoron*).

The not inconsiderable hymnody and ceremonial rites of the Order of the Panagia, which is celebrated, as its rubric indicates, only in monasteries on all days (and not solely on Sundays, although placed in the chapter on the Sunday service), can be divided into three parts:

1) The blessing of the refectory meal;
2) The elevation of the panagia;

=== LEAF p937 ===

3) The thanksgiving for the meal.

— Aside from the present place, the Typikon also touches upon the Order of the Panagia in Chapter 35, *“On Food and Drink”*, introducing certain supplements to the present exposition.

The rite opens, as does every church service, with a psalm. In accordance with the brevity of the rite and akin to Vespers, it possesses a single introductory psalm, namely Psalm 144 [145]: **“I will exalt Thee, my God”**. The psalm (alphabetical) presents an ecstatic praise unto God, unto His greatness as well as His goodness, especially outpoured upon them that call upon Him; it is taken here especially for verses 15–16: *“The eyes of all look toward Thee in hope...”* The psalm does not have **“Come, let us worship”** before it, by reason of connection and analogy with the Liturgy. The brethren all *“recite”* (*glagolyut*) the psalm; consequently, they chant it in recitative, as is done in the Solovki and other monasteries.[^2806] The psalm is chanted along the way from the church to the refectory; consequently, its chanting begins in the church immediately following the dismissal of the Liturgy and concludes in the refectory. The brethren walk two by two, preceded by the superior. Who carries the panagia and in what manner is not indicated either here or in Chapter 35 of the Typikon; yet in the Paschal Order of the Panagia, where it is replaced by the *Artos*, the latter is carried by the deacon upon a *panagiarion* (a proskomide vessel bearing an image of the Mother of God). In certain monasteries, for example in the Moscow Gethsemane Skete, the *“Most Pure”* (*Prechistaya*) prosphora, which during the Liturgy stands with the diskos or a dish of similar construction *(Orig. p. 776)* upon the High Place, is carried in front by a hieromonk, behind whom the brethren walk two by two in their monastic mantles.[^2807] According to Chapter 35 of the Typikon, the carrying of the panagia is accompanied by the striking of the semantron (*bilo*) or bell, which imparts a special solemnity to this procession and at the same time serves as the tolling (*blagovest*) for the refectory meal, which, thanks to the Order of the Panagia and the patristic reading, constitutes a genuine church service. The psalm is chanted with such calculation as to end it upon entering the refectory: *“And having arrived at the refectory and the psalm being finished, we recite (that is, in the same manner as the psalm—we chant in recitative) the refectory prayer: Our Father.”* The prayer is introduced here not only because, as the chief Christian prayer, it is necessary at every service, but also on account of its fourth petition; it is not omitted even in the Paschal Order of the Panagia, though it is absent at the evening meal. To the prayer is joined the blessing of the refectory meal, preceded by the usual introduction: **“Glory... Both now... Lord, have mercy (3). Bless.”** (see above, p. 719). The superior or priest (probably in the absence of the superior)[^2808] pronounces: **“Christ our God, bless the food and drink of Thy servants...”**, etc. (see in the *Sequenced Psalter*, whence it is evident that the final *“Amen”* of this blessing is likewise pronounced by him), blessing, as goes without saying, the table. *“And each one sits...”*

=== LEAF p938 ===

*“...down in his own place according to rank,”* says Chapter 35, *“with reverence and silence”*—with reverence, because the refectory meal here is the same as a divine service; with silence, also in view of the fact that during the meal a reading takes place. Regarding the reading, nothing is said in Chapter 2 of the Typikon, but in Chapter 35 the order of reading is also provided: *“the reader immediately (as soon as the brethren sit down) begins the preface of the reading which he intendeth to read”*, that is, the reader pronounces the title of the reading in the form, for example: *“The Life (or: The Passion) of the Holy Martyr N., bless, father.”* *“The superior or the priest of the day, blessing, saith: ‘Through the prayers of him whose reading this is...’”*, that is: *“Through the prayers of the holy martyr (or: ‘of our venerable father’) N., Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us.”* What is to be read in the refectory on ordinary Sundays and weekdays the Typikon does not specify either here (in Chapters 2 and 35) or anywhere else; only for certain feasts does it give such an indication—for example, on December 25 in the refectory *“we read the homily of Chrysostom whereof the beginning is: Anciently the patriarchs.”* By established practice it is customary to read the Lives of the Saints in the refectory, yet not from the *Prolog*, from which they are read after the 6th Ode, but from the *Chet'i-Minei* (Menologia), where the Lives are more extensive. And this is undoubtedly an ancient practice, only not recorded in the written Typikon. It is more fitting in the refectory to read the Life of the saint of the following day, in order to prepare for the forthcoming service unto him, and in view of the fact that the liturgical day concludes with the Liturgy (cf. the rubric of Bright Saturday concerning the Ninth Hour and the closing of the Holy Doors). During the meal the panagia in the panagiarion *“stands in an arranged place”*, that is, upon an analogion in front of the tables beneath the icons or the Holy Doors (if the meal takes place in the church). Here also must lie upon an analogion the icons of the Most Holy Trinity and the Most Holy Theotokos, necessary for the rite of elevation.

*(Orig. p. 777)*

Begun with prayer, the meal is likewise punctuated by recurring prayer, although very brief. Before each course of the dinner (*“at each serving of that which is offered”*), *“the superior strikes the kandya or bell, and when all have arisen, the server (the principal among those serving at dinner, that is, the cellarer) saith: ‘Lord, bless.’ The superior: ‘Glory to Thee, Christ God, our hope, glory to Thee’”* (Chapter 35 of the Typikon)—that is, each course is preceded by the same thanksgiving, only briefer, with which the dinner began and with which each church service customarily concludes. The reader does not interrupt the reading for these exclamations. Upon the conclusion of the meal, the reader stands at the end of the table and, turning toward the superior with his hands crossed upon his breast, proclaims aloud: *“Holy fathers, forgive me a sinner”*, bowing to the ground. The superior imparts unto him a blessing. This is the rite of forgiveness for errors in the reading.

=== LEAF p939 ===

The elevation of the panagia itself is performed after the eating, upon the conclusion of dinner. Consequently, the elevation is preceded by a brief thanksgiving for the meal (the full thanksgiving occurs after the elevation), which forms the transition to the rite of elevation:

*“After the meal, he that celebrateth the ministry (the priest of the day) saith the verse: ‘Through the prayers of our holy fathers, Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us.’ And we: ‘Amen.’”*

This is the blessing for thanksgiving and for the elevation of the panagia in place of **“Blessed is our God...”** (cf. at the beginning of morning and evening prayers), serving also as the signal to arise from the table.

*“And having arisen they recite (all recite together): ‘Blessed is God, Who hath mercy upon us and nourisheth us from our youth...’”*—a prayer unto God the Father, glorifying God for nourishing us and all creation, and asking for joy, contentment in all things, and an abundance of virtue (in full in the *Sequenced Psalter* in the Order of the Panagia); and **“Glory to Thee, O Lord, glory to Thee, O Holy One, glory to Thee, O King”**—another prayer unto the Holy Trinity, glorifying Him for bestowing upon us food unto gladness and asking Him to fill us with the Holy Spirit that we may not be put to shame at the Dread Judgment (in full, ibid.). These two prayers conclude with the petition for a blessing upon the elevation itself, which constitutes the solemn conclusion of the meal, in the usual form: **“Glory... Both now... Lord, have mercy (3). Bless.”** The requested blessing is imparted not by words, but by the rite itself. By whom the elevation is performed is not specified in Chapter 2 of the Typikon, and is apparently left open by the expression: *“he that is about to elevate the Most Holy”*; Chapter 35 of the Typikon assigns this to the deacon (*“here however we have the custom that the deacon performeth these things”*—cf. the exposition of the rite on Bright Saturday), evidently as the person who, according to the original concept of this order, is a servant of tables (Acts 6:2); in the Paschal rite the corresponding action is entrusted to the cellarer.

In view of the importance of the forthcoming sacred action, he who is about to perform it asks forgiveness (like the priest before celebrating the Liturgy and communicating) in the words customary for such occasions: *“‘Bless, holy fathers, forgive me a sinner’; and we: ‘God forgive thee and have mercy on thee.’”* A preparation necessary for the forthcoming exalted ministry—the offering of sacred sustenance unto exalted invisible Guests. Having cleansed himself *(Orig. p. 778)* through the forgiveness of offenses from men and sins from God, he who elevates the panagia proceeds to the very action of the elevation.

*“And taking the portion (the prosphora) with the outermost fingers of his hands* (with the thumb and little finger, and with the remaining fingers, consequently, making a canopy over the prosphora, though in the exposition of the rite on Bright

=== LEAF p940 ===

Saturday: *‘with three fingers of both hands’*) *he elevateth it slightly (raiseth it a little) above the icon of the Holy Trinity, saying with a loud voice: ‘Great is the name’*,” not daring to utter the Name itself, which is then done by *“the superior or the priest of the day: ‘Of the Holy Trinity.’ And transferring it above the icon of the Mother of God, he signeth it crosswise (not simply raising it as before, but cruciformly), saying: ‘Most Holy Theotokos, help us.’ And we (not the superior or the priest, as on that occasion): ‘Through her intercessions, O God, have mercy on us and save us.’”*

And since the rite has the most immediate relation unto the Mother of God, constituting an offering unto her (for unto God the most sacred sacrifice of the Eucharist has already been offered), there follow next prayers unto her alone. All recite together (*“we recite”*, as previously with the thanksgiving prayers, in contrast to the following *“we chant”*): **“All generations call thee blessed...”** (*Blazhim Tya vsi rodi...*). This is a joyful hymn unto the Theotokos, praising her in the name of all generations of mankind for containing the Uncontainable One and for her constant prayer on our behalf (*“day and night”*—not without reference to midday), which consolidates the civic order (which likewise secures our sustenance) (the hymn serves as the Theotokion to the troparia for the departed at the Midnight Office, where its tone is also indicated: Tone 2). Then there is chanted (the first chanting in the Order of the Panagia) the most customary hymn in honor of the Theotokos, **“It is truly meet...”** (concerning the replacement of this hymn by a Zadostoynik in the Order of the Panagia on feast days and afterfeasts nothing is indicated anywhere, yet during the Paschal season it is replaced by the entire 9th Ode of the Paschal Canon). The brethren partake of the panagia. *“And after all have communicated, the superior or the priest saith: ‘For the many prayers of our Most Pure Lady the Theotokos.’”* This exclamation serves as the blessing upon the third part of the Order of the Panagia now beginning—the thanksgiving for the meal—and as its link with the second part dedicated exclusively to the Mother of God, for which reason the response to this exclamation (*“we however”*, that is, all recite) is the previous: *“Through her intercessions, O God, have mercy on us and save us.”*
