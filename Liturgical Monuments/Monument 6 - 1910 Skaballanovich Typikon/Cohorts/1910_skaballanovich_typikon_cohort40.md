unto Anastasius of Sinai, the Dormition Fast is represented as having detached itself from the Fast of the Holy Apostles: the latter originally extended from the first Sunday after Pentecost until the Dormition, but subsequently, on account of human infirmity, the month of July was excluded therefrom. The Athonite monks around 1085 AD inquired of Nicholas Grammatikos, Patriarch of Constantinople, concerning the fasts, among which the Dormition Fast especially perplexed them. Concerning it they wrote unto the Patriarch: 'Concerning the fast which is in the month of August until the fifteenth day, we find [mention] in the union at the Council held in the city of Constantine concerning marriages, and being at a loss to commemorate it, we entreat: resolve us from contention.' The Patriarch, after deliberating with the Synod, replied: 'The fast was at that time transferred (that is, the fast was transposed from this time unto another) for this reason, lest they fall into the pagan fasts occurring at that time; nevertheless, many persons even yet fast for the sake of cleansing from illness' [^1778].

The nascent fast aroused such interest in society that around that same period—in the 11th–12th centuries—there appeared also a specialized treatise by a certain Anastasius of Caesarea. The antiquity of the fast is therein demonstrated by appeals unto the testimonies of the Patriarchs of Antioch and the Council of Union, and the author arrives at the conclusion that the fast was observed even prior unto Emperor Leo the Wise (886–911 AD) [^1779]. Finally, around that same period the question of the Dormition Fast, as well as the Nativity Fast, was once again examined at the Council of Constantinople under Patriarch Luke and Emperor Manuel Comnenus (12th century). According unto the account of Balsamon, certain persons at this Council asserted that in the decree of the preceding (above-mentioned) Council under Patriarch Nicholas mention had been made of transferring the Dormition Fast unto another time, and therefore the fast could have no place during the first half of August; but since that Council had not specified unto what time the fast had been transferred, while from the mention of the Council it was clear that the fast had been observed of old in August, this Council confirmed the observance of the fast in this month. During his patriarchate Balsamon wrote encyclical and private epistles exhorting the observance of this fast [^1780]. Nikon of the Black Mountain (11th century) states concerning the Dormition Fast that those who do not observe it have no foundation in antiquity for themselves, yet those who observe it are likewise established not upon apostolic tradition, but upon the custom of subsequent times, which is, however, exceedingly ancient [^1781].

### The Disciplinary Portion of the Typika

From the very emergence of eremitism and monasticism, the divine services came to be regarded merely as one of the constituent parts of spiritual ascetic labor (*podvig*) in general. The entire day of the monk, and indeed of every Christian, ought to be a divine service, and church prayer serves merely as the supreme moment of this latter. Therefore in the primitive monastic rules, such as those of St. Pachomius and St. Sabbas, exceedingly little attention is accorded unto the divine services. Not so from the 9th century. The Rule of St. Theodore the Studite, insofar as it can be reconstructed from his writings, as well as the earliest recording of this rule in the so-called *Hypotyposis*, together with the *Diatyposis* of St. Athanasius of Athos, devote approximately one-fourth of their content unto the divine services. Yet even here it constitutes only a small constituent part of the common ascetic labor. The ktetorial Studite Typika of the 11th and subsequent centuries are already predominantly monuments of a liturgical character, and the disciplinary portion occupies little space within them. Still less space does it occupy in the Jerusalem Typika, at times almost disappearing entirely. Nevertheless, by the very idea of Orthodox liturgical worship and by the historical circumstances of its development, in which monasticism played so vast a role, the liturgical Typikon unto this day deservedly does not dispense with the disciplinary element, however small it may be in the present-day Typikon in comparison with its archetypes of the 4th–6th centuries. This element within it—its disciplinary portion—continued its evolution during the present period in the history of the Typikon. This development was directed both toward the curtailment of the disciplinary portion within the Typikon, and toward the mitigation of its requirements.

### Monastic Discipline under St. Theodore, in the Hypotyposis and Diatyposis

The decline of monasticism and the relaxation of strictness of life within it, by which the iconoclast emperors at times justified their persecution thereof, compelled St. Theodore to attend unto the firm organization of his monastery, which numbered up to 1,000 monks [misprinted 100]. In his admonitions unto the monks he frequently insists upon the undeviating observance of the order established by him, attributing in general great importance unto the will of the superior (*hegumen*) and obedience thereunto:

=== LEAF p398 ===
> 'Let each of you watch with attention how and whither he directs his steps, whether they are in accordance with my humble commandment, with the cenobitic rule, with the requirement of cutting off one's own will' [^1791].

Of the hegumen he demands:

> 'Without extreme necessity do not alter the outline and *(Orig. p. 419)* rule which you have received from our humility in all things' [^1792].

Fidelity unto the monastic rule is maintained under St. Theodore, on the one hand, by a system of monastic hierarchy, and on the other, by a penal code for infractions thereof—epitimias (*penances*). In the monastic administration, after the hegumen, the second rank was occupied by the *deuterarios* (cf. in the Pachomian monasteries, above, p. 211), *ho ta deutera pheron* (*ὁ τὰ δεύτερα φέρων*), *ho deutereuon* (*ὁ δевτερεύων*) [^1793], his closest assistant and deputy—almost identical with the ecclesiarch of subsequent Typika. The third rank was occupied by the oikonomos (*steward*) [^1794]. Next followed the epistemonarchs—who examined offenses and assigned penances; according unto the *Hypotyposis*, 'unto them lesser transgressions are reported, and they apply measures of correction' [^1795]; taxiarchs assigned places unto all in assemblies; according unto the *Hypotyposis*, they reminded the brethren to stand decorously in the choirs; under St. Theodore there were four of them, while the *Hypotyposis* appoints two for the two choirs [^1796]; overseers (*epiteretai / ἐπιτηρηταί*)—supervised the fulfillment of the monastic rule [^1797]; according unto the *Hypotyposis*, two are appointed every evening; 'after the striking of the wooden semantron (*bilo*), they urge the slothful to go to Vespers and Compline, and upon the conclusion of the latter they inspect the hidden places in the monastery, and with due severity disperse those who gather for unseasonable assemblies' [^1798]; the awakener (*aphypnistes / ἀφυпνιστής*), according unto St. Theodore, awakens the brethren for Matins [^1799]; according unto the *Hypotyposis*, during the readings at Matins, quietly making the rounds of the brethren, he awakens those who have fallen asleep [^1800]. There are likewise mentioned in St. Theodore the *kellarites* (*κελαρίτης*)—the cellarer, in charge of provisions, and the *aristetarios* (*ἀριστητάριος*), literally the supervisor of morning collation, the refectorian [^1801]. The *Diatyposis*, of these officials (beginning from the epistemonarch), does not indicate taxiarchs, but defines the duties of epistemonarchs as the *Hypotyposis* defines those of the taxiarchs: unto them it likewise assigns the first half of the overseers' duties—to dispatch unto church after the bell-ringing those who have tarried, adding that from those remaining (in their cells during the service) they demand an explanation, and if they remained there not by order and not of necessity, they urge them unto diligence with moderate penances; furthermore, the *Diatyposis* speaks of only a single overseer, who 'day and night making the rounds of the cells, workshops, and other places of the Lavra with due severity and appropriate penance, disperses those who indulge in chatter and unseasonable strolls'; finally, the *Diatyposis* likewise indicates the office of the doorkeeper (*porter / thyroros / θυρωρός*), who, having charge of the entrance into the church, demands from those who did not arrive before the beginning of the service an account of their tardiness, and forbids those who leave unseasonably from exiting after one or two occurrences. Concerning penances (*epitimias*), although the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* mention them repeatedly, they do not specify their types and degrees, *(Orig. p. 420)* silently referring thereby unto the specialized instructions concerning them by St. Theodore in

=== LEAF p399 ===
the *Monastic Epitimias* [^1802] and the *Great Catechesis* (§§ 60, 69). In penances St. Theodore is exceedingly close unto Nicephorus, Patriarch of Constantinople († 828 AD), in his *Penitential Canons* [^1803]; from Basil the Great St. Theodore is distinguished by granting less discretion unto superiors in assigning penances [^1804]. The degrees of penance under St. Theodore are: 1) excommunication (*aphorismos / ἀφορισμός*)—that is, exclusion for a certain time from communion with the brethren at meals, in prayer, and in work [^1805]; it is appointed under St. Theodore in 23 cases: for example, for theft, inciting the brethren against the superior, evasion of confession and Holy Communion, negligence in service duties, idle wandering through the monastery, concealment of others' misdeeds (inter alia, also for reciting from memory unto the brethren from a book that was being transcribed); 2) xerophagy (*dry-eating / xerophagia / ξηроφαγία*)—subsistence upon bread and water, probably for a single day each time, for less important omissions in duty, and for tardiness unto divine services; 3) prostrations (*metanoiai / μετάνοιαι*) in varying numbers in church or in the cell; 4) standing (*parastasis / παράστασις*) during meals or in church at the departure of the brethren (the latter in a bowed posture—for slight tardiness unto the service); if the former was assigned for breaking dishware, the guilty brother stood holding the potsherds.

Without speaking definitively of any of these types of penance, the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* indicate only one, but more severe form thereof—confinement in a special chamber (*aphoristeria / ἀφοριστηρία*) upon dry-eating for the insubordinate and stubborn, wherein the former observes that 'correction by means of scourges (*dia mastigon / διὰ μαστίγων*, cf. above, p. 195 concerning the Nitrian monastery in the 4th–5th centuries), which is suited unto laymen, is justly rejected by our holy fathers' (*Hypotyposis*, § 25). Upon the inefficacy of penances recourse was had to expulsion from the monastery (according unto the *Diatyposis*). Of offenses, the *Diatyposis* speaks with special indignation concerning theft: 'those who commit any theft whatsoever of the monastery's property deserve the death of Ananias and Sapphira; but whereas they died bodily, these inherit death of the soul with a curse' (§ 33). Of the internal life of the monastery, both documents touch upon the following aspects: He who seeks admission into the monastery must dwell for two to three weeks in the monastic guest-house (*xenodocheion*), becoming acquainted with the order and being instructed by the hegumen; after this 'catechesis', the candidate was introduced into the assembly of the brethren, made a prostration before them, and they prayed for him (*Hypotyposis*, § 24; *Diatyposis*, § 11). Complete poverty (*non-acquisitiveness / ne-styazhanie*) is required of the monks: they may not possess even their own furniture (*Diatyposis*, § 30). The greatest attention is accorded unto the divine services. Among the offices, the *Hypotyposis* specifies the hours only for the Liturgy on fast days: when the meal is at the 9th hour (= 3:00 PM), the Liturgy is at the 6th hour (12:00 noon); on non-fast days, at the 3rd hour (9:00 AM) (§ 27); according unto the *Diatyposis*, weekday Matins is at the 7th hour of the night (1:00 AM), Sunday Matins at the 6th (12:00 midnight), festal Matins at the beginning of the 5th (10:00 PM) (§ 7). In the Great Fast, the First Hour is appointed to be celebrated separately from Matins at dawn (*Hypoty*(Orig. p. 421)*posis*, § 14). It is forbidden to stand in church with staves and even to enter therein with them, or to use stools (*skamnia / σκαмνίοις*, *Diatyposis*, § 13) when kneeling. According unto the *Hypotyposis*,

On the eve of Palm Sunday at *Lord, I have cried*, the choirs were exchanged, evidently for the entire year: the right choir became the left and vice versa—naturally in view of the greater difficulty and greater quantity of chanting upon the right choir (for which reason this is treated in the chapter 'On Proportion in the Services', *Hypotyposis*, § 34). In the Great Fast an elderly (according unto the *Diatyposis*—a 'discreet') brother is designated, who from the 3rd hour (9:00 AM) makes the rounds of all the work-places, and in each, having made a metania, proclaims: 'Brethren and fathers, let us take heed unto ourselves, for we die, we die, we die'; according unto another manuscript, there is added: 'let us call to mind also the Kingdom of Heaven'; according unto the *Diatyposis*: 'concerning eternal torment'; according unto the *Diatyposis*, in response unto this the brethren 'cease work, arise, recite the Trisagion, and sit down again' (*Hypotyposis*, § 23; *Diatyposis*, § 9). In a special article 'On Proportion in the Services' both documents regulate manual labor and furnish the schedule for the monastic day. According unto the *Hypotyposis*, the brethren all engage in bodily labors from sunrise or after the chanting of the First Hour, when the hours are appointed, until the 6th hour (12:00 noon); from the 6th unto the 8th hour (12:00–2:00 PM) rest is appointed for the brethren, whether there is dinner or not; from the 8th hour (2:00 PM) until Vespers there is work again: after Vespers, 'each retires into solitude as he desires' (§ 33). The *Diatyposis* indicates days when there is no labor: these are the first and Great Week of the Fast and the week of Pascha (*Bright Week*), especially until Wednesday; other great feasts are likewise understood. The *Diatyposis* does not speak of midday rest, but occupies itself in detail with the table for those performing heavy labors—blacksmiths, muleteers, boatmen, carpenters, vinedressers, and bakers; unto these workers before the common meal there is given at the 3rd hour (9:00 AM) in the great fasts bread, and on minor fasts and ordinary days wine also, and at the refectory an additional cup (*krasobolion*) of wine is granted them; on days free from labor they are content with the common allotment (*Diatyposis*, §§ 25, 26). On feasts when there is no labor, reading of books from the monastic library is recommended unto the brethren; the *Diatyposis* gives general counsel concerning this, while the *Hypotyposis* defines also the order for distributing books: the librarian strikes the wooden semantron (*bilo*) once, the brethren proceed unto the library, there each receives a book, and before Vespers, again upon the stroke of the semantron, returns it (*Hypotyposis*, § 26; *Diatyposis*, § 28). Those who had not learned the Psalter by heart presented themselves for Matins only at the Canon upon the threefold stroke on the semantron by the canonarch, employing the beginning of Matins (the Kathismata) for the study of the Psalter (*Hypotyposis*, § 36). Both documents determine in detail the quality and quantity of clothing (see ch. 39 of the Typikon) and of food throughout the entire year, principally during fasts (see chs. 32 and 33 of the Typikon).
