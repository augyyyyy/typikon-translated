"Benedictions," it is appointed to read them at the weekday divine service; whereas on the Sabbath and feasts, of the eighteen Benedictions only seven are recited: the first three, one middle prayer corresponding to the day, and the final three; on the New Year (*Rosh Hashanah*), the lesser of the feasts, nine benedictions were recited; and on fasts, to the eighteen Benedictions six more are added, so that in all twenty-four are obtained [^58]. On feast days, a proper "additional" prayer (*musaf*) [^59] is joined to the Benedictions, and furthermore, certain modifications and insertions (*havdalah*) are made in the text of the Benedictions, for example: "Blessed art Thou, O Lord, Who sanctifiest such-and-such feast"; or, for example, on the Day of Atonement the customary address to God is replaced by the address "King of Justice"; in rainy seasons to the divine Name is added "Who sendest down the rain," and in the summer season "Who bringest down the dew" [^60]. Following the Benedictions ensues the reading of Holy Scripture.

#### Reading of the Law and the Prophets
As stated, the chief purpose of the synagogue in its original concept was not prayer, but the reading of Holy Scripture. Thanks to the synagogue, Judaism, and following it Christianity, came to regard the reading of Holy Scripture as an integral part of divine worship, a species of prayer. From the Scriptures in the synagogue, there was always read preeminently the Law of Moses, the Pentateuch, which was read straight through (originally once every 3 or 3.5 years, across half of a sabbatical cycle of years [^61], *(Orig. p. 19)* and subsequently every single year), for which purpose the Pentateuch was divided into small sections termed *parashah* (section). As regards the other two parts of the Hebrew Bible—the Prophets (*Nevi'im*) and the Hagiographa (*Ketuvim*)—the former were not read in their entirety, but in excerpts, while the Hagiographa were read only infrequently. The reading from the Prophets was selected each time by the reader himself [^62], specifically such that it corresponded to the reading from the Law [^63], disclosing and elucidating it; consequently, it stood in relation to the first reading just as in our rite the Epistle (*Apostol*) stands to the Gospel [^64]. The reading from the Prophets was called *haftarah*, "dismissal reading," inasmuch as it occurred at the conclusion of the service. The cycle of readings commenced on the Sabbath following the Feast of Tabernacles. On feast days, not the ordinary *parashot* and *haftarot* were read, but those corresponding to the sacred commemorations: for example, at Pascha from Leviticus 23; at Pentecost, Deuteronomy 15:9–13; at the New Year, Leviticus 23:25; on the Day of Atonement, Leviticus 16; at Tabernacles, Numbers 29:12–39; on the second Sabbath prior to Pascha, concerning the water of purification; and on the final Sabbath, concerning the preparation of the paschal lamb [^65]. The great feasts possessed readings also from the Hagiographa (the *Megilloth*): at Pascha the Song of Songs was read; at Pentecost, Ruth; at Tabernacles, Ecclesiastes; at Purim, Esther; on the fast of the Ninth of Av (July/August) in commemoration of the burning of Jerusalem, Lamentations. At weekday services only the Law was read. Following the reading of each verse and even phrase there ensued translation (targum) and exposition, turning not infrequently into an entire homily. Holy Scripture in the synagogue could be read by any man, excluding a slave and one with a speech impediment.

---

=== LEAF p22 ===
Yet it was required that the first section be read by a priest (*kohen*), and the second by a Levite, if such were present in the synagogue. Before the beginning of the reading, the first reader exclaimed: "Bless ye the Lord," to which the congregation responded with a general blessing of the Lord, while the reader added a blessing for the bestowal of the Law, to which the congregation answered "Amen." Prior to the reading of the Prophets the blessing was not so elaborate: it consisted of a single formula pronounced by the reader with the response "Amen." The reading likewise concluded with a blessing and the *Kaddish* prayer.

Divine worship in the synagogue concluded with the blessing (*Birkat Kohanim*), which was imparted by all the priests together, if any were present; but if not, the formula of blessing was recited by the synagogal minister (*hazzan* / *sheliach tzibbur*).

In synagogal worship, chanting no longer occupied so large a place as in that of the Temple. Nonetheless, the character of synagogal chanting could not fail to exert an influence upon our own.

#### Synagogal Singing
One cannot judge of ancient synagogal singing by contemporary singing in the synagogue. When Christianity became the dominant religion, numerous restrictions were imposed everywhere upon the Jewish cultus; even where this cultus was tolerated, as for example in Italy, the chanting of psalms was positively forbidden *(Orig. p. 20)* in synagogues; this prohibition was confirmed at numerous synods of the Western Church. Its consequence was the introduction into the synagogues of so-called cantillation, representing an intermediate form between reading and chanting: it is a loud recitation in which certain syllables are more or less prolonged. However, there are certain grounds for tracing cantillation back even to the era of the Temple. In any case, the contemporary synagogal singing of psalms must retain certain traces of antiquity, the spirit of which breathes from it. Cantillation in the synagogue recalls the chanting of psalms in the Roman Church, with the distinction that the latter employs diverse psalm tones (*toni psalmorum*), whereas cantillation is uniform for Sabbaths and feasts and resembles the fifth tone of Roman Catholic Gregorian psalmody—whence it was surmised that Catholic psalmody passed into the Christian Church through the Apostles from the Temple of Jerusalem. Traces of antiquity were preserved also in another genre of synagogal chant, approaching still closer to recitation: namely, the very reading of the Law and the Prophets with musical accentuation (*te'amim* / cantillation tropes), the marks of which are identical with grammatical punctuation marks (of which there are twenty-seven in the Hebrew language); musical accentuation varies according to the contents of the books: the Pentateuch, according to the characterization of the rabbis, must have sounds that are gentle yet low; the Prophets, sounds high and menacing; Proverbs, persuasive and insinuating; the Song of Songs, lively and joyful; Ecclesiastes, serious and solemn [^66].

Among the external bodily signs that accompanied divine worship and prayer, there were in use in the synagogue the bowing of the head and the bending of the torso at the waist (*metania*). During fasts they prostrated themselves upon the floor in cruciform posture. During divine worship they sat for the most part, standing only for the Benedictions (*Shemoneh Esreh*) [^67].

Such was that divine worship in which the Apostles and the first Christians were nurtured, and upon the foundation of which they fashioned their new service of God in Spirit and in Truth.

The delineated depiction of synagogal worship demonstrates that its influence upon Christian divine services pertained to diverse minor particulars, partially to structural order and material elements, but not to spirit and essential content. Christianity introduced into divine worship such intimacy with the Godhead and such hope as the synagogue could not even conceive. There, one may say, was the shadow, but here the very archetype of communion with God [^68].

### Worship of the Therapeutae
Scholars discover points of contact between Christian divine worship and the worship of the Therapeutae. The Therapeutae—according to recent investigations [^69]—were a circle of ascetically minded Alexandrian Jewish legal scholars, touched by Platonic philosophy and striving (like Philo, who so deeply sympathized with them) toward the spiritualization of Judaism. This could not fail to draw them near to Christianity, which explains why the church historian Eusebius regarded them as disciples of the Evangelist Mark [^70].

#### All-Night Vigils and Banquets of the Therapeutae
Philo enthusiastically describes both the ascetic life of the Therapeutae and their liturgical assemblies—"all-night vigils" (*pannychides*), as Eusebius terms them—which were joined to a common banquet. The liturgical assembly among the Therapeutae commenced with a discourse by the president concerning some passage of Holy Scripture or another question.

"Then the president ariseth and chanteth a hymn to the glory of God [^71], either newly composed by himself, or borrowed from one of the ancient poets, who left behind many hymns appointed for execution during libations and sacrifices upon the altar (psalms?), for singing at tables and choral dances. After the president, the others likewise chant hymns in order, in due decency amid profound silence and universal attention [^72], except in those cases when it is necessary to sing the verse-terminations (*akroteleutia*) [^73] or refrains

---

=== LEAF p24 ===
(*ephymnia*) [^74]. When each hath concluded his hymn [^75], the youths bring in the table, upon which is set the most holy (*panagestaton*) nourishment: leavened bread with salt and hyssop, after the likeness of the showbread. After the supper they celebrate the sacred all-night vigil (*pannychida*). The all-night vigil is celebrated in the following manner."

#### Singing of the Therapeutae
"They all arise together, and in the midst of the banquet hall first form two choirs: one of men and one of women; as leader and precentor in each choir *(Orig. p. 22)* is chosen the most venerable and skilled singer. Thus do they sing hymns composed in honor of God in diverse meters and melodies, now in unison, now in antiphonal harmony (*ἀνтифώνοις ἁрμονίαις*), in the latter case gesturing with their hands and dancing (*ἐпоρχούμενοι*), inspiredly performing (*ἐпиθειάζοντες*) melodies now processional (*prosodia*) [^76], now stationary (*stasima*), executing the necessary strophes and antistrophes. Then, when the company of men and the company of women separately have enjoyed all this, having become intoxicated, as it were at bacchic revels, with this God-beloved wine, they unite and form a single choir [^77], in imitation of that choir established of old at the Red Sea in thanksgiving for the wonders wrought there... Seeing and experiencing a wonder surpassing all imagination and description, wrought for them beyond expectation, then the Hebrews, seized with divine inspiration, men and women together forming a single choir, sang songs of thanksgiving to God the Savior, Moses the Prophet leading the men, and Miriam the Prophetess the women [^78]. It is of this very choir that the choir of the Therapeutae and Therapeutides forms the most exact copy, when to the deep (*barys*) [^79] voice of the men responds the shrill, high (*oxys*) voice of the women in resounding (*antechois*) and antiphonal melodies, which produces a truly harmonious and musical symphony" [^80].

Thus the Therapeutae significantly expanded and developed the musical element of synagogal worship, which had been so negligible there. Along this same path Christian divine worship likewise proceeded. To the hypophonic and epiphonic chanting of the synagogue, the Therapeutae added antiphonal singing [^81].

## 1st Century: Apostolic Worship

### Worship according to the Acts of the Apostles
According to the author of the Acts of the Apostles, the first newly converted Christians in Jerusalem continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine, in fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers [^82]. Their entire day was, as it were, a continuous liturgical worship, which consisted first and foremost in "the apostles' doctrine" (*ἐν τῇ διδαχῇ τῶν ἀпоστόλων* [^83]), that is, in their narratives concerning the life and preaching of the Savior, which subsequently formed the contents of the written Gospels, and in the instructions of the Apostles themselves, later set down in writing by several of them in their Epistles. To this teaching, which possessed a singular sweetness thanks to the freshness of events and the vividness of recollections, was joined the intimate fellowship (*koinonia*) of the believers among themselves, reaching even to their constant inseparability one from another and to the community of goods, but manifesting itself with particular radiance and reaching its zenith in the breaking of bread. By the breaking of bread is understood the Eucharist [^84] celebrated at common meals. These meals, on the basis of the Savior's commandment: **"This do in remembrance of Me,"** without doubt received a liturgical significance from the very beginning: they contained the commemoration of the Lord's death and the acknowledgement of the sacrificial value of that death, while also serving as the expression of inner *(Orig. p. 24)* communion of the disciples among themselves. Nonetheless, these meals originally did not possess an exclusively liturgical significance, but served in primitive Christian times also for the satisfaction of the natural need for nourishment [^85]. Alongside the breaking of bread the author of Acts places prayers. The plural form—prayers—points to diverse prayers, which could be in part new Christian prayers unbound by fixed formula, and in part psalms and prayers customary among the Jews [^86].

This general picture of Christian life at that time, which by virtue of the singular character of that life constitutes simultaneously a picture of divine worship, the author of Acts presents more vividly when he adds: and they, continuing daily with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house, did eat their meat with gladness and singleness of heart, praising God [^87]. Here the divine worship of the primitive Christians is presented in two forms: on the one hand, they participate in the Jewish, Old Testament worship of the Temple and what must have been the synagogue situated within its precincts; on the other hand, they possess as a supplement to it their own domestic worship. That the first Christians visited the Temple and participated in its divine services was entirely natural. In this case they were doing nothing other than that

---

=== LEAF p26 ===
which Christ Himself had done. It was precisely in the Temple that He taught the people, and zeal for it even consumed Him [^88]. Following the Ascension of Christ and until Pentecost, the Apostles went daily to the Temple [^89], and after Pentecost we see them very frequently within it [^90]. True, Christians now recognized only a prefigurative, typological significance in many things that previously were regarded as essential; yet the prayers, sacrifices, and liturgical rites of the Old Testament spoke to their souls no less now that they had come to know their true spiritual meaning [^91].

It was precisely upon the soil of this Old Testament piety of the first Christians that a distinct Christian divine worship had to develop. Already within the Temple itself, during its ordinary services, Christians inwardly celebrated another worship of their own, as the author of Acts indicates in another passage: they were all with one accord in Solomon's porch; and of the rest durst no man join himself to them, but the people magnified them [^92]. In the Temple Christians kept themselves distinct from the Jews, many of whom involuntarily held them in reverence, yet feared openly to join them. In the vast courts of the Temple, Christians chose for themselves the most secluded and remote area—the portico stretching along the outer eastern wall of the Temple *(Orig. p. 25)* precinct (on the inner side, of course, of that wall [^93]), situated thus within the Court of the Gentiles [^94].

Nevertheless, when present in the Old Testament Temple, Christians could participate only in the worship celebrated there, which could no longer fully satisfy them and was inherently insufficient. Their own, and at the same time genuine, divine worship they could hold (resuming our interrupted examination of Acts 2:46) only "from house to house" (*καт᾿ οἴκους*), celebrating it now in one, now in another Christian home (for example, that of Mary the mother of Mark, of Joseph, of Nicodemus [^95], or the Upper Room of Zion [^96]). Here alone could Christians celebrate that breaking of bread of which the Book of Acts repeatedly speaks, and which, as we noted, serves as the designation of common meals possessing a Eucharistic character. Following the example of the Savior's Supper, these were evening meals. In the cycle of daily and day-long prayers, this was their final sublime moment, the culminating summit in which was consummated the prayerful fervor that had mounted throughout the entire day. By virtue of this, the very partaking of food among Christians took place always "in jubilant exultation" (*agalliasei*). The uniting at the meal of the poor with the rich stripped it of all envy and imparted to it also "singleness of heart." This meal, like the entire day of the Christians of that time, was wholly prayerful, with the praising of God: praising (*ainountes*) God—perhaps an allusion to sacred hymns [^97].

Thus was laid the foundation of the agapae, or love-feasts, which existed in the Christian Church until the fourth century, even after the Eucharist had separated from them and was transferred to the morning or midday, upon the foundation

---

=== LEAF p27 ===
of which arose our contemporary Vespers with its blessing of loaves (*artoklasia*). As in the Old Testament religion, the evening was initially regarded in Christianity as the most sacred and prayerful part of the day (it being remembered that days began with the evening), to which the most solemn service was assigned; just as in the Old Testament Temple the "evening sacrifice" [^98] was more sacred and more solemn than the morning sacrifice (with the lighting of the lamps), so too our contemporary Vespers, which took the place of the ancient Eucharist and agape, approaches closest in its structure to the most sacred service of all—the Divine Liturgy; it consists primarily of ectenias, possesses *(Orig. p. 26)* only a single Introductory Psalm, has no canon, yet can possess the most complete readings from Holy Scripture, not only the Gospel, but also the Epistle. Thus, already in apostolic times and in living connection with the Old Testament cultus, was laid the foundation of one of our contemporary daily offices with all its distinctive structure.

Yet the Eucharist with the agape was only, as observed, the highest consummation of the daily apostolic worship, which, although day-long, could not after all be completely continuous. Like Jewish divine worship, apostolic worship likewise probably consisted of three daily services: in addition to the evening service—the principal one—there was a morning and a midday service. In this same Book of Acts we find clear indications of precisely a threefold number of daily prayers or services during apostolic times. Thus on Pentecost the Apostles with the believers gathered for prayer in the morning around the third hour (by our reckoning, 9:00 a.m.) [^99], approximately the hour of the morning sacrifice in the Temple; the Apostle Peter offered prayer at his lodging around the sixth hour (12:00 noon) [^100]; and the Apostles Peter and John went up to the Temple at the hour of prayer, being the ninth hour [^101], the hour of the evening sacrifice.

### Constituent Elements of Apostolic Worship
Thus the Book of Acts provides indications—albeit fragmentary—concerning the divine worship of apostolic times, yet of such a nature that from them one may form a judgment in general outlines regarding both the composition and, still more, the character of this worship. To be sure, regarding the composition of the service, the data of the Book of Acts do not present everything in the desired clarity. Concerning the four essential constituent parts of divine worship—prayer, chanting, the reading of Holy Scripture, and homiletic instruction—one can assert on the basis of the Book of Acts only that all these existed during apostolic divine worship, yet it remains unknown in what precise form they existed.

Thus, concerning prayers, not only are no liturgical formulas reported, but it is not even stated what their specific content was. Concerning the prayers of that era one can judge only by a few specimens cited in the Book of Acts [^102]; yet these are all prayers on special occasions. One may suppose, although nowhere in the New Testament is there an explicit statement to this effect, that in the divine worship of that time there began already to be employed,

---

=== LEAF p28 ===
in accordance with the commandment of the Savior, the Lord's Prayer: a monument of the early second century, *The Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* (*Didache*), prescribes its recitation three times a day [^103], that is, precisely as many times as, according to the data cited above, there could have been prayer gatherings during the course of a day in apostolic times. The Apostle Paul in one passage [^104] apparently indicates the genres of prayer of that era: supplications (*deeseis*, from *deomai*, "I need"—*(Orig. p. 27)* the general generic concept of prayer as the expression of our helplessness before God), prayers (*proseuchai*—prayer directed toward God), intercessions (*enteuxeis*, from *entygchano*, "I approach / attain someone"—intercessory prayer on behalf of others), and thanksgivings (*eucharistiai*) [^105].

Concerning chanting in apostolic worship, the Apostle Paul speaks more definitively than the Book of Acts. In two passages of his Epistles he distinguishes three genres of sacred songs in use at that time: psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs (*ᾠδαὶ πνευματικαί*) [^106]. By psalms it is most natural to understand the Old Testament psalms, the extensive liturgical use of which among the Christians of that time is attested also by the expression of the Apostle James: **"Is any merry? let him sing psalms"** (*psalleto*) [^107], as well as by the more frequent quotation of the Psalter in comparison with other Old Testament books by the Apostles [^108]. Yet inasmuch as psalms are represented in the Apostle Paul as the result of a special inspiration ("be filled with the Spirit; speaking to yourselves in psalms... spiritual"), one may understand also psalms of the singers' own composition [^109]. By hymns ("lauds") there is no ground to understand the so-called biblical canticles: the Septuagint renders with *hymnos* many Hebrew titles of psalms: *shir*, *tefillah*; by hymns the Apostle probably denotes songs of a preeminently laudatory character [^110] in distinction from prayerful ones and others of general content designated by the word songs (*odai*), singling out the former from the latter as their finest part: according to St. John Chrysostom, "hymns are a holier thing than psalms, for the angelic hosts sing hymns (*hymnousi*), and do not sing psalms" [^111]. The evangelical canticles of Zechariah, of the Mother of God, and of Symeon the God-Receiver present "fine examples of how, in close connection with Old Testament types, the Christian spirit wrought new canticles" [^112].

Nowhere in the apostolic Epistles is mention made of the reading of Old Testament scriptures at the divine worship of that era; yet this may be presumed in view of the fact that the preaching of the Apostles was grounded entirely upon the Law and the Prophets, and the latter the Apostle Paul designates alongside the Apostles as the foundation of the Church [^113]. Concerning the reading of New Testament scriptures during divine worship testimony is offered by: the injunction of the Apostle Paul in the Epistle to the Thessalonians that this epistle be read unto all the holy brethren [^114], which could most conveniently be carried out at a liturgical gathering; the injunction *(Orig. p. 28)* to the Colossians to exchange epistles with the Church of the Laodiceans [^115]; and likewise the existence, according to the testimony of the Evangelist Luke, by his time of many written records concerning the life of the Savior [^116].

As regards the homily (preaching), in apostolic divine worship, in accordance with the needs of that era and owing to the undeveloped state of other liturgical elements, it occupied an exceptionally prominent place; in the picture of divine worship provided by the Book of Acts, "doctrine" is placed first; the Apostle James had already to contend with talkative, self-appointed preachers [^117]; and the Apostle Paul regards teaching as the paramount duty of the bishop and presbyter [^118].

### Development of Apostolic Worship
Still less can be said on the basis of the New Testament writings regarding the sequential order in which these constituent parts followed upon one another during the divine worship of that time. It is possible that, at least in the initial stages and within the Jerusalem Church, among Jewish-Christians divine worship possessed an ordo close to that of the synagogue. The Apostle James not only designates the place of Christian assembly a "synagogue," but also points to certain customs within it characteristic exclusively of the latter: **"For if there come unto your assembly [into your synagogue, *εἰς τὴν συναγωγήν*, Slav. *в сонмище*] a man with a gold ring, in goodly apparel, and there come in also a poor man in vile raiment; and ye have respect to him that weareth the gay clothing, and say unto him, Sit thou here in a good place; and say to the poor, Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool: are ye not then partial in yourselves?"** [^119]. Here is the customary scene of a synagogue furnished with benches, with armchair seats in front for dignitaries, and with the distribution of worshippers according to their legal status in society [^120]. Even the Apostle Paul designates prayer by the synagogal term "blessing" [^121].

Yet if in the initial stages of church life, and especially in the Jewish-Christian Jerusalem community, divine worship in general could recall *(Orig. p. 29)* to mind that of the Temple and synagogue, nonetheless with the passage of time and in Gentile-Christian churches—and especially following the persecutions of the Church by the Jews and the dispersal of the Apostles from Jerusalem—this affinity could not fail to diminish. It is characteristic that the Apostle Paul designates liturgical assemblies no longer as *συναγωγή*, but as *ἐκκλησία*, the Church [^122]. In general, the New Testament writings following Acts, and especially the Pauline Epistles, clearly indicate that apostolic worship after the aforementioned events entered into the second stage of its historical development in comparison with that at which it is depicted by the Book of Acts and, in part, the Epistle of the Apostle James.

### Worship according to 1 Corinthians
This stage is characterized by two marks, apparently disparate, yet between which a connection can be discovered. The first is

---

=== LEAF p30 ===
the degeneration of the institution of the agapae; the second is the development of spiritual gifts (charisms) and their extensive employment during divine worship. Concerning both matters the First Epistle to the Corinthians speaks in detail. Chapters 10–14 of this epistle are devoted entirely to the regularization of Corinthian divine worship, and provide a full and distinct picture of that form of worship which it assumed among Gentile-Christians in the middle of the apostolic period. This entire extensive section of the epistle falls into two parts, the first of which regulates the more external, bodily (in the broadest sense of the word) dimension of worship (chapters 10 and 11), while the second, as its very opening demonstrates—**"Now concerning spiritual gifts, brethren, I would not have you ignorant"**—does the same with the spiritual dimension of worship, its intellectual and pneumatological content [^123].

In the former respect, the Apostle directs attention to the disregard manifested at the Lord's Supper (*kyriakon deipnon*) [^124] toward its most sacred act: the communion of the Body and Blood of Christ. Certain Corinthian Christians came to the "Lord's Supper" with the intent to eat and drink; and inasmuch as at these suppers the poor were fed from the oblations that were offered, the wealthy participants endeavored to sate themselves as quickly as possible from their own offerings, lest the poor leave them hungry. The Eucharist for such participants of the "supper" occupied second place. Concerning such unworthy conduct at the "Lord's Supper" the Apostle Jude likewise complains, mentioning "spots in your feasts of charity" (*сквернители в любвах*) [^125]. In his writing, therefore, the love-feasts are already designated by this very name—*ἀγάπαι*—which indicates a further step in their historical development. At the same time, the conduct of the baser Christians toward them is branded with a more forceful term—*spilades*, blemishes / sunken reefs (which can signify also hidden rocks upon which an institution such as the agape might suffer shipwreck) [^126]. Such conduct toward *(Orig. p. 30)* the agapae was undoubtedly the consequence of pagan habits among newly converted Christians, who carried over into them the customs of Greco-Roman *eranoi* (potluck club banquets) and *symposia* (common drinking parties). The Apostle Paul with bitterness and indignation admonishes such participants in Christian suppers to eat at home, so as not to defile the table of the Lord by greed. Thus, already in this youthful period of their existence, the agapae bore within themselves the seeds of the separation of the Eucharist from them, and subsequently the abolition of their entire material aspect, as offering occasion for such abuses; in other words, even then they were close to being transformed from a supper into Vespers.

Following the instructions in the First Epistle to the Corinthians regarding the external aspect of the agapae and of divine worship in general, the Apostle proceeds to the internal, spiritual dimension of the latter (chapters 12–14), and here paints for us a magnificent picture of that divine worship which was possible only then and never again—a divine worship truly accomplished by the Holy Spirit Himself, not only through unutterable groanings in human hearts, but also through His outward manifestation in a whole array of diverse spiritual gifts (*πνευματικὰ χαρίσματα*), inasmuch as "the grace of the Holy Spirit in the primitive
