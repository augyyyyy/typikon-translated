=== LEAF p251 ===
The Sunday following upon Pascha was likewise marked by a special celebration in other churches. In St. Gregory the Theologian, St. John Chrysostom, and Blessed Augustine there are discourses and homilies upon this day; in the first two they are inscribed: 'Upon the New Sunday' (*καινὴ* or *διακαινήσιμος*), and in the latter 'Upon White Sunday' (*dominica in albis*). It is unknown whether these inscriptions belong unto the authors themselves, and whether in this epoch there arose the custom which gave occasion unto the latter appellation—namely, that the newly baptized (*neophytes*) from this Sunday exchanged the white garments placed upon them at baptism for ordinary attire. [^1359]

### The Ascension

Upon the Ascension the earliest homilies are in St. Gregory of Nyssa and St. John Chrysostom. Blessed Augustine testifieth that the festival is honored everywhere, and Socrates styleth it a 'universal (*πάνδημος*) festival.' [^1360] The *Apostolic Constitutions* designate the Ascension as a festival free from labor. [^1361] Unto the establishment of the festival the erection by Empress Helena upon the Mount of Olives of a magnificent basilica [^1362] (destroyed by the Saracens) could have contributed; besides it, upon the very place of the Ascension there was a church [^1363] (which existeth even now). Yet it is remarkable that upon the Mount of Olives, judging from the description of the Aquitanian pilgrim lady, no service was celebrated upon this festival, which by the pilgrim lady is not even styled 'the Ascension,' but 'the 40th day following upon Pascha.' In anticipation of this day, already upon Wednesday in the Bethlehem cave of the Nativity of Christ there was a vigil, unto which they proceeded from Jerusalem 'after the sixth hour' (= 12 o'clock midday); upon the following day in this same place there was a Liturgy with appropriate homilies by the presbyters and the bishop, so that they returned unto Jerusalem late. [^1364]

=== LEAF p252 ===
### Pentecost

By Pentecost, as also in the III century, was accounted either the fifty-day period from Pascha, or the concluding day thereof, the Day of Pentecost. Pentecost in the sense of a period was a season of unceasing festival and joy. [^1365] Therefore during this period, as also upon Sundays, fasting and kneeling at prayer were prohibited: [^1366] 'Throughout all the fifty days of Pentecost neither doth genuflection take place, nor is fasting enjoined *(Orig. p. 264)* (*οὔτε προσετάκται* [al. *προστέτακται*]).' [^1367] Yet this custom did not as yet possess universal dissemination. 'Since there are certain persons who bend the knee upon the Lord's Day and during the days of Pentecost, therefore, in order that all things may be observed uniformly across all dioceses, it hath seemed good unto the Holy Council that prayers be offered unto God standing.' [^1368] Blessed Augustine saith: 'I know not whether it be observed everywhere that one prayeth standing during these days (of Pentecost) and upon all Sundays.' [^1369] According unto the testimony of St. John Cassian, in Egypt 'throughout all the days of Pentecost no one whatsoever bendeth the knee or observeth a fast until the ninth hour,' yet Cassian had not seen 'that this was observed with such strictness in Syrian monasteries'; and in Egyptian monasteries the abolition of fasting during Pentecost was expressed only in this: that 'food was partaken of at the sixth hour (= 12 o'clock midday) instead of the ninth hour, while the quantity and quality thereof were not altered.' [^1370] According unto the words of the Aquitanian pilgrim lady, in Jerusalem 'from Pascha unto Pentecost absolutely no one fasteth, not even the *apotaktites* (monks)'; yet the service there during Pentecost did not differ from the customary non-fasting office, except on Wednesday and Friday, when the stational procession (*proceditur*) [al. *oriceditur*] customary upon these days unto Sion was performed in the morning, since there was no fast; and there took place 'a Mass (Liturgy?) according unto its order.' [^1371]

Pentecost in the strict sense was accounted a festival solely in honor of the Holy Spirit. 'Honor the Day of the Spirit,' exhorteth St. Gregory the Theologian in his oration upon this festival. [^1372] Perhaps in view of this and by virtue of the connection between baptism and chrismation there existed the custom, mentioned already in the III century by Tertullian, [^1373] of administering baptism at Pentecost. In Blessed Augustine there is an address upon Pentecost unto the newly baptized, wherein he saith unto them, among other things: 'That which you see taking place here before you upon the altar of God, you have already seen also in the preceding night'; [^1374] consequently, in the night preceding the festival there was a vigil at which baptism was administered. Emperor Theodosius the Younger prohibiteth theatrical spectacles 'at Pascha and Pentecost, so long as the white baptismal garments are worn and the commemoration of the apostolic suffering (the Acts of the Apostles) is read.' [^1375] In the Jerusalem Church Pentecost possessed, according unto

=== LEAF p253 ===
the description of the Aquitanian pilgrim lady, an eminent divine service as well. On this day, saith the pilgrim lady, 'there is great labor for the people.' The festival openeth, however, with the customary Sunday Matins with the identical Gospel in the Church of the Anastasis, beginning, as doth daily Matins, from the crowing of the cocks. With the advent of morning in the Martyrium the Liturgy is celebrated with homilies by the presbyters and the bishop; *(Orig. p. 265)* by reason of the impending stational processions the dismissal is hastened and takes place before the third hour (= 9 o'clock in the morning). Immediately following upon the dismissal the people escort the bishop with hymnody unto Sion, where in the church upon the place of the descent of the Holy Spirit the passage from the Acts of the Apostles concerning this event is read, and homilies are delivered by the presbyters upon the same theme; 'then according unto its order the Mass takes place, and an offering is made here also (*offertur*—the Liturgy).' The people are dismissed for the midday meal. Following upon the meal divine worship takes place upon the Mount of Olives—namely, first of all Vespers (though not so named) upon the Imbomon (the place of the Ascension):

> 'There sit down [^1376] the bishop, the presbyters, and all the people; readings take place, and interspersed there are sung hymns and antiphons suited unto the day and place; the prayers which are inserted always possess such content as fitteth the day and place (concerning the descent of the Holy Spirit); that passage from the Gospel is likewise read wherein the ascension of the Lord into heaven following upon the Resurrection is recounted; [^1377] upon the conclusion the catechumens are blessed, then the faithful, and they descend thence at the ninth hour and with hymns proceed unto the Eleona Church over the cave wherein the Lord sat and taught the Apostles. Upon arriving there already after the tenth hour (= 4 o'clock in the afternoon) Vespers (*lucernare*) is celebrated there; prayer is offered; the catechumens are blessed, and thereafter the faithful also (this is properly speaking only a stational litany unto the Vespers celebrated upon the Imbomon). [^1378] Thence all the people unto the last man descend with the bishop with the singing of hymns and antiphons appropriate unto this day, and proceed slowly toward the Martyrium. When they approach the city gates it is already night (after 6 o'clock in the evening), and they bring forth to meet the people a multitude of church lanterns; and since it is rather far from the gates unto the Martyrium, they arrive thither at approximately the second hour of the night (= 7 o'clock in the evening), for they proceed slowly and that entirely for the sake of the people, lest they become exhausted. All the people with the bishop enter with hymns into the Martyrium through the great doors which open from the side of the public square. Upon entering into the church, hymns are sung, prayers are pronounced, the catechumens are blessed, and after them the faithful (the second stational litany unto the Vespers begun upon the Imbomon). And thence once again with hymns they proceed into

=== LEAF p254 ===
the Anastasis; and when they arrive in the Anastasis, in like manner hymns and antiphons are sung, prayer is pronounced, the catechumens are blessed, and after them the faithful (the third stational litany unto the same Vespers). The like is performed at the Cross (the fourth stational litany). And thence once again the entire Christian populace unto a single man escort the bishop with hymns unto *(Orig. p. 266)* Sion; when they arrive thither there take place appropriate readings, psalms and antiphons are sung, prayer is offered, the catechumens are blessed, after them the faithful, and the dismissal takes place (the fifth stational litany, the most solemn, with readings—that is to say, doubtless an entire Vespers). Following upon the dismissal all approach the hand of the bishop, and then each returneth unto his own home at approximately midnight.' [^1379]

Thus the divine service of Pentecost in the Jerusalem Church of the IV century in the number of its stational processions and duration recalleth the service of Great Thursday night into Friday, save that the former occupied an entire day, whereas the latter took place at night.

### The Week of Pentecost and the Fast Thereafter

The *Apostolic Constitutions* enjoin celebrating Pentecost for an entire week. [^1380] Yet in the Jerusalem Church, according unto the words of the Aquitanian pilgrim lady, 'from the day following upon Pentecost all fast according unto custom, as throughout the entire year, each as he is able, excepting Sabbath days and Sundays'; [^1381] and the customary divine services are restored. 'Following upon it (Pentecost) fast for one week, for it is just that you should both rejoice concerning the gift of God and fast following upon the relaxation,' [^1382] enjoin the *Apostolic Constitutions*. In this manner there arose in this very epoch the beginning of the Apostles' Fast (Peter's Fast), as a solemn restoration of the customary fasts of the year following upon their fifty-day abolition.

### The Immovable Feasts

Having elaborated almost unto its present composition the cycle of the most important movable feasts, the period under consideration accomplished less for the immovable feasts; the most significant development in this latter regard was the introduction of three new festivals: the Nativity of Christ, the Meeting of the Lord, and the Exaltation of the Cross.

### The Nativity of Christ and Theophany

=== LEAF p255 ===
The festival of the Nativity of Christ became segregated from Theophany (*Epiphany*) by reason of the fact that this latter festival embraced an excessively complex series of commemorations. The destinies of these two festivals are intimately connected, and they share a common history. — Having originated in the III century, but not possessing at that time universal dissemination, [^1383] the festival of Theophany (*τὰ θεοφάνεια* or *ἐπιφάνεια*) was now introduced into the most remote lands: it is mentioned in Thrace in 304 AD, in Gaul in 362 AD, and in Spain in 380 AD; [^1384] only the Donatists in Africa rejected it as an innovation. [^1385] By a law of circa 400 AD circus performances were banned upon Theophany, yet from judicial proceedings this day was rendered exempt *(Orig. p. 267)* only under Justinian. [^1386] Yet not everywhere were identical commemorations attached unto this festival. According unto the *Apostolic Constitutions* (Syria) [^1387] and the most ancient Coptic calendar [^1388] this festival was dedicated unto the Baptism of the Lord. [^1389] Blessed Augustine in his six sermons upon this festival speaketh solely of the adoration of the Magi. [^1390] For almost the entire East until the close of the IV century, however, it was a festival in honor of the Nativity of Christ. [^1391] From the description of Theophany in Jerusalem in the Aquitanian pilgrim lady it is evident that it was a festival in honor of the Nativity of Christ. The opening of the description hath not been preserved, and the surviving portion thereof speaketh already of the return of the stational procession unto Jerusalem from Bethlehem with the chanting of **'Blessed is He that cometh in the Name of the Lord'**; the procession approached Jerusalem already before dawn; consequently, throughout the entire night preceding the festival there was a vigil in the Bethlehem church, which upon the departure of the procession was continued there by the clergy and monastics; the procession with the bishop entered into the Anastasis, 'where countless lamps were already burning; there a psalm is sung, prayer is pronounced, and first the catechumens and then the faithful receive a blessing from the bishop.' At the second hour (= 7 o'clock in the morning) the Liturgy takes place in the Martyrium with a homily and readings, and following upon it they proceed with singing into the Anastasis, and the dismissal takes place at approximately the sixth hour (= 12 o'clock midday). Vespers is the daily office. Upon the second and third days of the festival the service is likewise held (*proceditur*) in the Martyrium (upon Golgotha) of equal duration until the sixth hour and of equal joy (*laetitia*); upon the fourth day they celebrate in like manner (*celebrantur*) in the Eleona Church, upon the fifth day in the Church of Lazarus, upon the sixth on Sion, upon the seventh in the Anastasis, and upon the eighth again in the Martyrium. Throughout all these days an equally solemn service is celebrated in Bethlehem also by the local clergy and monastics resident there. The pilgrim lady noteth the lavish adornment of the churches upon this festival:

> 'There one seeth nothing save gold, precious gems, and silk; whether thou lookest upon the vestments, they are of pure silk embroidered with gold; whether thou lookest upon the curtains, they are likewise of pure silk embroidered with gold (consequently, the temple was draped with fabrics, as is done at present in St. Peter's Basilica in Rome for solemnities); and the vessels all

=== LEAF p256 ===
brought forth upon this day are of gold, embellished with gems.' [^1392]

That this festival, which the pilgrim lady in another passage styleth 'Theophany' (*epiphania*), [^1393] was dedicated unto the Nativity of Christ, was apparent from the intimate and extensive connection of the festal *(Orig. p. 268)* divine worship with Bethlehem, as well as from the fact that forty days following upon this festival the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*) was commemorated. [^1394]

Not in Jerusalem alone by this epoch (the close of the IV century) had the Nativity of Christ as it were overshadowed the other commemorations associated with the festival of Theophany. Such was the case throughout the entire East. St. Epiphanius of Cyprus demonstrateth (in controversy with the Alogi) that the birth of the Saviour occurred on January 6, and His Baptism on November 8. [^1395] According unto the testimony of St. John Cassian, in Egypt under Theophany 'they understand the Baptism of the Lord and His Nativity according unto the flesh, and for this reason celebrate the solemnity of both mysteries not upon two separate days, as in western regions, but upon a single day.' [^1396] The Armenian Church even unto this day celebrateth the Nativity of Christ on January 6. Even after the East, under western influence, separated the Nativity of Christ from Theophany, men continued to apply unto the former the designation of Theophany. St. Basil the Great saith concerning the Nativity of Christ: 'Let us designate this festival of ours Theophany.' [^1397] St. Gregory the Theologian saith: 'Now we celebrate Theophany, or the feast of the Nativity.' [^1398]

Earliest of all did the festival of the Nativity of Christ separate from Theophany in the Roman Church. According unto the testimony of John, Bishop of Nicaea (IX century), based upon grounds unknown, this church began to celebrate the Nativity of Christ on December 25 under Pope Julius (337–352 AD). [^1399] In a composite calendar-chronographic monument brought down unto the year 354 AD and bearing diverse titles (*Anonymus Cuspiniani*, *Catalogus Bucherianus*, *Calendarium Furii Philocali*), [^1400] in the section *Depositio martyrum* (the demise days of the martyrs) at the eighth of the Kalends of January (= December 25) there is indicated 'the day of the birth of Christ in Bethlehem,' while in the section *Annales* there is the note: 'In the consulate of Caesar Augustus and Aemilius Paulus, Christ was born on the eighth of the Kalends of January, on a Friday, on the 15th day of the new moon.' Consequently, the Nativity of Christ in Rome came to be celebrated on December 25 prior unto 354 AD, and doubtless long before that year. Pope Liberius (352–366 AD), upon consecrating as a nun Marcellina, the sister of St. Ambrose of Milan, pointed out unto her the significance in the coincidence of this event *(Orig. p. 269)* of her life with the day of the Nativity of the Saviour from the Virgin, and in doing so mentioned the miracles of the changing of water into wine at Cana and the feeding of the four thousand. [^1401] This demonstrateth that, on the one hand, in

=== LEAF p257 ===
Rome there existed already an independent festival of the Nativity of Christ not identical with Theophany (with which latter St. Hippolytus of Rome connecteth the commemoration of the Baptism alone), [^1402] while on the other hand that this festival was still associated in the consciousness of Roman Christians with Theophany (the miracle at Cana and the miracle of the feeding being Theophany commemorations). [^1403] The earliest discourse upon the Nativity of Christ *(Orig. p. 270)* among Western fathers is that of St. Zeno of Verona (†380 AD). — In the East the festival first became known in Cappadocia: unto St. Basil the Great (†379 AD) there already belongeth, as we have seen, a homily inscribed 'Upon the Holy Nativity of Christ'; it may be that unto him belongeth also the initial introduction of this festival in the East. [^1404] The friend of Basil the Great, St. Gregory the Theologian, planted the festival in Constantinople. When following upon the death of Valens and the accession unto the throne of Theodosius the Great the Orthodox in Constantinople breathed freely from Arian oppressions, they received in the person of the Isaurian solitary Gregory the Theologian, formerly Bishop of Sasima, their first bishop; his ministry he began in a private church which he named Anastasia; in it he for the first time in Constantinople in 379 AD on December 25 celebrated the feast of the Nativity of Christ. [^1405] Thus the first act of the restoration of Orthodoxy in the capital was the introduction of the new festival. The activity of St. Gregory the Theologian in Constantinople was of brief duration (until 381 AD); upon his departure his innovation could have been discontinued, and therefore it is not surprising that one account, albeit rather late (in John of Nicaea, IX century), assigneth the introduction of the feast of the Nativity of Christ in Constantinople unto the era of Emperor Arcadius (395–408 AD): Emperor Honorius, having visited his mother and brother, inclined them toward establishing this festival after the example of Rome. [^1406] In Antioch St. John Chrysostom, on the commemoration of the Martyr Chrysogonus [al. St. Philogonius] on December 20, 386 or 388 AD, [^1407] stated that on the approaching December 25 the Nativity of Christ would be celebrated here for the first time—that in the West this festival had been known from antiquity, whereas unto Antioch reports concerning it had penetrated approximately ten years previously; that he himself had long desired and even secretly prayed that this festival might be celebrated in Antioch as well. [^1408] The festival which arrived a few days thereafter gathered a multitude of people, and in his homily unto them Chrysostom again touched upon the history of the feast. He stated that concerning the festival there had been much contention in Antioch, some regarding it as an innovation, while others pointed out that it had anciently been celebrated in the West from Thrace unto Cadiz; upon three grounds Chrysostom exhorteth his hearers unto veneration of the festival: (a) by reason of the rapidity wherewith it was spreading:

> 'Although ten years have not yet elapsed since this day became known unto us, nevertheless, as if delivered unto us from antiquity and many years ago, it hath so flourished through your zeal; for even as noble

=== LEAF p258 ===
plants, when planted in the earth, quickly attain unto great height and are laden with fruit, so also this day... hath suddenly so grown and brought forth so great fruit, that, as may now be seen, our court is filled, and the entire church is thronged with a multitude of people'; (b) that December 25 is the day of the Nativity of the Saviour, *(Orig. p. 271)* according unto Chrysostom, is confirmed by the documents preserved in Rome concerning the census under Augustus; (c) this is apparent also from the fact that the conception of the Saviour according unto the Evangelist Luke followed six months after the apparition of the Archangel Gabriel unto Zechariah, and this latter must have taken place in the month of Tishri (= September) upon the Day of Atonement; [^1409] and if the conception of the Saviour was in March, then His Nativity must fall in December. [^1410] — Somewhat later the new festival received recognition in two other centers of the Christianity of that epoch: Alexandria and Jerusalem. Whereas during the stay of Cassian in Egypt (circa 380 AD) there was as yet no feast of December 25 there, [^1411] in 432 AD (in the patriarchate of St. Cyril of Alexandria) in Alexandria a sermon upon the Nativity of Christ was delivered on the 29th day of the Egyptian month of Choiak (= December 25) by the visiting Bishop Paul of Emesa. [^1412] — As regards Jerusalem, where, as we have seen, the Aquitanian pilgrim lady (circa 385 AD) celebrated the Nativity of Christ on January 6, according unto the testimony of Basil of Seleucia, 'Juvenal (425–458 AD, Bishop of Jerusalem), who now occupieth the glorious and weighty throne of James, began to celebrate the day of the Nativity of the Lord.' [^1413] In accord with these trustworthy reports are in general also the later traditions, embellished in favor of the Roman Church and not free from anachronisms, [^1414] concerning the introduction of the feast of the Nativity of Christ in the East. According unto one of these traditions, Bishop Cyril of Jerusalem, according unto another, Juvenal, in an epistle unto Pope Julius complained of the inconvenience of celebrating the Nativity of Christ and the Baptism upon the same day: 'One cannot go at one and the same time unto Bethlehem and unto the Jordan,' and requested that he examine and determine the day of the Nativity of Christ; the Pope recommended the practice of his own church—the celebration of the Nativity of Christ on December 25. St. Basil the Great commissioned St. Gregory the Theologian to establish this celebration at the Council of Constantinople. St. John Chrysostom likewise gave his consent unto this. From Cyprus Epiphanius was summoned, that he, as a native Jew, might confirm the day upon the basis of the Scriptures. Patriarchs Theophilus of Alexandria and Flavian of Antioch accepted the new festival. In view of such unanimity, even heretical sects followed in this instance the Catholic Church. — Unto the majesty of the commemorated event the new festival was of course indebted for its success. Yet its introduction required no little time and did not occur without struggle. Already St. Epiphanius of Cyprus, who accounted the day of the Saviour's birth to be January 6, upon the basis of the observation of St. Luke that at His Baptism the Saviour was about thirty years of age

=== LEAF p259 ===
[^1415] persistently *(Orig. p. 272)* proveth ('many times have I been compelled to say') that the Nativity of the Saviour could not have been upon the same day of the month as His Baptism [^1416]. Against this identification Chrysostom likewise objecteth: 'Not that day wherein the Saviour was born ought one to style Theophany (*ἐπιφάνεια*), but that wherein He was baptized; not following upon His birth did He become known unto all, but when He was baptized' (there followeth a citation of John 1:33); [^1417] and in passing Blessed Jerome remarketh: 'In His Nativity the Son of God appeared unto the world secretly, but in His Baptism manifestly.' [^1418] The custom of celebrating the Nativity of Christ and the Baptism upon a single day was not abandoned in certain localities in the East even in the VI century. Cosmas Indicopleustes (circa 530 AD) remarketh that whereas the Nativity of Christ is everywhere celebrated in December, the Jerusalemites alone 'by a credulous conjecture (*στοχασμοῦ*)' celebrate it together with Theophany. [^1419]

With the segregation of the Nativity of Christ into a distinct festival, in the East there remained for Theophany the sole commemoration of the Baptism. For the *Apostolic Constitutions* Theophany is a festival exclusively in honor of the Baptism. [^1420] St. Ephrem the Syrian also in his hymns upon Theophany speaketh of the Baptism of Christ and of the catechumens. [^1421] Simultaneously with the division of the festivals men in the East must have assigned the baptism of catechumens unto January 6, in connection with which there arose at this very time the custom of a special veneration of the water sanctified on this festival for the baptism of catechumens. St. John Chrysostom in his homily upon the Baptism of the Lord saith:

> 'Christ was baptized and sanctified the nature of waters, and therefore upon this festival all, having drawn water (*ὑδреυσάмеνοι*) [al. *ὑδρεσάμενοι*] at midnight, carry it home and preserve throughout the entire year the moisture (*τὰ νάματα*) from the waters sanctified today; and there occurreth a manifest sign: the nature of those waters is not corrupted by duration of time, but for an entire year, and frequently two or three years, the water drawn today remaineth incorrupt and fresh (*ἀκεραίου καὶ νεαроῦ*) [al. *ἀκερκίου καὶ νεαроῦ*], and following upon such an interval doth not yield unto waters newly drawn from a fountain.' [^1422]

### The Meeting of the Lord

The Jerusalem Church, according unto the testimony of the Gallic pilgrim lady, already in this epoch celebrated the day of the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*). The festival possesseth no distinct name in the pilgrim lady, but is designated 'the 40th day from Theophany,' and her turn of phrase ('it is celebrated here with great honor') apparently makes it understood that in the homeland of the pilgrim lady this festival did not exist.

=== LEAF p260 ===
> 'Upon this day the service takes place in the Anastasis (not in the Martyrium, as upon the greatest festivals and as the Liturgy upon the Lord's Day), and all things are performed according unto order with the greatest solemnity, as if at Pascha (!); all the presbyters preach, and *(Orig. p. 273)* thereafter the bishop, expounding always upon that passage of the Gospel where upon the 40th day Joseph and Mary brought the Lord into the Temple.' [^1423]

### The Exaltation and Dedication of the Church of the Anastasis

In the same Jerusalem Church there now took its origin also the festival of the Finding (and Exaltation) of the Precious Cross, which stood in intimate connection with the festival of the Dedication of the Church of the Anastasis, even as these events themselves are directly linked one with another. Emperor Constantine, as soon as he became sole ruler, resolved to erect a church upon the Sepulchre of the Lord, which the pagans, according unto the testimony of Eusebius, had buried, leveled with the ground, and erected over it a temple of Venus; the cave of the Sepulchre was discovered deep beneath the earth, and over it a magnificent temple was constructed. [^1424] Eusebius recounteth also the visit by Constantine's mother, St. Helena, unto the holy places, and her lavish gifts unto the churches erected by her son in Bethlehem and upon the Mount of Olives. Concerning the finding of the Cross Eusebius makes no mention. Of the latter Socrates and Sozomen already speak. [^1425] According unto their narrative, they determined which was the Cross of the Lord among the three discovered by the miracle of healing wrought through laying it upon a terminally ill woman, while according unto later accounts (for example, in St. Paulinus of Nola, VI century) [^1426] by the miracle of a resurrection from the dead. Socrates erroneously placeth the finding of the Cross after the Council of Nicaea, since Helena reposed in the year of the latter; more accurately the year of the event is indicated by the Alexandrian Chronicle: the year 320; the chronographer Theophanes dateth it yet earlier. Beyond Jerusalem the festival in this epoch scarcely had any dissemination; no one mentioneth it: it could exist perhaps in churches that had received a portion of the Precious Wood (for example, in Constantinople); an ecumenical veneration it began to receive, doubtless, only upon the restitution unto Emperor Heraclius of the portion of the Wood seized by the Persian King Chosroes. The day of this latter event, May 3, came in the West to be celebrated earlier (from the VII century) than September 14. [^1427]

Understandably, in Jerusalem the day of the Finding (Exaltation) of the Cross was a supreme festival in an epoch when the memory of the event was still fresh. Here is the description of the festival in the pilgrim lady:

> 'The Day of Dedications (*Encaenia*) is the name given unto that season when the holy church upon Golgotha, which is called the Martyrium, was consecrated unto God; and the holy church in the Anastasis—that is to say, in that place where the Lord rose following upon His Passion—it also was consecrated on this day unto God. The Dedication of these holy churches is celebrated with supreme honor
