Unto another passage of the *Didascalia*, however, where it speaketh of honoring the Lord's day, the *Apostolic Constitutions* make no addition concerning Saturday, although they likewise rework it (the text of the *Didascalia* is marked in italics): "On the Lord's day of the Lord, that is, on the *Kyriakē* (Κυριακή), assemble steadfastly, giving thanks unto God and confessing wherewith God hath favored you through Christ" [^504]. The *Testamentum of Our Lord Jesus Christ* appointeth for Saturday a liturgy with mystagogy, as for Sunday, Pascha, Pentecost, and Epiphany [^505], and prescribeth that at the Eucharist on *(Orig. p. 114)* Saturday three loaves be offered in the image of the Trinity, and on Sunday four—in the image of the Gospel [^506].

#### Wednesday and Friday

Among the other days of the week, Wednesday and Friday continued in the 3rd century to be honored with fasting. The true "gnostic" according unto Clement "knoweth the riddle (*ainigma* [printed: *μύνημα*]) of fasting on those days, that is, on Wednesday and Friday" [^507]. According unto Origen, "we have the 4th and 6th days of the week, on which we are accustomed to fast" [^508]. Origen prizeth Friday especially highly, juxtaposing it with Sunday [^509], and even with Pascha and Pentecost [^510]. The *Syriac Didascalia* speaketh of the fast on Wednesday and Friday in connection with the Paschal fast (in the section on fasting): "He said unto us, teaching us: 'Do ye fast for My sake in these days, or have I need that ye should afflict your soul? Nay, for the sake of your brethren (the Jews—for the pardon of their sin against Christ) have ye done this, and so do ye in the days wherein ye fast—on Wednesday and on Friday—continually, as it is written in Zechariah: the fast of the fourth day and the fast of the fifth, that is, Friday' [^511]."

The *Canons of Hippolytus* regard those who do not fast on Wednesday and Friday as adversaries of God, Who fasted for our sake [^512]. The 69th Apostolic Canon already threateneth a cleric with deposition and a layman with excommunication for non-observance of these fasts.

The *Testamentum*, in the prescription that "the sacrifice (Eucharist) must be offered on Saturday and Sunday only and on a day of fasting" [^513], apparently hath in view the weekly fasts.

#### Fasting in General

This latter passage showeth that fasting was viewed as a kind of feast, a solemnity. Yet there existed also an opposite tendency in the view of fasting, mentioned disapprovingly by Tertullian, which leaned toward the opinion that the fast is broken by the reception of the Eucharist: "Many think that on the days of the stations [^514] they ought not to attend the prayers of the sacrifices, *(Orig. p. 115)* because upon receiving the Body of the Lord the station would be broken. What then? Doth submission unto the Eucharist release a man from a vow unto God? Will not thy station be more solemn

=== LEAF p112 ===
if a man stands at the altar of God? When the Body of the Lord hath been received and reserved [^515], then both will be salvific—both participation in the sacrifice and the fulfillment of duty. If the station received its name from a military example (for we also are soldiers of God), then indeed no joy or sorrow occurring in military camps breaketh the station of the soldiers; for joy makes the fulfillment of discipline more pleasant, and sorrow makes it more diligent" [^516].

The fast continued until the ninth hour. The Orthodox reproached the Montanists, among other things, for extending the day's fast beyond this hour. "They (the Orthodox) look upon our stations as unworthy," saith Tertullian, having become a Montanist; "some of them (the stations) they even accuse of novelty, as having been introduced later, saying also that this duty (of fasting) cannot be performed arbitrarily and prolonged beyond the 9th hour, asserting this, of course, on the ground of their custom" [^517].

Apart from Wednesday and Friday and the Paschal fast, of which we shall speak presently, other fasts did not yet exist; yet public fasts on occasion of calamities, such as drought, were customary. Such fasts are touchingly described by Tertullian. "When there are no rains, and famine threateneth in consequence," saith Tertullian unto the pagans, "ye, daily sated and ever ready to eat..., seek heaven at the Capitol and await clouds from the ceilings, having turned away from God Himself and from heaven itself. But we, dried up with fasts, enfeebled with abstinence, withdrawn from every pleasure of life, clad in sackcloth and sprinkled with ashes, knock at heaven and touch God; and when we have obtained mercy, Jupiter is honored" [^518].

#### Prolongation of the Paschal Fast

The forty-day fast before Pascha, which replaced in the 3rd century—though not yet everywhere—the earlier, shorter fast, in its origin and character hath something in common with the fasts of this latter kind. If it did not arise, it at least lengthened unto 40 days undoubtedly under the influence of the practice of the catechumenate, from the desire to cooperate through fast and prayer in the preparation of catechumens for baptism. Concerning the custom of fasting with the catechumens, monuments of the 2nd century already speak. According unto the words of St. Justin, those converting unto Christianity "are taught to ask of God with prayer and fasting the remission of their former sins, and we *(Orig. p. 116)* pray and fast with them" [^519]. "Before baptism let them fast," prescribeth the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*, "the baptizer and the one being baptized, and, if they can, certain others. But command the one being baptized to fast beforehand one or two days" [^520]. "Those approaching unto baptism," saith Tertullian, "must pray with frequent prayers, with fasts, genuflections, and vigils, with confession of all former sins" [^521]. Since in the 3rd century baptism was assigned unto Pascha, this fast

=== LEAF p113 ===
turned out to be pre-Paschal. The *Testamentum* acquainteth us with the very first stage in the existence of the 40-day fast before Pascha, with its inception. It speaketh of it in such a connection. From among those receiving catechesis, the term of which is appointed by the *Testamentum* at 3 years, those already prepared for baptism are chosen and "are exorcised from that very day wherein they are chosen (apparently, daily), and are baptized on the days of Pascha. During the 40 days of Pascha the people abide in the temple watching, praying, listening to the Scriptures, hymns, and discourses of instruction" [^522]. There followeth a description of the baptism, chrismation, and communion of the newly baptized. The fast of the Forty Days (Четыредесятница / Lent), if only "40" is not a later insertion [^523], is represented here as that fellowship of the faithful in prayer and fasting with those preparing for baptism which the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* requireth and of which Justin the Martyr speaketh. Thus also according unto Tertullian, "a solemn day for baptism is Pascha, when the Passion of the Lord was accomplished, into which we are baptized... In view of the fact that the Savior fasted after His baptism 40 days, someone will say that it behooveth us rather to fast after baptism. But what restraineth, if not the necessity of joy and thanksgiving?" In these latter words of Tertullian there already clearly floateth the idea of a 40-day fast before baptism, and consequently before Pascha. (At the same time they shed light also upon the original meaning of exemptions in Pentecost from fasting and genuflection). But although the fast of the Forty Days arose in connection with baptism, already in the 3rd century it is viewed as something of intrinsic value. Such it appeareth in part in the testimony of the *Testamentum* concerning it. The *Canons of Hippolytus*, moreover, speak of it even without reference unto baptism, although immediately after the order of baptism: "On the days of fasting which are established in the canons for the fourth day of the week, and for the sixth, and the Forty Days... (corrupt) whoever joineth other fasts unto them shall receive a reward. But whoever resisteth this, and whom sickness, calamity, or necessity doth not excuse therein, resisteth God, Who fasted for our sake" [^524]. Finally, Origen makes mention of the Forty-Day Fast: "We have the days of the Forty Days consecrated unto fasting" [^525].

*(Orig. p. 117)*

Yet not in all Churches had the pre-Paschal fast acquired a 40-day duration. Concerning that same Alexandria where Origen lived and labored, and from the very era of Origen, we have testimony that the pre-Paschal fast was restricted unto several days. St. Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria (246–265), was queried by Bishop Basilides as to when the Paschal fast ought to be ended—whether on Saturday evening, or on Sunday morning at cockcrow, as was done in Rome. Having expressed his view regarding the various times for the end of the fast, the Alexandrian bishop addeth: "and the six days of the fast are not observed by all equally and alike; for some pass all the days without food, while others pass not even one; unto those who are exceedingly enfeebled by the prolonged

=== LEAF p114 ===
fast and well-nigh perish from exhaustion, an earlier taking of food is pardonable; but if others during the four preceding days of the fast not only did not fast without intermission, but even lived luxuriously, and then, when the final two days arrive—that is, Friday and Saturday—fast continuously and think that they accomplish something great and glorious if they abstain until morning, I think that they have not engaged in such an ascetic struggle as those who exercised themselves for more days" [^526].

The contradiction between Dionysius and Origen can be reconciled by the fact that Origen, having lived long in Palestine, speaketh of the practice of the Church there. The lengthening of the fast unto 40 days could originally have occurred in Palestine; and since the Church of Jerusalem enjoyed great authority, from thence the 40-day fast could easily have spread throughout the entire Christian world. At the very beginning of the 4th century, as we shall see, we find it already everywhere; consequently it must have arisen no later than the 3rd century [^527]. A transition from *(Orig. p. 118)* a one-week fast unto a 40-day fast could have been a two- or three-week fast—a term unto which the pre-Paschal fast was limited in some places even in the 4th century, according unto the testimony of the historian Socrates [^528]. A three-week fast is prescribed by the *Testamentum* for a bishop and presbyter after their ordination [^529].

#### Pascha According to 3rd-Century Writers

Pascha in the 3rd century continued to remain that sorrowful-joyful fast in honor of Christ's Passion which, according unto the testimony of St. Irenaeus, it was in the 2nd century; but the termination of this fast on the night after Saturday is now invested with special solemnity and thus giveth beginning unto Pascha with its present character, although it is not yet fully separated from the preceding fast.

Characteristic is the complete silence concerning fasting in such a writer as Clement of Alexandria. Unto Origen's heart as well the feast of Pascha speaketh little. Explaining the prescriptions of the Book of Exodus concerning the institution of the Passover and the Gospel passages concerning the Savior's celebration of the Old Testament Passover, Origen makes no mention whatever of an analogous Christian feast, insisting solely upon the thought that our Pascha is Christ [^530]. Yet Origen nevertheless twice makes mention of the Christian feast of Pascha. Proving unto Celsus that Christianity is higher not only than pagan, but also than Jewish feasts, Origen remarketh: "If anyone object that among us also something similar is done on the Lord's day (Sunday), Friday, Pascha, and Pentecost, one may answer that the perfect Christian, abiding in words, deeds, and thoughts with the Word, God by nature, ever liveth on Sunday, ever passeth Sundays (the Lord's days)" [^531]. Another mention by Origen of Pascha we have seen above [^532]. Tertullian knoweth, apparently, only a fasting Pascha. The words of God at the institution of the Old Testament Passover, "it is the Lord's passover" [Ex. 12:11],

=== LEAF p115 ===
Tertullian explaineth: "that is, the Passion of Christ" [^533]. Although in one passage Tertullian expresseth himself concerning Pascha that "we celebrate (*celebramus*) it annually in the first month" [^534], this "celebration" in other passages he reduceth unto a vigil and strict fasting. Demonstrating unto a Christian woman the inconvenience of having a pagan husband, Tertullian saith: "at the solemnities (*solennibus*) of Pascha will he bear with indifference thy *(Orig. p. 119)* absence by night (*abnoctantem*)?" [^535]. Disapproving the custom of abstaining in fasts from the kiss customary at prayer, Tertullian remarketh: "but on the day of Pascha, when fasting is a common and as it were public religious duty, we rightly abstain from the kiss of peace" [^536].

#### Pascha According to Canonical Monuments of the 3rd Century

Of the canonical monuments of the 3rd century, the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* speak of Pascha more briefly than the others. Apparently, the prescription concerning Pascha in one canon collection constituteth a reworking of the other, or both have behind them a common original source. Therefore a comparison of the passages pertaining hereto from both monuments is not without interest for the history of the feast.

##### The Canons of Hippolytus

For both monuments Pascha is a fast in honor of Christ's Passion, but for the former into its concept there enters already the solemn release from the fast in the night of the Resurrection.

Another and, apparently, more complex phase in the development of the Paschal ritual is represented by the *Syriac Didascalia*. Remarkable is the tenderness and animation wherewith it speaketh of Pascha, which are absent from the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*. Interesting likewise is that redaction unto which the prescriptions of the *Didascalia* concerning Pascha were subjected in the *Apostolic Constitutions*. Besides the fact that the latter supplement Pascha with an entire cycle of new feasts and fasts—the Nativity of Christ, Theophany, the Forty-Day Fast, Thomas Sunday, Ascension, Pentecost, and the one-week fast after Pentecost (the Fast of the Holy Apostles / Peter's Fast)—the very tone of the prescriptions of the *Didascalia* concerning Pascha in the *Apostolic Constitutions* hath changed, and not everywhere for the better.
