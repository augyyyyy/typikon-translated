### History of the Hour
The Psalms of the Sixth Hour already in the most ancient manuscript Horologia coincide with the present ones. The same is true of the Lenten troparia following the Psalms. Only in the most ancient Sinaitic Horologion (Sin. gr. 863, 8th–9th c.) is this troparion, although close in text to the present one, more extensive; at *Glory...* another troparion is appointed according to the day of the week. Troparia after the Trisagion are absent in some Horologia (the same ones as for the other hours). The Horologia of the Moscow Synodal Typography Library (No. 46, 14th c., and No. 48, 15th c.) lack the second troparion; Sin. gr. 865 (12th c.) does not prescribe for Wednesday and Friday the special Theotokion **"Most praised art Thou"** [*Preproslavlena yesi*]. ## THE VARIABLE PARTS OF THE DIVINE LITURGY (SUNDAY)

The Divine Liturgy is so sacred, so "divine" a service that the Orthodox Church does not venture to alter it even for such great feasts as Pascha. It is sufficiently joyful even in its ordinary form for all feasts, for which reason during fasts it is entirely cancelled. In these respects it reflects upon itself the immutability and all-blessedness of God. Yet all this holds true only for the most essential portion of the Liturgy, for its "canon" (Anaphora), where it becomes the Mystery of the Eucharist. Only here does the Orthodox Church, in contrast to the Roman Catholic Church, not venture to alter so much as a single word for any feast. In the liturgical prayers that surround this most sacred portion, however—in the preparatory and concluding parts of the Liturgy—adaptation to the commemorations of the day is permitted and, one may say, increases in proportion to the distance of these parts from the "canon." In view of this, the beginning of the Liturgy is entirely distinct (not counting the litanies, which are identical with those of other services) for weekdays, for medium feasts, and for great feasts.

### The Typika (Typical Antiphons)
The Liturgy begins with three series of chants, rightly termed in the *Sluzhebnik* (*Hieratikon*), by reason of their manner of performance, "antiphons." It is precisely these antiphons that conform to the festive rank of the day. For Sundays and less momentous feasts, beginning with sixfold saints[^2739] up to and including the Great Twelve (*Dodekaorton*) feasts of the Mother of God, these "antiphons" are served by the so-called "Typika" (*izobrazitelnye*) and the "Beatitudes" (*Blazhenny*).

### Content of the Typical Psalms
Psalms 102 and 145 are called "Typika" (*izobrazitelnye*, τυπικά) not because, as was previously thought, they depict (*izobrazhayut*) the blessings of redemption, but rather, in all likelihood, because within the composition of the Liturgy only this part is subject to the regulation of the *Typikon* (the rule of the order), being capable of assuming varied "forms" (τύπος). These specific Psalms were chosen here because they represent an ecstatically joyful glorification arising from the depths of the soul ("Bless the Lord, O my soul," "Praise the Lord, O my soul," and likewise Ps. 103; cf. the Canticle of the Mother of God)

=== LEAF p903 ===

and thanksgiving to God (so fitting for the Eucharist) for His tender mercy, manifested especially in the forgiveness of sins and help to the afflicted. Psalm 102 *(Orig. p. 746)*, **"Bless the Lord, O my soul, and all that is within me bless His holy name,"** glorifies God for healing from mortal sickness ("Who redeemeth thy life from corruption")[^2740], and then generally for His mercies toward Israel, and for His tender mercy in general and toward sins in particular, elicited in God, among other things, by the awareness of human insignificance; in conclusion, the Psalm depicts the sovereign dominion of God (thus gradually broadening the sphere of contemplation), summoning all Angels and all creatures to the glorification of God. Psalm 145, **"Praise the Lord, O my soul,"** glorifies God as our steadfast hope, in contrast to the unreliability of even the highest-ranking men, grounding this thought upon the creative power of God, His eternal truth, His constant benefactions to all who are oppressed, and His thwarting of sinners[^2741].

### History
Among non-Orthodox liturgies, whole Psalms at the beginning are found in the Nestorian Liturgy attributed to the Apostles Addai and Mari—Psalm 14 [LXX][^2742]—and the Coptic Liturgy attributed to Basil the Great—Psalm 50 [LXX] immediately before the Gospel[^2743].

The Roman Liturgy frequently employs excerpts from the Psalms as the *introitus* (entrance verse)[^2744].

The Liturgy of the Apostle James, the prototype of our own, apparently possessed at the beginning neither Psalms nor antiphons (the manuscripts indicate none)[^2745].

The Studite-Alexian Typikon forbids the Typical Psalms and the Beatitudes at the weekday Liturgy during the Apostles' Fast (Peter's Fast)[^2746].

The Western Studite (Calabrian) and Evergetis Typika prescribe the Typika for the Sunday Liturgy[^2747].

Manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon are silent (the Slavic ones speak of the Beatitudes). Manuscripts of Euchologia of the 16th century state: "After the litany of peace, the choir chants the 1st antiphon, or the Typika if it is Sunday; but if not, of the day... After the litany of peace, the second choir chants the second antiphon, or the second Typika if it is Sunday; but if not, of the day"[^2748].

### The Order of Chanting the Typical Psalms
The order of chanting the Typical Psalms is set forth in the *Heirmologion*[^2749] and the *Horologion* in the order of the Typika (*Izobrazitelnye*). They are appointed here to be chanted in Tone 8, in all likelihood as the most

=== LEAF p904 ===

calm and solemn, wherein the troparic melody *(Orig. p. 747)* of this tone is intended, because only such is employed at the Liturgy (at the Beatitudes, the canonic melody is also used). Musical tradition diverges from this rubric: it assigns to the Typika the troparic melody of Tone 1, evidently presuming that they ought to follow the tone of the day (just as the Alleluiaria are always chanted in Tone 1). The silence of the *Horologion* regarding the tone likewise supports this supposition.

The Typika are chanted antiphonally. The distribution of verses for chanting between the two choirs is given in the *Heirmologion*. It coincides with the biblical division of verses, except that in Psalm 102, verse 6 is joined with 7, and 9 with 10, by virtue of which the total number of verses in this Psalm for antiphonal chanting amounts to 20 (instead of the 22 biblical verses); and in Psalm 145, verse 1 is joined with 2, by reason of which the verses amount to 9 (instead of the 10 biblical verses). The Psalms possess a refrain as well, but only at the beginning and the end. The refrain for Psalm 102 is the same as that for the neighboring and cognate Psalm 103 in the Bible, the Introductory Psalm of Vespers (the daily office closest to the Liturgy), namely: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord"** (Ps. 118:12 [LXX]). This refrain is appended to the opening words of verse 1: **"Bless the Lord, O my soul,"** as in the Introductory Psalm and at *Lord, I have cried*, and to the entire 1st verse, but only when this verse is repeated at the end of the Psalm, after *Glory... Both now...*. In this way, the employment of the refrain with this Psalm is exceptional, applied in exactly this manner nowhere else, which corresponds to the uniqueness of the Divine Liturgy among the cycle of services. At the same time, this usage bears the character of singular simplicity: the refrain is purely scriptural (psalmic) and most concise; at *Glory... Both now...* there are no additions complicating this refrain to the Psalms as found at other services. Antiphonally chanted are not only the verses of the Psalm, but also *Glory... Both now...*; this latter is chanted in a "higher voice," likewise half by each choir, and the first choir chants again at the end the 1st verse with the refrain (at the place indicated above), thus returning the Psalm to its beginning. Psalm 145 is chanted according to a different order. Its refrain is served solely by *Glory... Both now...*, but in such a manner that *Glory...* is chanted before the Psalm, and *Both now...* after it, accompanied by the hymn **"Only-begotten Son"** [*Yedinorodnyi Syne*], wherein the chanting here is begun by the second choir, and by virtue of this the hymn falls to the first choir.

### Historical References
The method of appending the refrain to Psalm 102 resembles the antiphon in Roman Catholic worship (at the end of the psalm the complete verse, and before the psalm two or three opening words). Concerning

### The Beatitudes: Content
After the 1st and 2nd antiphons have "depicted" the Kingdom of God (Ps. 102:19; 145:10 [LXX]) from the Old Testament perspective, the 3rd antiphon—during which the rite symbolizing the "entrance of the saints" into the Heavenly Kingdom is performed—provides the New Testament portrait of this Kingdom, naturally opening it with the prayer (of the Wise Thief, repeated also at the Great Entrance) for *(Orig. p. 748)* our admission into it. Inasmuch as there is no more vivid and touchingly joyful teaching concerning the Kingdom of God than that given by the Beatitudes, they are summoned here, elevating and intensifying the sensation of delight in the blessings of God that breathes within the Typical Psalms. At the same time, into the variable portion of the Liturgy, which is already drawing near to its end, extra-biblical hymnographic material is now introduced for the first time—in the form of special additional hymns to the verses of the Beatitudes, termed, like these verses, "Beatitudes" (*Blazhenny*, μακαρισμοί). They glorify the celebrated event, just as do the troparia of the canon, by which they are sometimes replaced, without reference to the accompanying biblical verses, with the exception of the 1st Beatitude, which, like the heirmos in the canon, bears such a reference, that is, it always speaks of the repentance of the thief crucified together with Christ.

### Melody
The Beatitudes are chanted to a special melody of the tone of the week, close to the troparic and canonic melodies. Thus, from the diverse melodies of the tone, the richest are taken into the Liturgy: the troparic, the canonic, and the prokeimenon melodies.

### Order of Chanting
For chanting, the Gospel Beatitudes, with the prayer preceding them: **"In Thy kingdom remember us, O Lord,"** and the concluding doxology to them (by analogy with the preceding Psalms), are divided into 12 verses[^2750], wherein the prayer with the 1st Beatitude is united into a single verse, while from the final Beatitude, by reason of its length, the promise is separated: **"Rejoice and be glad..."**, just as *Glory...* is separated from *Both now...*. The troparia are appended to the Gospel Beatitudes at the ordinary

=== LEAF p906 ===

Sunday Liturgy, unencumbered by other sacred commemorations, not from the 1st verse, but, like the stichera at *Lord, I have cried*, from one of the subsequent verses, specifically from the 3rd, so that in total there are 10 of them, and without *Glory... Both now...*, 8—two more than at a weekday Liturgy. When there coincides with Sunday any commemoration possessing its own Beatitudes, the number of Sunday Beatitudes is reduced to 6 and 4, while the total number of Beatitudes may increase to 12, beginning from the 1st verse. Yet Beatitudes in the proper sense are possessed only by the Octoechos. For all other commemorations, the Beatitudes are served by odes of the canon, specifically the 3rd and 6th odes, or the 3rd ode alone. Such odes in the canon were selected, in all probability, because their numbering corresponds to the Third and Sixth Hours with which the Liturgy is celebrated, or because of a correspondence in the content of the biblical canticles underlying them with the Beatitudes (consolation in affliction)[^2751]. If on Sunday there falls one commemoration possessing an ode of the canon at the Beatitudes, the Sunday Beatitudes are chanted on 6 and the 3rd ode of the canon on 4; if there are two such commemorations, the Sunday Beatitudes are on 4 and each of the odes of the canon—the 3rd and the 6th—on 4 (concerning this, see Chapters 3, 4, and 5 of the *Typikon*, in the Menologion, for example *(Orig. p. 749)* September 1 and 9, in the Triodion, and in the Pentecostarion). The odes of the canon must evidently be chanted without a heirmos, since it is replaced by the first troparion (*blazhenna*) of the Octoechos (when only odes of the canon are chanted at the Beatitudes, then it is with a heirmos; see Sunday of Antipascha at Vespers, 2nd *Mark* [*Zri*]).

### "O Come, Let Us Worship"
The conclusion of the Beatitudes, which serve, as it were, as the chant of the Little Entrance at the Liturgy, being properly the "Entrance Verse" (*introitus* / *eisodikon*), is served by the chant: **"O come, let us worship and fall down before Christ,"** a verse that opens other services as well (conceptually, the Liturgy begins from here, from the Entrance, which is why before it the bishop takes no part in it), and which is so fitting for the Entrance, with the refrain: **"Save us, O Son of God, (on Sunday:) Who art risen from the dead (on weekdays: 'Who art wonderful in the saints'; on feasts of the Mother of God: 'through the prayers of the Theotokos'), who sing unto Thee: Alleluia."** Alleluia is chanted once, as is expressly stated in the *Sluzhebnik* and as is done at the corresponding festive refrain to the 2nd antiphon, and not thrice, because the hymn refers to the Savior alone, and not to the Holy Trinity, as does, for example, the Alleluia at Lenten Matins.

### Significance of the Beatitudes

=== LEAF p907 ===

Nowhere in the Liturgy is there such a convergence of specifically festive hymns as is presented by the Beatitudes with their 8–12 troparia. This is, one may say, the most festive part of the Liturgy, especially if one takes into account their melody—the richest in melody of all the tone melodies. It is not surprising, therefore, that the Typikon sometimes cancels even the antiphons of the Great Twelve Feasts of the Lord in order to preserve the Beatitudes. Thus, if the commemoration of St. John the Theologian falls on Ascension, the antiphons of the feast are omitted and the Typical Psalms are chanted solely so that at the Entrance the memory of the saint can be honored with the ode of his canon. The same is done if the patronal feast of a saint falls even on Pentecost[^2752].

### History of Usage
The Beatitudes within the structure of the Liturgy are so closely bound up with the Typika that they could appear within it only concurrently with them, consequently comparatively late (see above). Their presence at Matins of the Great Canon, of Great Friday, and in the Order of Burial—a service so close in structure to Matins—may argue that originally they were employed at that office. It is strange that Roman Catholic worship did not utilize so edifying and artistic a page of the Gospel.

### Troparia and Kontakia
In the thought that at the entrance of the clergy into the altar there occurs the entrance thither of the saints as well, the Church naturally at this moment sings praises unto the saints, appointing after the Entrance troparia and kontakia to them. At the Divine Liturgy, sometimes (on weekdays) this is the sole place dedicated to the commemoration of the day. For this reason, from the hymns of the day the most general are chosen here, those expressing to the highest degree the essence of the celebrated event. Both troparia and kontakia are distinguished by precisely this character, often supplementing one another in content (see above, p. 689), which is why both here (and only here) are provided in combination with one another. This group of hymns endeavors to embrace all commemorations united with the day of the celebration of the Liturgy, to demonstrate that the bloodless sacrifice is offered on behalf of all and for all; on this ground, on weekdays at the Liturgy the troparion and kontakion of the day of the week are also chanted (on Monday, of the Bodiless Powers, etc.), which are chanted nowhere else—neither at Vespers, nor at Matins, nor at the Hours.

=== LEAF p908 ===

Finally, only here throughout the daily cycle of services are chanted the troparion and kontakion of the temple, in which alone the celebration of the Liturgy is possible. There results a series that is varied also in a musical respect, wherein the melody passes from one tone to another from minute to minute. Since the series of hymns by virtue of all this turns out to be lengthy, the troparia at the Liturgy (in contrast to Vespers and Matins) are not chanted more than once. The hymns are arranged according to the rank of importance of the commemorations, yet such that troparia precede kontakia. The conclusion to this series of hymns, according to the rule adopted for other cases as well, is served (in the majority of instances) by the kontakion of the Mother of God, identical for both festive and weekday Liturgies, **"O Protection of Christians"** [*Predstatelstvo khristian*], taken from the Molieben and containing an intensified supplication, united with glorification, to the Mother of God for aid and intercession (in accordance with the intercessory character of the Liturgy), which is why the kontakion is chanted in the plaintive Tone 6 (cf. **"With the saints give rest"** [*So svyatymi upokoy*], **"My soul"** [*Dushe moya*]).

In view of the fact that the order of troparia and kontakia at the Liturgy is determined by the importance of the commemorations, it cannot be identical for different temples, which is why it requires an especially complex regulation, such as is set forth in Chapter 32 of the *Typikon*. Here it is provided only for an ordinary Sunday, unencumbered by any special commemorations. The general rule of alternation is as follows: first come the resurrection hymns, then those of the temple, and finally those of the saints of the day. Yet this is adhered to strictly only for the troparia. Even for them, an exception is sometimes made from this order: since the Resurrection of Christ was the crown of His life, after the resurrection troparion and kontakion it would be superfluous to chant the troparion and kontakion to a temple of Christ[^2753] (in honor of some other event from the life of the Savior); therefore, following the resurrection troparion there is chanted only the troparion of a temple of the Mother of God or of a saint, and thereafter of the saint or saints of the day. The kontakia follow this same order, with the distinction that, since *(Orig. p. 751)* it is customary to assign *Both now...* to the Theotokion kontakion, here there is chanted, omitting its earlier turn, the kontakion of the temple if the temple is dedicated to the Mother of God; "if not," **"O Protection of Christians"**; and at *Glory...* the final kontakion is chanted—that of the saint of the day. The present passage of the *Typikon* gives no definite indication of what to chant at *Both now...* if the temple is of Christ. Chapter 52 of the *Typikon* indicates that in such a case one is to chant the resurrection kontakion at *Both now...*, transferring it hither from the 1st place. This ordinary order of chanting troparia and kontakia at the Liturgy undergoes changes indicated in other passages of the *Typikon*. Thus, in the presence of a great saint, and during forefeasts and afterfeasts, the troparion and kontakion of the temple patron saint are not chanted (Chapters 3 and 52); in the presence of a Polyeleos saint, they follow

=== LEAF p909 ===

after the saint of the day (Chapter 4); when the service to the saint of the day is cancelled or transferred, his troparion and kontakion are not chanted (Sundays preparatory to Great Lent, of Great Lent, and of the Pentecostarion).

### History of Usage
Concerning the chanting of troparia and kontakia at the Sunday Liturgy according to the Studite Typikon, nothing is known.

According to the Evergetis Typikon: "the resurrection troparion and Theotokion."

According to the Slavic (the Greek are silent) manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon: "the resurrection troparion of the tone of the week; *Glory... Both now...* O Defender [= Protection] of Christians."

According to the present Greek *Typikon*: "the resurrection troparion, of the celebrated saint, of the saint of the monastery, and the kontakion"; according to the *Hieratikon*: the kontakion **"O Protection of Christians"**, except for forefeasts, afterfeasts, and the period of the Triodion, when the kontakion of that season is chanted[^2754].

According to the Old Believer Typikon, troparia and kontakia are chanted "to the temples" (that is, to the side-altar chapels as well); to a temple of Christ only the troparion is not chanted; at *Both now...* the kontakion to the temple of the Mother of God or of Christ; in a temple of Christ, **"O Protection"** [= *Zastupnitse*] is also permitted; the Typikon does not mention the resurrection kontakion, but in practice it is chanted[^2755].

### Prokeimenon and Alleluiarion
The most important variable parts of the Liturgy are the Epistle (*Apostol*) and the Gospel. Both, in view of this, possess before them an exceedingly complex preparation, whose variable elements before the former are served by the Prokeimenon, and before the latter by the Alleluiarion.

### The Prokeimenon
The Prokeimenon is chanted at the Liturgy according to the same order as at Vespers, though not of Sunday Vespers, but of any other, that is, 2 1/2 times, and with the same preliminary exclamations, with the exception of the final "Let us attend!", in all likelihood because it occurs before the reading, and repeating it before the Prokeimenon would rank the latter higher than the reading. In contrast to Vespers, the Prokeimenon varies at the Liturgy according to the tones, yet the melody of the tone is the same as at Vespers. Being distinguished *(Orig. p. 752)* in general by an exceptional melodic richness notwithstanding its brevity, this melody is exceedingly varied for different tones, for

=== LEAF p910 ===

some assuming the most joyful coloration, as for Tone 8 (Tone 8 prokeimena of Pascha and the Nativity of Christ), and for others the most plaintive, as Tone 6 (the Prokeimenon for the departed) and Tone 7 (**"The rulers of the peoples"** [*Knyazi lyudstii*]).

### In the Western Church
In the Roman Liturgy (Mass), that which corresponds to our Prokeimenon is the *Graduale*, but it is chanted not before the Epistle (*epistola*), but after it. The *Graduale* received its name from the fact that it was chanted from that step (*gradus*) of the ambo from which the Epistle was read. It, like our Prokeimenon (see above, p. 562), replaced the chanting at this point of the Liturgy—between its scriptural readings—of an entire Psalm; the replacement occurred in the 5th–6th centuries: under Leo the Great, apparently, they still chanted an entire Psalm[^2756]—and was occasioned by the increasing complexity of the music. The order of chanting the *Graduale* closely resembles the chanting of our Prokeimenon, especially in its most ancient form; according to *Ordo Romanus II* (7th c.): "a single cantor begins the *responsum* (the 1st verse of the *Graduale*, subsequently repeated), and all in the choir respond, and likewise alone he chants the verse of the responsory; after this the choir chants again the 1st verse, and the cantor repeats it once more, but in a higher pitch; finally, the choir chants it a third time"[^2757]. The present rubric (*Graduale Romanum*, 1886) has shortened this order: two cantors begin the *responsum*, chanting from it only one or two opening words, while the choir concludes, and then two cantors chant the verse.

It is remarkable that the *Graduale* is not appointed during the period of the Pentecostarion from New Sunday (Antipascha) until Trinity Sunday; this the medieval liturgists explained by stating that in its tone it is too mournful a chant in comparison with the subsequent Alleluia, the preparatory hymn for the Gospel; it signifies the penitential preaching of John the Forerunner, whereas the Alleluia signifies the joyful Gospel of Christ; that it is nevertheless chanted during Bright Week they explained by stating that the newly baptized, who wore their white baptismal garments until Bright Saturday, were required during the course of this week to remain still in a penitential disposition[^2758]. In all probability, it was precisely the view of the *Graduale* as a mournful chant that displaced it from the specified period, without daring to touch Pascha itself.

### Sunday Prokeimena
Of the 8 Sunday Liturgical Prokeimena, only two are of supplicatory content: Tone 1, **"Let Thy mercy, O Lord, be upon us"** (Ps. 32:22 [LXX]), and Tone 6, **"Save, O Lord, Thy people"** (Ps. 27:9 [LXX]),
