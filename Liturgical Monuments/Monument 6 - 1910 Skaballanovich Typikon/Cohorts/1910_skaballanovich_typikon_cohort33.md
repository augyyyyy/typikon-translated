In this manner accentuation plays the principal role in the construction of Greek hymns. For example, in the kontakion of the Nativity of Christ:

Ἡ παρθένος σήμερον  
Τὸν ὑπερούσιον τίκτει  
Καὶ ἡ γῆ τὸ σπήλαιον

(In the translation into Church Slavonic, understandably, the rhythm is not preserved). For the purposes of this rhythm grammatical liberties are likewise permitted: thus in disyllabic particles, prepositions, and pronouns (*ἀλλά*, *παρά*, *ἐγώ*) the shifting of the accent unto the first syllable is allowed; trisyllabic words with accents upon the first or last syllable are in case of necessity recognized as possessing two accents, both upon the first and upon the last syllable (as is done likewise in Russian versification): *πόлемос*, *τάπεινος*. The poetic meter of Greek ecclesiastical hymns is confirmed likewise by the fact that many of them possess an acrostic (Slavonic: *krayegranesiye*) and rhyme; for example, in St. Romanos in the kontakion for the Nativity of Christ *σήμερον* rhymes with *σπήλαιον*, and *δοξολογοῦσι* with *ὁδοιποροῦσι* ("today" — "the cave", "they give glory" — "they travel") [^1571]. Yet it must be observed that the canons of St. John of Damascus for the Nativity of Christ (**"He saved the people"** [*Spase liudi* / *Ἔσωσε λαόν*]), Theophany (**"He traverses the sea"** [*Shestvuyet morskuyu* / *Στείβει θαλάσσης*]), and Pentecost (**"Covered by divine protection"** [*Bozhestvennym pokroven* / *Θείῳ καλυφθείς*]) are written in ancient classical *(Orig. p. 334)* meter, namely iambic trimeter, and their acrostics in dactylic hexameter (the latter being exceedingly difficult).
 
### Western Hymnography

Concerning the West, in view of the above-noted unfavorable attitude of ecclesiastical authority toward hymns during divine worship, only a few new hymns appeared in this era; their authors are either entirely unknown or doubtful (thus several hymns are attributed unto St. Gregory the Great).
 
### The Octoechos

With regard unto ecclesiastical chant, the present era accomplished especially much likewise in that only now was the cycle of liturgical chants definitively established both in the West and in the East. In the West this achievement is attributed unto Pope St. Gregory the Great; in the East, unto St. John of Damascus. According unto the words of his biographer, the Deacon John (9th century), St. Gregory assembled the melodies that had arisen across centuries in the Church, removed the less

A still later tradition attributes the ordering of ecclesiastical melodies in the East unto St. John of Damascus: unto his name and that of his teacher Cosmas is inscribed a musical treatise bearing the title 'The Hagiopolite' (*Sviatogradets*) [^1573]. As we have seen (see above, pp. 160–161), the ordering of church melodies must have begun far earlier than both Gregory the Great and John of Damascus; and tradition links the beginning of this ordering in the West with the name of St. Ambrose, whereas unto Gregory the Great is attributed solely the addition unto the 4 Ambrosian tones existing prior unto him—at the foundation of which lay the Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, and Mixolydian modes (see *ibid.*)—of four new melodies kindred unto them, the 'plagal tones' (*toni plagales*), namely: the Hypodorian, beginning upon A and having its dominant upon the 6th degree; the Hypophrygian, beginning upon B with its dominant upon the 7th degree; the Hypolydian, upon F with its dominant upon the 6th degree; and the Hypomixolydian, upon D with its dominant upon the 7th degree. ## B. The Liturgical Typikon from the Appearance of Its Complete Manuscripts

### From the 9th Century

Now it remained for the liturgical typikon to define itself only in the minutest details and particulars, and its history from this era onward must assume the character of an explanatory (*tolkovy*) Typikon. By the 9th century our Typikon had already essentially formed in its fundamental conception. Now complete manuscript copies thereof could make their appearance, which, by virtue of the preceding foundational labor, might diverge from one another only in non-essential particulars. Soon, moreover, even such divergence between them disappeared, and a single liturgical typikon arose for virtually the entire Eastern Church.
 
### The Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople and the Sung Office

Nevertheless, even now such unification was long hindered by the existence within the sphere of influence of this increasingly unified typikon—the persistence of an entirely distinct liturgical rite competing with, if not surpassing it in antiquity: an order long maintained in the principal temple of the imperial capital, Hagia Sophia of Constantinople, and in the cathedrals of other major cities. We have in mind the so-called Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople and the order of the Sung Office (*ᾀσματικὴ ἀκολουθία* / *Asmatike Akolouthia*), which is closely related unto it, if not identical with it.

The daily cycle of divine worship at Hagia Sophia of Constantinople hath remained unto this day an enigma, notwithstanding the fact that there hath come down unto us—and that in more than one redaction—a complete Typikon of this church from the 9th century [^1575], alongside several other monuments of this rite. In this Typikon there are indicated only the proper characteristics *(Orig. p. 337)* of divine worship for each day, and those mostly ceremonial; nowhere is a complete order of service provided, and exceedingly few hints are given regarding its constituent elements.

In order to comprehend these sparse intimations, one must bear in mind that the Typikon of the Great Church—that is, of Hagia Sophia of Constantinople—presupposes the services subsequently denominated the 'Sung Office' (*ᾀσματικὴ ἀκολουθία*). Its order is set forth in detail by St. Symeon, Archbishop of Thessalonica († 1430), who terms it the 'Office of the Typikon of the Great Church', adopted in all cathedral churches, yet adds that since the capture of Constantinople by the Latins (from 1204) this order had ceased to be observed even in the 'imperial city' itself, much less in other churches, and that in his day this order was preserved solely in the Cathedral of Thessalonica.

=== LEAF p324 ===
The designation 'Sung' St. Symeon explains by the circumstance that in this office 'nothing was uttered without chant (*χωρὶς μέλους*) except the prayers of the priest and the petitions (litanies) of the deacon' [^1576]. Such an order of worship could naturally develop in the magnificent cathedral of Hagia Sophia, erected in 532–538 by Emperor Justinian, which was the principal palatine church and was endowed with an extensive staff of clergy and cantors. Divine worship here could not fail to be distinguished by extraordinary solemnity and to acquire certain peculiarities in comparison not only with monasteries, but likewise with parish churches. The presence of the Emperor at divine services demanded not only solemn receptions for him, but likewise a certain adaptation of the liturgy unto him, in which he at times took an active part (performing censing, participating in entrances). In such divine worship certain elements could receive special development and elaboration, while others were relegated unto the background or even disappeared. Under such conditions divine worship could not easily admit (as it did not permit of excessive length) any additions, particularly of a monastic (ascetic) character, such as the mandatory chanting of the entire Psalter within a fixed *(Orig. p. 338)* period (kathismata and biblical canticles, that is, the canon) [^1577]. All of this we observe in the Typikon of Hagia Sophia and in the Sung Office as set forth by St. Symeon of Thessalonica. Yet it must be remembered that the order of the Sung Office given by St. Symeon is separated from the surviving Typika of the Great Church by five centuries, during which Constantinople and Hagia Sophia experienced such convulsions as devastation by the Crusaders. Following the expulsion of the latter, Hagia Sophia ceased to be a palatine church, since the emperors took up residence in another palace and attended divine worship in the Church of Blachernae, traveling unto Hagia Sophia only on solemn occasions. This could not fail to affect its typikon in the direction of diminishing solemnity and approaching the ordinary monastic-parochial order of services. In any event, the chanted order of services provided by Symeon of Thessalonica cannot be transferred in its entirety unto the practice of Hagia Sophia of the 9th–10th centuries, nor may one fill in from it the frequent silences of its contemporary Typikon. The same must be said concerning other surviving monuments of the Sung Office and rites of the Great Church, which for the most part are no older than the 15th century, such as, for example: the 'Order (*τάξις* / *taxis*) observed at Vespers, Matins, the Divine Liturgy, and the Consecration of a Bishop' in a 15th-century canonical manuscript collection of the Russian Skete of St. Andrew on Mount Athos [^1578], which hath Hagia Sophia in view, yet dates from an era later than the surviving complete Typika (it, moreover, describes exclusively patriarchal and imperial processions and contains virtually no liturgical directions); the 'Order of Sung Vespers' in a 16th-century Typikon of the Jerusalem Library [^1579], and others. (These monuments are important solely in that they may supplement Symeon of Thessalonica). Nor can the same

=== LEAF p325 ===
be said of the treatise *De Officiis Aulae Byzantinae* [*De Ceremoniis*], attributed unto Emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus (912–959), yet definitively redacted, in all likelihood, in the 11th century [^1580]: the liturgical directions occasionally provided therein are close in date unto the practice of the surviving complete Typika of the Great Church and may be adduced for their elucidation and supplementation. Nonetheless, a portion of the fragmentary indications of these Typika finds explanation in Symeon of Thessalonica and in the monuments mentioned; and, in any event, these Typika confirm the testimony *(Orig. p. 339)* of St. Symeon that the Great Church possessed a distinctive liturgical order, justly denominated 'Sung', distinct from the contemporary practice of monasteries and perhaps of other churches, and partially coinciding with the 'Sung Office' of the era of the Hierarch of Thessalonica.
 
### Vespers and Matins according to the Typika of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th–11th Centuries

Apart from the Liturgy, the Great Church of the 9th–11th centuries possessed, apparently, among its services only Vespers and Matins, occasionally (on the eves of feasts) after Vespers a *pannychis* (*παννυχίς*, an all-night vigil distinct from Matins), and during Great Lent the office of *trithekte* (*тритекти*). The Hours are not mentioned. Of the constituent elements of Vespers there is indicated, at least at times, as its beginning: **"Incline Thine ear, O Lord"** (*"Prikloni Gospodi ukho Tvoye"*), that is, Ps. 85, with which the Vespers of the Sung Office began according unto Symeon of Thessalonica; after it a certain *τὸ τελευταῖον* (in all likelihood, the final antiphon of the opening antiphons of Vespers: see below) and **"Lord, I have cried"** (invariably); after **"Lord, I have cried"** there is indicated a troparion or '3 small antiphons' (at times these occur after the prokeimenon) or immediately the *synapte* prayer with petition (*εὐχὴ συναπτὴ μετὰ αἰτήсеως*), that is, litanies, in all probability the Great Synapte or Augmented Litany with the Litany of Supplication (at times this occurs after the prokeimenon and antiphons), then the Entrance (at times), the prokeimenon, and readings (paremias); in conclusion invariably—a troparion, at times thrice with the verses of the psalm and the *perisse* (*καὶ τὴν περισσήν*, that is, the troparion in conclusion; thus on the eve of Pentecost, followed by the deacon's exclamation: **"Wisdom!"**, whereupon the reading of the Apostle begins until the pannychis) [^1581]. Still fewer are the indications regarding the composition of Matins. At it there is customarily named the *amomos* (*ἄμωμος*, the "Blameless"), that is, indubitably, Ps. 118 [the 17th Kathisma], indicated in the 1st part of Matins of the Sung Office likewise by Symeon of Thessalonica; the Blameless [Amomos] was divided into 3 antiphons, and at the third a troparion was at times appointed and an entrance took place [^1582]. Apparently on more solemn Sundays (Cheesefare Sunday, Palm Sunday, Thomas Sunday [Antipascha], Myrrhbearers Sunday) and kindred days (Lazarus Saturday, Great Saturday, from Pascha through Wednesday inclusive, the Saturday of the 2nd Week after Pascha for the commemoration of James the Brother of the Lord, at Ascension, and at Pentecost) [^1583], after the Amomos there was appointed a certain *Eulogetos* (*Εὐλογητός*, "Blessed", perhaps the verse of Ps. 118: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord"**, in an especially solemn execution or with

On immovable Great Feasts there is no mention at Matins either of the Amomos or of the *Eulogetos*; yet in place of both at the Nativity of Christ there stand 18 antiphons *σὺν τοῦ μεγάλου* ("with the Great"—in all likelihood the beginning of a certain hymn), one of two troparia, then another troparion at **"God is the Lord"**; consequently on all immovable feasts (and perhaps on certain Sundays as well, since the Matins of some of them likewise begins with the indication of a troparion at the 50th Psalm) the Amomos was not chanted and, in all probability, *(Orig. p. 340)* was replaced by another psalm. In a manuscript typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library, No. 333–391, of the 14th century (fol. 150v) there is an article under the heading: 'And these are the kathismata for feasts according to the typikon of the cathedral church', appointing one kathisma for each of the major feasts and ranks of saints (such kathismata are known likewise unto the most ancient Studite typika): for the Nativity of Christ—the 10th Kathisma; Theophany—the 16th; Transfiguration—the 12th; Exaltation—the 13th; Ascension—the 7th; Descent of the Holy Spirit—the 19th; Dormition—the 17th; Entry into the Temple—the 6th; for hierarchs and unmercenaries—the 5th; for martyrs—the 9th. It may be that these kathismata replaced the Amomos on feast days. All of this may be designated the first part of Matins according unto the ancient Sophia Typikon. From this type of Matins, provided by the Patmos manuscript of the Typikon (the earliest of the surviving copies), the first part of Matins in the Dresden manuscript (the latest, yet no later than the 10th–11th century) diverges considerably: for the Exaltation it is stated here concerning Matins: 'there is chanted *τὸ πρῶτον* (the first, the 1st antiphon?) of the day and 12 antiphons with the canon (*μετὰ καὶ τοῦ κανόνος*), and there is chanted likewise the Polyeleos and the *Benedicite* [*Благословите*]'. What is meant here by the canon, the polyeleos, and the *Benedicite* (the Canticle of the Three Holy Children?), it is difficult to say, yet scarcely do the first two terms bear their present significance. The subsequent constant, if not the most central, portion of Sophia Matins was constituted by Psalm 50; for all festal and many Sunday Matins there is invariably indicated a troparion *εἰς τὸν Ν* (that is, at Psalm 50), at times two troparia; the troparion was chanted, if not with every verse of the psalm, then at least with several; in the Dresden manuscript at the above-mentioned Matins a proper troparion is appointed for each of the first five verses of Psalm 50 and for the Doxology (*Glory...*) [^1584]. In addition unto Psalm 50, there is mentioned in this part of Matins **"Glory to God in the highest"** [^1585] (the Great Doxology). From these fragmentary and incomplete indications, naturally, it is impossible to construct an exhaustive conception of the order of Vespers and Matins at Hagia Sophia in the 9th century; nevertheless, scarcely is it accidental that no mention is made of such prayers as **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** and **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** at Vespers, nor of the opening morning psalms (the Six Psalms), the biblical canticles, and the Gospel at Matins, the more so as several of these elements are not mentioned by Symeon of Thessalonica either. Their absence is all the more remarkable given that they entered into the composition of the services, at least in certain localities, as early as the 4th century, and some not only in the East but in the West as well. Rome, No. 77 (8th–9th c.), the Cryptferratan [Grottaferrata] so-called Bessarion codex (9th c.), Sinai Library No. 958 (10th c.), Paris National Library No. 213 (11th c.), Barberini No. 88 (13th c.), and the Trebnik of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 675/371 (14th–15th c.) have 'after the prokeimenon and **"Lord, have mercy"**' the following prayers: 1) 'Prayer of the (1st) Antiphon, upon which is chanted **"By the prayers of the Theotokos"**': **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord our God, Who hast enlightened the day with the light of the sun…"** (identical with one of our present prayers of Pentecost), with the exclamation: **"For Thine it is to have mercy…"**; 2) 'Prayer of the (2nd) Antiphon, upon which is chanted **"Only-Begotten Son"**': **"O Lord, Who hast delivered us"** (identical with the prayer of Pentecost and of Great Compline), with the exclamation: **"By the favor and goodness…"**; 3) 'Prayer of the (3rd) Antiphon of the Trisagion': **"O God, great and eternal…"** (containing likewise a petition for the worthy chanting of the Trisagion), with the exclamation: **"For holy art Thou…"**; *(Orig. p. 344)* 4) 'Prayer of the Catechumens after the Trisagion': **"O God, Who knowest secret things…"**, with the exclamation: **"That they also with us [may glorify Thine all-honorable and majestic Name]…"**; 5) 'First Prayer of the Faithful': **"O Lord our God, even now…"**, with its central petition that He clothe us with the whole armor of the Holy Spirit against the spirits of wickedness, and with the exclamation: **"For unto Thee is due [all glory, honor, and worship]…"**; 6) 'Second Prayer of the Faithful': **"O Lord God, Who dwellest in light unapproachable…"**, with a petition for the enlightenment of the eyes of our souls, and with the exclamation: **"For Thine is the majesty…"**; 7) Prayer or Dismissal: **"O God, great and highest…"**, identical with our present 7th Lucernarium prayer, with the exclamation: **"For good art Thou and the Lover of mankind…"**; 8) our present Prayer of the Bowing of Heads, with the exclamation: **"Blessed and glorified be the majesty of Thy Kingdom…"** Unto this series of prayers certain monuments (the Ilitarion or Kondakarion of Sinai Library No. 956 of the 10th c., the aforementioned Euchologion of Paris National Library No. 213 of the 11th c., and the Trebnik of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 371/675 of the 14th–15th c.) append yet three more: a) 'Prayer said in the skeuophylakion of the vestry (*καταγύρων*, in the sacristy)', which is appointed to be pronounced after the exclamation **"Let us depart in peace"**, containing a petition to make us sacred vessels, with the exclamation: **"For holy art Thou…"**; b) 'Prayer said in the Great Baptistery after the conclusion of Vespers', containing thanksgiving unto God Who hath made us worthy of the lot of the saints, delivered us from the power of darkness, and translated us into the Kingdom of His love; after **"Peace be unto all"**, **"Bow your heads…"**; and furthermore c) a prayer containing thanksgiving for regeneration by water and the Spirit and a petition to preserve within us the grace of baptism, with the exclamation: **"For Thou art our God, a God Who hath mercy…"** [^1589] In this manner the faithful were accompanied by prayers upon departing from the church unto the very portico; remarkable it is that according unto the Ambrosian rite as well there are at Vespers and Matins special prayers and responsories *in baptisterio* (see above, p. 317). Concerning the prayer for the catechumens the Grottaferrata Euchologion observes: 'it must be known that the catechumens (that is, prayers for them) at Vespers do not always take place, but only on Thursday and Tuesday, and on these days not unconditionally, but when in the church there is no Liturgy or litiya'.
