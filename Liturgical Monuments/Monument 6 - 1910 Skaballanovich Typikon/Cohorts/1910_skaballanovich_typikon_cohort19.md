=== LEAF p181 ===
[^948] As a memorial of these brief anchoritic prayers there serveth among us the prayer **"Lord Jesus *(Orig. p. 191)* Christ"** (*Gospodi Iisuse Khriste*), which is appointed to be recited hundreds of times by the illiterate in place of omitted church services. As the perpetual fast among the desert-dwellers was intensified during periods of church fasts, so likewise was their customary prayer rule increased for feast days.

"They recounted concerning Abba Arsenius, that on Saturday evenings toward the Lord's Day he would stand with his back unto the sun, and lifting up his hands unto heaven, would pray until the sun began to shine in his face; after which he sat down" (a vigil truly all-night) [^949].

On Sundays, as likewise on Saturday, the anchorites customarily communicated. "The monks dwelling in the desert are burned by the venom of evil demons, and they wait with impatience for Saturday and Sunday to go unto the fountains of water, that is, to draw near unto the Body and Blood of the Lord, in order to cleanse themselves from the defilements of the wicked one" [^950]. For this purpose the anchorites went for the most part unto neighboring churches, notwithstanding their remoteness [^951]. "They recounted that Abba Sisoes of Thebes was accustomed to run in haste unto his cell at the conclusion of the church service, and they said: a demon is in him" [^952]. Concerning the communion of anchorites with reserved Gifts, St. Basil the Great saith: "all the hermits (*monazontes*) dwelling in the desert where there is no priest, keeping the communion in their dwelling, communicate themselves" [^953]. There are indications of the celebration of the Liturgy specifically for an anchorite in his cell by a presbyter. "They recounted concerning Abba Mark of Egypt: he lived for 30 years without ever going forth from his cell; unto him there customarily came a presbyter to celebrate for him the holy oblation (*poiein autōi tēn hagian prosphoran*)" [^954]. "Unto a certain anchorite there went a presbyter, celebrating for him the oblation of the Holy Mysteries (*tōn hagiōn mystēriōn tēn prosphoran*)" [^955].

##### The Anchoritic Typikon

Thus in regard both unto fasting and prayer or divine worship there gradually became established among the holy anchorites a more or less uniform practice, common rules, a single typikon. This will not appear surprising if one take into consideration that intimate and constant

=== LEAF p182 ===
intercourse wherein the desert-dwellers stood with one another, not only within the bounds of Egypt, but also the Sinaitic and Palestinian ascetics with the Egyptian; they frequently visited one another for mutual edification, while those nearest lovingly cared for one another; among them here and there luminaries of asceticism arose, becoming centers of spiritual instruction for a wide surrounding region. The anchorites constituted a single corporation, as it were a single monastery with a gradually evolving typikon and a certain organization. Beginners and the young *(Orig. p. 192)* had directors, yet the authority of the latter was wholly grounded upon immeasurable moral superiority [^956]. This was the most extensive and excellent monastery that the earth had ever beheld.

#### Anchoritic Monasteries of Ancient Christian Egypt

St. Anthony relinquished his seclusion after thirty-five years, at the second place of his ascetic labors. "Acquaintances coming unto him, seeing that he did not permit them to enter within the fortification (the abandoned one wherein he struggled), not infrequently spent days and nights in the courtyard. But since many strove and desired to imitate his ascetic life, while certain of his acquaintances came and broke down and opened the door by force, Anthony came forth as an initiate of sacred mysteries and a God-bearer from a certain sanctuary." Following the healings performed on the spot and words of edification, the venerable father "persuaded many to choose the monastic life; thus in the mountains monasteries appeared; the desert was populated by monks who had forsaken their possessions... By the power of his marvelous word numerous monasteries arose" [^957]. By the term "monastery" (*monastērion*, from *monazō*, to dwell alone; *monastēs*, one dwelling solitary) there is designated here (in Athanasius' *Life of St. Anthony*) a cell-cottage [^958] or cave [^959], as is evident from the fact that the abandoned fortification wherein St. Anthony dwelt is likewise termed his "monastery" [^960], as is further evident from the remark that before St. Anthony "monasteries were few in Egypt" [^961], and from the comparison of a monastery with a tent [^962]. When St. Anthony withdrew unto the final site of his ascetic labors, there soon arose there likewise an entire colony of monastic cells; the neighboring mountains were covered with them, but these too soon became cramped for the imitators of St. Anthony, and the monks began to settle upon the other side of the Nile near the city of Arsinoë [^963]. "These monasteries in the mountains were like taberna-*(Orig. p. 193)*cles filled with divine choirs of psalmists, lovers of learning, fasters, men of prayer, who were gladdened by the hope of future blessings and engaged in handiwork to distribute alms" [^964]. In this description are indicated also the occupations of the monks in the "monasteries" of St. Anthony. In the monastery-cell the entire day of the monk was to pass. In it he was to remain as far as possible without going forth: "As a fish," said St. Anthony, "taken out of the water dieth, so likewise perisheth the fear of God in the heart of a monk if he vainly goeth forth from his cell"

=== LEAF p183 ===
[^965]. In this seclusion or enclosure of the monk within his cell St. Anthony distinguished diverse degrees. Unto total enclosure, wherein he himself lived (which, moreover, was likewise not absolute), he considered capable and admitted only a few. Unto St. Paul the Simple he saith: "If thou desirest to be a monk, go unto a cenobitic monastery (*koinobion*), where there are many brethren who are able to bear with thy infirmities; for I dwell here alone"; only when Paul had endured a severe probation did St. Anthony prepare a cell for him some three or four furlongs (*stadia*) from his own [^966]. The majority of St. Anthony's disciples, while dwelling solitary in their cells, nonetheless maintained a certain communion with one another: "For instruction Scripture is sufficient," Anthony said unto his disciples, "yet it is fitting for us to comfort one another by faith and encourage one another with a word" [^967]. In what specifically and concretely the common life in the "monasteries" directed immediately by St. Anthony was expressed, the preserved accounts do not permit us to say. They make no mention of the monks' prayers, whether common or private [^968], nor of their food and measure of abstinence. There are indications only of a common meal [^969]. Yet the system of spiritual direction and communal governance emerges quite clearly in the accounts. Apparently the young monks were under the unflagging supervision of the senior and experienced ones. "A monk," said St. Anthony, "ought frankly to tell the elders how many steps he taketh, or how many drops he drinketh in his cell, lest in any way he err therein" [^970]. As the supreme director of spiritual life in the monasteries that sprang up beside the site of St. Anthony's ascetic labors, he himself, of course, was recognized. In the "monasteries" nearest unto his dwelling he "frequently discoursed" [^971] with the monks. One such discourse, which must be a typical one, uniting *(Orig. p. 194)* within itself the diverse instructions of the venerable father at different times (on the struggle with demons), is transmitted and described by St. Athanasius: "On a certain day he came forth, and all the monks gathered unto him, desiring to hear a word from him. Anthony then spoke unto them in the Egyptian tongue" [^972]. Sometimes the venerable father took part also in the common meal of the monks: "Not infrequently, when drawing near with many other monks to taste of food and calling to mind spiritual food, he refused to taste thereof and went far away from them, accounting it a shame for himself if others saw him eating. Yet by reason of the necessary requirement of the body he took food, but in solitude, and not infrequently also together with the brethren, being as much ashamed before them as hoping to offer them an edifying word for their profit" [^973]. The influence and authority of St. Anthony in his monasteries were wholly grounded upon his moral height. His customary designation among the monks was "the Great": "The Great hath spoken," "The Great hath done" [^974]. "And his countenance possessed a grave and extraordinary grace. Anthony received also from the Savior this gift: whenever the venerable father was surrounded by a multitude of monks, and anyone who had not known him previously desired to see him, the one wishing so, passing by the others, went straightway unto Anthony, as though drawn

=== LEAF p184 ===
by his gaze" [^975].

Thus the monasteries of St. Anthony were of the anchoritic type, "serving as it were as a transitional stage from strict anchoritism unto a strict cenobitic community" [^976]. In them there were not only no written rules whatever [^977], but the order of life was not elaborated in detail; only the general spirit of this order was outlined. It is scarcely probable that in the monastic settlements, the monasteries (in the broad and later sense of the word) founded by St. Anthony, there were, beside St. Anthony himself, any general abbas or hegumens [^978]. *(Orig. p. 195)*

##### Nitria

Somewhat more complex in arrangement and closer unto cenobitic monasteries (*cenobia*) was a series of Egyptian monasteries founded during the lifetime of St. Anthony by his fellow-laborers or younger contemporaries. The most significant of these monasteries were Nitria, Scete, and Kellia (*The Cells*). The first was founded upon the Nitrian (*nitron*—natron, nitre) mountain (a desert plateau) south of Lake Mareotis, at a distance of 40 Roman miles (*millia*, 40,000 paces) from Alexandria. Of the Egyptian ascetics the first to settle here, and consequently the founder of this monastery, was St. Ammon, who began his solitary life in the 40th year of his age, passed about 20 years in the desert, and fell asleep prior unto St. Anthony (c. 350), with whom he had met [^979]. At the beginning of the 5th century there were up to 5,000 monks upon the Nitrian mountain [^980]; they lived in separate cell-dwellings (*tabernacula*), whereof there were up to 50 of diverse size; in some of these dwellings there lived more monks, in others fewer (*pauci*), and in yet others only one. The monks were under the direction of a single "father" (*sub uno patre posita*) [^981]. This direction belonged unto one of the eight presbyters who were attached unto the church of Nitria, and passed upon his death unto the next among them: "so long as the first presbyter liveth, the others celebrate not, judge not, and deliver no instructions, but only sit with him in silence." Corporal punishments (strokes of the scourge) were employed. The life of the monks was one of labor. The monastery possessed a well-ordered economy: in it were bakeries preparing bread not only for its own needs, but "likewise for the anchorites who had withdrawn into the inner desert, unto the number of 600"; there were their own physicians and apothecaries; wine was prepared not only for their own consumption, but also for sale; and there was a guest-house (*xenodochium*). In church they assembled only on Saturdays and Sundays. Yet daily, at least in the evening, divine service was celebrated in the cells: "upon the onset of evening one may stand and listen in each cell unto songs of praise and psalms chanted unto Christ, and prayers sent up unto the heavens; one would think that he had been caught up and transported unto heaven" [^982].

##### Kellia

=== LEAF p185 ===
At a distance of 10 Roman miles (*millia*) from Nitria was situated another remarkable monastery of ancient Christian Egypt—Kellia (*The Cells*). It was founded by Nitrian monks, lovers of complete solitude, and was of a purely anchoritic type. One of the first director-presbyters was St. Macarius of Alexandria († 494) [^983]. The monastery consisted of a multitude of solitary cells, the distance between which was such that from one cell it was *(Orig. p. 196)* not possible either to see another or to hear anything from thence [^984]. Some cells were so small that in them it was impossible to lie down stretched out unto one's full stature [^985]. The monks sat without leaving their cells; only on Saturday and Sunday did they assemble in church, and "there they looked upon one another as though returned from heaven"; if anyone was absent from this assembly, this was a sign of his illness, and the brethren visited him, supplying him with what was necessary for life [^986]. Some in the monastery led an even more reclusive life, not going forth from their cell for 40 days [^987]. On feast days a meal was likewise arranged in the church [^988]. The clothing of the monks differed somewhat from the ordinary local attire: "A certain father related that under Abba Isaac (5th c.) a brother came into the church in Kellia in a small cowl (*koukoulion*); the elder cast him out, saying: 'Here are monks, but thou art a layman; thou canst not be here'" [^989]. Like other Egyptian monasteries, the monks were buried here in that *leviton* (a tunic with short sleeves) and cowl wherein they had received the monastic habit and which in the course of life they put on only for communion of the Holy Mysteries [^990]. Concerning the height of the ascetic life in the monastery such an incident testifieth: once a bunch of fresh grapes was sent unto St. Macarius; although he then was very hungry, he sent the grapes unto a sick brother, the latter sent them unto another, and so the grapes made the round of the entire monastery and returned again unto the superior [^991]. Monophysitism penetrated into the monastery, and subsequently there were two churches therein: one for the Orthodox, and the other for the Monophysites [^992].

##### Scete

Almost the most significant of the Egyptian monasteries of that era was the so-called Scete (Coptic *Shiēt*—vast plain). Founded by St. Macarius the Great or of Egypt († 390) [^993], it was situated south of Kellia, still further into the depth of the Egyptian (Libyan) desert, at a distance of twenty-four hours' journey from Nitria; unto this place there was not even a road, but the way had to be directed by the courses of the stars. For life in such a wilderness, destitute of a sufficient quantity of water, an extraordinary strength of spirit was required [^994]. The governance of the monastery was con-*(Orig. p. 197)*centrated in the assembly of the brethren, which took place customarily in church under the presidency of the presbyter [^995]; at these assemblies theological questions were likewise decided [^996]. Minor matters of administration were concentrated in the hands of the presbyter; he, for example, had charge of the economic department of the monastery [^997], and in general occupied an honorable

=== LEAF p186 ===
position among the brethren [^998]. (The first to be ordained unto the presbyterate in Scete was St. Macarius the Great) [^999]. Scete was renowned for fasting. The customary food of the brethren here was bread alone. Unto the question of John Cassian concerning the daily norm of food for a monk, the Scetic Abba Moses replied: "We know that concerning this matter our fathers frequently discoursed; and having examined diverse modes of abstinence, consisting in that some nourished themselves only with legumes (*leguminibus*), others only with herbs (*oleribus*) or fruits (*pomis*), they preferred unto them all the nourishment of bread alone, recognizing as the most suitable quantity thereof two small loaves (*paxinacia*), weighing both together about a pound (*libra* = 12 ounces or 24 *lota*)." Unto the remark of Cassian's companion that it would be too much to eat such bread all at once, Moses replied: "If you desire to experience the power of this determination, adhere steadfastly unto this measure, adding nothing cooked (*coctionis pulmentum*) either on Sunday or on Saturday, nor on the occasion of a visit of brethren; for by strengthening oneself with cooked food, one may not only be content with a smaller measure on the remaining days, but likewise without labor refrain for a long time from using any other food" [^1000]. Unto Germanus' question, what then is to be done if after taking food (bread) in the specified measure, that is, after the ninth hour, a guest visit the monk, unto whom it is necessary to offer a meal and share it with him, Moses adviseth: at the ninth hour (the customary time of taking food) eat only one of the two loaves, and leave the other until evening in case of a brother's visit; if he come, eat the loaf together with him; yet if not, then alone [^1001]. Any other food beside bread was considered in Scete an impermissible luxury.

"Once Abba Achillas (5th c.) in Scete came into the cell of Abba Isaiah and saw that he was eating. Upon his dish was water with salt (*halas*). The elder, observing that Abba Isaiah hid the dish behind a basket, asked him: 'Tell me, what didst thou eat?' Isaiah replied: 'Forgive me, abba: I was cutting branches, and the heat came on; I took a morsel with salt, yet my throat was dried up from the heat, and the morsel would not go down; therefore I was constrained to pour a little water into the salt, that I might be able to eat. But forgive me!' Then the elder said: 'Come, look upon Isaiah, how he suppeth broth (*zōmon*) in Scete! If thou desirest to sup broth, go unto Egypt'" [^1002].

Unto John Moschus and Sophronius (early 6th c.) Abba John of Petra said: "Believe my words: when I was yet a youth in Scete, one of the fathers fell ill of the spleen. In the four lavras of Scete they sought for a little vinegar and found none. Such poverty and abstinence were among them, and there were about 3,500 fathers there" [^1003]. Abba Benjamin, presbyter of *(Orig. p. 198)* Kellia († ca. 392), recounted (concerning his stay in Scete):

"When after the harvest we returned unto Scete, there was brought unto us an alms from Alexandria, for each an alabaster vessel of pure oil. Upon the onset of the following harvest, the brethren brought into the church whatever remained with them. I had not opened the vessel, but piercing it with a needle I tasted a little of the oil, and it was in my heart as though I had committed a great transgression. When however the brethren brought their vessels as they were, while mine was pierced, I was put to shame, as though convicted of fornication" [^1004].

The same abba recounted:

"We came in Scete unto a certain elder and wished to give him a little oil; yet he said unto us: Behold where the small vessel lieth which you brought me three years ago; as you placed it, so it hath remained" [^1005].

"At the time of first-fruits there was brought unto Scete a jar (*saitis*) of wine, that a cup might be distributed unto each of the brethren. When one brother desired to flee from this into a tholos (*tholos*—a round cottage), the tholos collapsed. Those gathered at the noise saw the brother prostrate and began to reproach him, saying: 'It hath rightly happened unto thee, vain one'; yet the abba defended him and said: 'Leave my son alone; he hath done a good deed; and as the Lord liveth, this tholos shall not be rebuilt in my days, that the world may understand that through a cup of wine a tholos collapsed in Scete'" [^1006].

Departures from this austere regime [^1007] were made for feast days, especially at the agape meals [^1008], yet reluctantly [^1009], and for guests [^1010]. *(Orig. p. 199)* The strictness of life in Scete began to decline already in the 4th century. According unto the testimony of Poemen (5th c.), "from the third Scetic generation and from the time of Abba Moses († 400) the brethren have made no new progress" [^1011]. In the words of the same Poemen, "Abba Isidore, presbyter of Scete, once spoke unto the assembly thus: 'Brethren, was it not for labor that we came unto this place? and now here there is no longer labor. Therefore, taking my sheepskin cloak (*melote*), I will go where there is labor, and there I shall find rest'" [^1012]. In the early 5th century the monastery was devastated by nomads, concerning which Abba Arsenius, having in view Alaric's sack of Rome in 410, said: "The world hath lost Rome, and the monks Scete" [^1013]. Scete continued, however, to exist even after this devastation. In the 6th century Monophysitism drew away the majority of the Scetic monks; today in the desert of Scete there is a Jacobite monastery [^1014].

##### Other Anchoritic Monasteries of Egypt

Beside these three most important monasteries, which possessed no written typikon but contributed unto its formation, in ancient Christian Egypt there arose yet another series of monasteries of the same type, which without doubt likewise exercised a certain influence upon the elaboration of later monastic rules. Thus upon Mount Pherme, south of Nitria on the way unto Scete, there lived up to 500 ascetics [^1015], among whom St. Paul was especially renowned, and who probably constituted a single community. Near Hermopolis (in the Thebaid) was the monastery of Apollos or Apollonius (according unto Rufinus), having about 500 brethren. A peculiarity of this monastery was the daily communion of the Eucharist at the ninth hour of the day: "After communion (sometimes, according unto Rufinus) they sat down to listen unto the instruction *(Orig. p. 200)* of Apollos, which continued until evening; then (according unto Rufinus; according unto Palladius—before the instruction, immediately after communion) they took food, after which some withdrew into the desert and there throughout the entire night recited Holy Scripture from memory, while others remained at the place of assembly and spent the whole night until dawn in hymns and praises (*laudibus*) unto God; and yet others, having descended from the mountain at the ninth hour (for communion) and received the Eucharist, straightway withdrew again, contenting themselves with spiritual food alone until the next ninth hour; and thus they acted (according unto Palladius—many of them) during many (*plurimos*) days. And amid such ascetic labors they had always such joy, gladness, and exultation (*exultatio*) as is impossible upon earth among ordinary men; among them was none gloomy or sorrowful; and if anyone appeared sorrowful, Abba Apollos immediately asked concerning the cause of his grief and revealed what was in the secret of each one's heart."

Apollos likewise instilled in all the thought of the necessity of daily communion for a monk [^1016].

Among other monasteries of presumably the same type were the monasteries of the Thebaid city of Oxyrhynchus. According unto Rufinus, in this city (which now presents sand-covered ruins) there were about 10,000 monks and about 20,000 virgins; ancient public edifices and temples were converted here into monasteries, and of the latter there were more than of private houses; under the influence of the monasteries the inhabitants of the city were distinguished by singular piety (possessing twelve churches beside the monastic ones) and by hospitality, especially toward itinerant monks [^1017]. Near the city of

=== LEAF p189 ===
Heracleopolis there was the monastery of St. Isidore, distinguished by strict enclosure: the brethren had absolutely no communication with the world and admitted strangers no further than the guest-house at the gates; the monks of this monastery, as its porter-presbyter recounted unto Rufinus and Palladius, were "so holy that almost all were able to work miracles, and none of them was ever sick unto death; yet when the time of departure arrived for any of them, he, having beforehand announced this unto all, lay down and died" [^1018]. Near the city of Arsinoë was a series of monasteries with 10,000 monks under the direction of St. Serapion: "Through the brethren he gathered a multitude of provisions; for at the time of harvest all the monks brought unto him the grain products which they received in place of wages for reaping the neighboring fields, and all this the abba used for the relief of the poor, so that none suffered want in the vicinity; bread was sent unto the poor even as far as Alexandria; this, moreover, was done also by other monasteries of Egypt" [^1019]. It is possible that of the same semi-cenobitic type were the monasteries near Alexandria, wherein the author of the *Lausiac History* lived for about three years, and wherein he reckons about 2,000 "exceedingly great, zealous, and valiant men" [^1020]. *(Orig. p. 201)*

#### St. Pachomius and His Monasteries

St. Pachomius († 348) marked an epoch in the development of monasticism in that he founded the first cenobium (*koinos bios*—common life), a strictly cenobitic monastery, and gave it a written typikon. A native of Upper Egypt—a region considerably distant from Lower Egypt (the sphere of life and activity of the first Egyptian anchorites and the majority of the above-named monasteries), and differing from it in climate (being more torrid)—a pagan by birth, who embraced Christianity under the captivating power of Christian virtues, St. Pachomius initially secluded himself in an abandoned temple of Serapis, cultivating vegetables and palm trees and deriving thence sustenance not only for himself, but likewise for the poor; after three years Pachomius went unto the renowned anchorite of that region, Palamon, around whom a community of monks had already arisen; since few were able to endure that way of life which Palamon demanded of his disciples, the latter began to turn Pachomius away from him also, pointing out unto him the arduousness of the impending ascetic feats:

"We always watch through half of the night, meditating upon the word of God; very frequently from evening until morning we twist ropes, in order to combat sleep and furnish ourselves with

=== LEAF p190 ===
what is necessary for the support of the body; whatever remaineth over beyond our need we give unto the poor. We know not what it meaneth to eat oil or anything cooked or to drink wine; in summer we fast daily until evening, and in winter for two and three days together. The rule of common prayers [^1021] among us is to pray 60 times by day and 60 times by night, beside those prayers which we offer at every moment and whereof we know no count."

After several days of testing Pachomius, Palamon clothed him in monastic garments, girded him with the monastic belt, and began to accustom him especially unto night prayers and vigils. Upon the death of Palamon, Pachomius settled in the deserted village of Tabennisi ("palm of Isis") [^1022], concerning which he had received a revelation while Palamon was yet living, that a great monastery would arise here. Thither came unto him his brother John, who soon died, and then gradually around him a monastery arose. Initially it consisted, beside Pachomius, of three men. But soon the renown of Pachomius attracted unto him anchorites well known in Egypt (for example, St. Cornelius). With his monks the venerable father built and maintained a church in a neighboring village, and when up to 100 brethren had gathered, he built also a church within the monastery, whereon Saturdays the monks attended divine service in the village church, while on Sundays a presbyter came unto the monastery and celebrated the Liturgy here. Holy orders Pachomius himself did not receive, nor did he permit others in his monastery to receive them, to prevent pride. The monastery consisted of several houses, in each of which there was a superior and an assistant ("second"); the monks engaged in diverse crafts, especially the plaiting of rush mats; all this afforded the monastery considerable means, but, of course, the monies received went for common needs *(Orig. p. 202)* and charity (being at the disposal of a special steward); among the monks, moreover, were such as could not distinguish gold from silver (having entered the monastery with their parents). The principal director of the monastery, of course, was Pachomius himself, and the principal instrument of governance in his hands was discourse with the monks. Beside private discourses, St. Pachomius delivered three general instructions in the course of the week: one on Saturday and two on Sunday. On Wednesday and Friday the house superiors delivered instructions unto their monks. Food on non-fasting days consisted of cheese, eggs [^1023], cooked vegetables, and broth made from them; on Wednesdays and Fridays many ate nothing at all; others tasted food once in two days. All ate not more than once a day—some at the 6th hour, others at the 7th, 8th, 9th, 10th, 11th, or in the evening with the appearance of the stars.
