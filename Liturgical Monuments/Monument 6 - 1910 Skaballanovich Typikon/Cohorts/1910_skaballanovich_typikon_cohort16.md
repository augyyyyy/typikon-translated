Several morning and evening prayers, as well as prayers before and after communion, are attributed unto St. John Chrysostom and St. Macarius the Great. The prayer **"Spotless, undefiled, incorrupt..."** (*«Нескверная, неблазная...»*) is attributed unto St. Ephrem the Syrian [^679]. If these ascriptions are not always reliable (frequently they contradict one another in different monuments), they nevertheless bear witness unto the fact that the 4th and 5th centuries were from ancient times venerated as the most normative in regard unto church prayers.

#### Chant

Chant during divine worship in the 4th and 5th centuries received far more room in comparison with the 3rd century. At the Matins of the *Testamentum* it fills only what appears to be a necessary intermission between two long sequences of prayers; whereas unto the services described by the 4th-century pilgrim the title of "sung offices" (*asmatikai akolouthiai*) could well be applied.

##### The Psalter

The musical portion of the office was composed, as it is now, of biblical material and hymns of Christian composition. The former, both in the examined monuments and in the writers of that period, is reduced almost exclusively unto the psalms. Of the other canticles of Holy Scripture mention is made, as we have seen, of the liturgical use of the Canticle of the Three Youths and the Canticle of Simeon the God-Receiver [^680]. The liturgical use of the Psalter was no narrower than that of the present day: "In our assemblies," says St. John Chrysostom, "David is first, middle, and last" [^681]. "David is our Simonides, Pindar, Alcaeus, Flaccus, Catullus, and Serenus," says Blessed Jerome [^682]. From the temple the Psalter penetrated also into domestic use: according unto Socrates, the Emperor Theodosius the Younger with his sisters performed psalmody daily [^683]. According unto Blessed Jerome, "the farmer at the plow sings Alleluia; the reaper, covered with sweat, consoles himself with psalms; and the vine-dresser with his pruning-hook sings something of David" [^684]. The choice of psalms for singing at divine worship still depended in large measure upon the presiding bishop: "we prepared for ourselves a short psalm, which we bade the lector chant," says Blessed Augustine [^685].

##### Methods of Psalmody

As regards the method of chanting the psalms, responsorial and antiphonal singing was spreading more and more, apparently, in place of congregational singing.

##### Unison Singing

The passage cited above from the letter of St. Basil the Great unto the clergy of Neocaesarea [^686] seems to give it to be understood that the Neocaesareans considered the first two methods of singing an innovation. From the words of St. John Chrysostom:

=== LEAF p152 ===
"once the whole congregation sang with one voice, as we do even now" [^687], one may likewise conclude that this was the primitive method of singing, and that it had now begun to give way unto others. Blessed Augustine likewise mentions such a method of chanting the psalms: "We sang the psalm with one voice (*una voce*), encouraging one another, saying with one accord: **'Come, let us rejoice'**" [^688].

##### Solo Chanting

The opposite unto this method of singing was constituted by that wherein a psalm (or canticle, or hymn) was sung solely by a choir of chanters or by a single chanter without the participation of the worshipers. It has been observed that St. Cyril of Jerusalem, referring unto the psalms that had been chanted in his catechetical lectures, never expresses himself: "as we or you have sung," but always: "as you have heard" [^689]. The Council of Laodicea even forbids anyone to sing in church except the "canonical chanters" [^690]. According unto John Cassian, among the Egyptian monks the psalm was always sung by one of the brethren, while the rest sat and listened [^691]. Characterizing this mode of psalm execution in greater detail, John Cassian gives it to be understood that it was performed with an even chant (*parili pronunciatione*), in some monasteries without interruption verse after verse (*contiguis versibus*) [^692], in others with their division into two or three sections (*intercisiones*) with intermediate silent prayers [^693]. From the custom of continuous singing of a psalm by a single chanter there arose in the West a method of executing psalms unto which was attached somewhat later the name *psalmus tractus* (*tractim dicere*—to speak without interruption), and which consisted in this, that the psalm or a verse thereof was sung by a single chanter without any response made unto him by the choir (according unto the definition of Amalarius, a 9th-century writer) [^694]. From such singing of psalms has arisen our present reading of them, which is properly speaking a recitative chant.

##### Responsorial or Hypophonic Chant

Alongside the unbroken singing of the psalm by the whole people or by a single chanter, 4th- and 5th-century writers frequently mention the responsorial chanting of the psalm. "The Fathers established," says St. John Chrysostom, "that the people, when they knew not *(Orig. p. 156)* the whole psalm, should chant as a refrain (*ὑπηχεῖν*) from the psalm a forceful verse containing some sublime doctrine, and thence draw the necessary instruction" [^695]. What a powerful impression this singing produced is shown by the well-known incident with St. Athanasius: when during divine worship soldiers entered the church to arrest St. Athanasius in order to bring him to trial (at the demand of the Arians), he ordered the psalm to be sung with the refrain: **"For His mercy endureth for ever"** (*«Яко в век милость Его»*), executed by the whole people, and this enabled St. Athanasius to escape from the soldiers [^696]. According unto Sozomen, at the translation of the relics of the Hieromartyr Babylas unto Antioch the people chanted in response unto each verse of the psalm: **"Confounded be all they that worship carved images, that boast themselves of idols"** [^697]. At times such refrains were

=== LEAF p153 ===
very long, embracing two verses from the psalm. Thus, in his exposition of the 18th Psalm, Blessed Augustine speaks of the singing by the whole people of its 13th and 14th verses: **"Who can understand his lapses? Cleanse Thou me from my secret faults..."** [^698] The entire psalm in such a method of execution must have been sung or read by a single chanter. "The chanter (*ὁ ψάλλων*) sings (*ψάλλει*) alone, although all chant the refrain (*ὑπηχοῦσι*), as if the voice were sent up from a single mouth" [^699]. (Concerning the reading of psalms, St. Ambrose remarks: "you heard today how it was read (*lectum*): **'Be ye angry, and sin not'**" [^700]; and Blessed Augustine: "best of all was that which they related concerning Athanasius, the Bishop of Alexandria, that he so instructed the reader to chant the psalms that he seemed not so much to sing as to read" [^701]). The *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia speaks, apparently, predominantly of responsorial psalmody. Unto the wide dissemination of this method of singing there contributed, as we have seen from the words of St. Chrysostom, the imperfect knowledge of the text of the psalms among the people and, in all likelihood, the rarity of written manuscripts thereof. Out of responsorial psalmody arose our prokeimena and the Roman Catholic responsories [^702]: instead of entire psalms, there were selected for singing with a refrain at first, in all probability, certain sections of them, and subsequently merely a few verses (at first four, whence the Great Prokeimenon, and later two). Yet the testimonies from the 4th and 5th centuries still presuppose the integral singing of the psalm with a refrain. Such singing of an entire psalm with a refrain unto each verse has been preserved in our present Typikon only for Psalm 135 (LXX; Masoretic Ps. 136), and that because the refrain *(Orig. p. 157)* (**"For His mercy endureth for ever"**) is placed here after every verse in the biblical text itself.

##### Antiphonal Chant

Still more complex was the antiphonal method of singing. It is not known with certainty what kind of singing was designated by this term in the 4th and 5th centuries. Originally, in all probability, this was the name given unto the alternating singing of the verses of a psalm between two halves of the worshipers. If so, then the initial occasion for the introduction and spread of such singing could have been the increase in the number of believers: unison singing of the psalm by all amid an excessively large number of worshipers could disturb the harmony of the chant. Unto this precise method of singing St. Basil points in the passage cited above from his epistle unto the clergy of Neocaesarea [^703]. Socrates, who traces, as was pointed out above [^704], the origin of antiphonal singing unto Antioch and regards as its founders the presbyters Diodore and Flavian (4th–5th cc.), speaks of singing by two choirs (consequently, by trained singers, and not by the general people).

By the same term there was designated, apparently, another method of singing somewhat distinct from this, namely that wherein before the psalm or after the psalm, executed alternately by two choirs, there was sung a particular verse of this psalm or of some other; this verse, closely related unto the content of the psalm, was called an antiphon (an echo, a reverberation) [^705]. According unto the definition of the aforementioned Amalarius: "until the symphony of the

=== LEAF p154 ===
antiphon, the psalm is sung by two choirs, and at the antiphon itself the choirs unite" [^706]. Such antiphons must have been borrowed not only from the psalms and from Holy Scripture in general, but could also be of independent Christian composition. Sylvia, as we have seen, noticeably distinguishes them from the psalms, as well as, indeed, from the hymns. Yet Socrates in that same passage expresses himself: *ἀντιφώνους ὕμνους* ("it is necessary to state also whence the custom of antiphonal hymns in the Church took its origin" [^707]). Blessed Augustine, attributing unto St. Ambrose the introduction into Milan of the Eastern method of singing, apparently has in mind antiphonal singing of this very latter kind, since responsorial singing in general was already widely disseminated everywhere, while antiphonal singing in the broad sense (the first of the aforementioned modes) did not differ essentially from it. "Then," says Blessed Augustine concerning the episcopate of St. Ambrose in Milan, "it was established that singing should be performed according unto the custom of the Eastern regions, lest the people languish in weariness" [^708]. Today both among us and in the Roman Catholic Church antiphons are precisely designated as special verses, not always drawn from the psalms [^709] and not always *(Orig. p. 158)* (among us) united with the singing of psalms; among us, however, antiphons also designate the alternating chanting of the verses of a psalm (thus, for example, the divisions of the Kathismata are termed antiphons [^710]); a remnant of the antiphonal execution of a psalm by the entire assembly of worshipers is preserved among us in the chanting of **"Let my prayer be set forth"** (*«Да исправится»*) at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts [^711].

#### Eastern Hymnographers

If the term "antiphon" possessed the sense of an independent addition unto a psalm at its beginning and end (or an addition unto a specific division of a psalm), then it formed a transition from the psalms unto those canticles and hymns which already in the 3rd century had acquired a significant place in divine worship. As in the 3rd century, the revival of hymnographic activity in the Church was fostered by the attempts of certain heretics to disseminate their doctrine by means of hymns. This was done in Syria by the followers of Bardesanes [^712]; under the name of Arius there was known a collection of hymns entitled *Thalia* (*Θάλεια*) [^713] (one of the Muses); under the name of Apollinaris, the *New Psalter* [^714]; the Donatists likewise made use of hymns for the propagation of their doctrine [^715]. In combating this heretical hymnography there attained renown Saints Athanasius the Great, Ephrem the Syrian, Gregory the Theologian, and John Chrysostom. But the first and the last of these fought rather by way of the organization of divine worship. Unto St. Chrysostom tradition attributes the introduction into divine worship of troparia-antiphons [^716], wherein there may perhaps be kept in view merely their transference from Antioch unto Constantinople [^717]. Saints Ephrem and Gregory, on the other hand, emerged as renowned hymnographers. Unto the former († 373 / 378) the Syrians and Copts attribute twelve to fourteen thousand hymns; but the majority of such hymns bear traces of later composition and

=== LEAF p155 ===
merely imitate the authentic hymns of Ephrem [^718]. In the *Acts (Life) of St. Ephrem* it is stated that "by his sublime and spiritual canticles (*odas*) he delivered instruction concerning the Nativity of Christ, the Baptism, the Fast, the Passion (of Christ), the Resurrection, the Ascension, and the other mysteries of that divine Providence; unto these he joined also other hymns—concerning the martyrs, concerning repent*(Orig. p. 159)*ance, and concerning the departed" [^719]. (Let us note that the cycle of feasts given in this enumeration conforms entirely unto the 4th century). The canticles of St. Ephrem for feasts in their character strongly recall the later *stichera*; thus, for example, the canticle for the Nativity of Christ depicts the inner disposition of the Righteous Joseph at the sight of the Divine Infant: "Who hath given unto me as a son the Son of the Most High?... Vain was my suspicion toward Mary... David, place the crown upon His head..." [^720] Certain of the hymns of St. Ephrem for feasts are used even today by the Maronites (Syrian Monothelites). Unto St. Ephrem are also attributed about fifty canticles in honor of the Mother of God, the majority of which are likewise used today among the Maronites; there is an account [^721] that from these canticles of St. Ephrem Paul of Akmoreia (in the 11th–12th cc.) extracted his stichera unto the Most Holy Theotokos, appointed in our Octoechos at **"Lord, I have cried"** (*«Господи воззвах»*) at Sunday Vespers "where there is no Menaion." The hymns of St. Ephrem, notwithstanding the warmth of religious feeling, did not enter into our church use, in all probability because they were written in the Syriac tongue. Unto Gregory the Theologian († 389 / 390), a rather prolific poet, are attributed about twelve hymns, the finest among which are: the "Hymn to God," rather close in content unto the **"Te Deum Laudamus"** (*«Тебе Бога хвалим»*) [^722], and the "Hymn to Christ after Silence (Fasting) at Pascha," wherein, however, there is glorified not the Resurrection, but the creative and providential working of God and the Incarnation. The hymns of St. Gregory are of a somewhat abstract content and on that account were not received into divine worship (it cannot be maintained, however, that they were not used in divine worship in antiquity). On the other hand, a number of passages from the orations of St. Gregory were transformed by later hymnographers into church canticles (for example, **"Christ is born"** [*«Христос рождается»*]). Marked by even greater abstraction are the hymns of Synesius of Cyrene, which are somewhat imbued with Neoplatonism [^723].

#### Latin Hymnography

The West in this period brought forward more illustrious hymnographers: Hilary of Poitiers († 367), Pope Damasus († 384), St. Ambrose of Milan († 397), Blessed Augustine († 430), Prudentius (Aurelius Prudentius Clemens, b. 348), Sedulius (Coelius Sedulius, first half of the 5th c.), and Fortunatus (Venantius Fortunatus, † c. 609). Unto this same epoch belongs in the West *(Orig. p. 160)* the hymn **"Te Deum Laudamus"** (*«Тебе Бога хвалим»*) (judging by the mention made thereof by 6th-century writers [^724]), superscribed in

=== LEAF p156 ===
Roman Catholic liturgical books with the names of Saints Ambrose and Augustine. According unto one tradition, it was composed by St. Ambrose in thanksgiving for the definitive victory over Arianism. According unto other traditions, it was chanted under divine inspiration (improvised) by St. Ambrose and Blessed Augustine at the baptism of the latter by the former, when they both stood in the baptismal font; St. Ambrose sang one verse, and Blessed Augustine another; the final verse: **"In Thee, O Lord, have I hoped..."** (*«На Тя, Господи, уповах...»*) was concluded by Blessed Augustine [^725]. Unto St. Ambrose and Blessed Augustine this hymn cannot belong, because it is not mentioned in their writings (whereas other hymns of theirs are mentioned) and differs from their undoubtedly authentic hymns.

In hymnographic terms the 4th and 5th centuries, consequently, were more fruitful in the West than in the East: they contributed unto Roman Catholic worship no less material than perhaps all subsequent centuries combined. Hence Roman Catholic worship prides itself before ours on the antiquity of its hymns, as well as on their poetic form (the aforementioned are written for the most part in unrhymed iambics). Yet the Latin hymns of the 4th and 5th centuries, which served as models also for subsequent Latin hymnography, in comparison with the later Eastern *stichera* that entered into our divine worship, are of a very abstract and general character—they are not attached unto the sacred commemorations of the day (recalling in this respect, from Eastern hymnography, the contemporary compositions of St. Gregory the Theologian and Synesius) [^726]. Such a development of hymn-writing in the 4th and 5th centuries in all likelihood provoked the decrees in the East of the Council of Laodicea against the liturgical use of "private psalms" (*ἰδιωτικοὺς ψαλμοὺς*) and non-canonical books [^727], and in the West of the Fourth Council of Toledo of 633, which permitted the use of hymns only of Saints Hilary and Ambrose, as well as the Lesser and Greater Doxologies [^728].

#### Melodies and Modes

If in the 3rd century the melody of church canticles engaged the care of pastors, then in the present period these concerns were bound to intensify. However, no reliable reports upon this point have been preserved from this period. There exists an account concerning St. Basil the Great († 379) that he applied unto church canticles the melody of Pindar's epinicia [^729]. Unto the name of St. Ambrose is superscribed an Antiphonary set to musical notation of later *(Orig. p. 161)* origin [^730]. It contains four melodies based upon the four Greek musical modes: Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, and Mixolydian; the distinction of these melodies was grounded in the fact that they began from different pitches of the octachord: the first on D, the second on E, the third on F, and the fourth on G, and therefore their reciting tone (dominant), attached unto the first degree of the octachord (in the second melody unto the sixth, since on the fifth degree stood the variable B), occurred on different notes. Thus arose the four authentic modes (unto which were later added another

=== LEAF p157 ===
four plagal modes) [^731]. These modes bear the name of Ambrosian; in the composition of the present Roman Catholic (Gregorian) Octoechos they correspond unto modes 1, 3, 5, and 7. Among our Byzantine-Ruthenian Tones, the first three of them are close unto Tones 1, 2, and 3.

#### Liturgical Reading in the 4th and 5th Centuries

Liturgical reading in the 4th and 5th centuries, in comparison with the 3rd century, underwent significant changes in regard unto the volume of that which was read, the assignment of readings unto one or another service, and the selection of readings.

##### Formation of the Canon

In the first respect, a sharp boundary was definitively drawn between canonical and non-canonical sacred books. Apparently, at the very beginning of the 4th century there was still some hesitation in this regard, but from the second half of this century the canon of sacred books everywhere received its present composition. Thus Eusebius of Caesarea († c. 339 / 340) still hesitates in recognizing the Apocalypse as a canonical book, as well as in the authenticity of the Catholic Epistles of James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2 and 3 John [^732]. St. Athanasius († 373) [^733], as well as the Councils of Laodicea (second half of the 4th c.) [^734] and Carthage (419) [^735], know the canon already in its present form (the Council of Laodicea does not name, however, the Apocalypse in its list of canonical books). The precise establishment of the canon could not fail to elevate the authority of biblical literature, nor to intensify, so to speak, the sense of its divine inspiration and reverence toward it. The reading of sacred books during divine worship now definitively receives that sacramental significance which was attributed unto it in part also in earlier times (not without the influence of the synagogue). At the same time—in comparison with the 3rd century—the Old Testament is now pushed more and more into the background in liturgical reading.

##### Number of Readings from the Old and New Testaments

At the beginning of this period there is noticeable still a certain uniformity in the reading of both Testaments at divine worship. Thus the *Apostolic Constitutions* appoint *(Orig. p. 162)* (for the Liturgy, at which alone at that time readings were held) two readings from the Old Testament [^736]: from the Law and the Prophets [^737], and two or three from the New: from the Acts, the Epistles of Paul, and the Gospels [^738]. Later, or in other churches, there was, apparently, one reading from the Old Testament and two from the New. St. John Chrysostom in his homilies delivered at Constantinople says: "Let us touch today upon what was read... And blessed David draweth unto himself our ministration, as doth the apostolic word spoken unto Timothy; and Isaiah philosophizeth concerning human nature; and our Master Jesus, conversing with His disciples: **'The harvest is plenteous...'**" [^739] "The reader begins: 'The prophecy of Isaiah,' and no one

=== LEAF p158 ===
attendeth... Then he crieth aloud: 'Thus saith the Lord,' and no one attendeth" [^740]. "In vain entering hither did one prophet speak, did one apostle converse with us, and concerning what?" [^741] St. Basil the Great in his homily unto those preparing for baptism mentions the reading on that day after the psalms from Isaiah, Chapter 2, Acts, Chapter 2, and Matthew 11 [^742]. In another homily mention is made of reading "at the morning hour" after the psalms from Proverbs, the Apostle, and the Gospels [^743]. Cf. in the Armenian Liturgy—*lectio prophetica*, *Apostolus*, *Evangelium* [^744]. So it was also in the Western churches. Maximus, Bishop of Turin (in Italy, in the 5th c.), speaks of readings on a single day from Isaiah, the Gospel of Matthew, and John [^745]. Blessed Augustine sometimes speaks of readings only from the Epistles and the Gospels [^746], but sometimes also from the Prophets, for example Isaiah (57:13) [^747]. The most ancient manuscripts of the Western liturgies—Gallican [^748], Ambrosian, Mozarabic (in Spain)—and the most ancient lectionary of the Spanish Church, the *Liber Comicus* [^749] (c. 6th c.), have an Old Testament reading (*lectio*) before the apostolic (*epistola*) and the evangelical (*lectio sancti evangelii*) [^750]. On the contrary, the most ancient manuscripts of the Roman Liturgy in the *Sacramentarium Gregorianum* (attributed unto St. Gregory the Great, † 604) and the most ancient Gallican lectionaries, which do not ascend higher than the 8th century (*Comes ab Albino emendatus* under Charlemagne, and others), have only New Testament readings—*Apostolus* and *(Orig. p. 163)* *Evangelium* [^751]. Thence scholars conclude that in the Roman Liturgy there were never Old Testament readings [^752]. According unto Walafrid Strabo († 849), Pope St. Celestine I (422–432) first introduced an Old Testament element into the Liturgy by prefixing unto the apostolic and gospel readings the Introit antiphons (*ad introitum*): "before his time prior to the oblation only the reading of the Apostle and the Gospel was read" [^753]. (Cf. below the testimony of John Cassian). It may be that under the influence of Roman practice Old Testament readings were gradually abolished also in the Eastern liturgies. In the 6th century there are no longer Old Testament readings in the Greek Liturgy: they are not mentioned in the acts of the Council of Constantinople of 536, nor in the *Life of St. Sabbas the Sanctified* composed by Cyril of Scythopolis, where mention is made of the Liturgy and its readings [^754]. St. Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem, although he speaks in his work *On the Sacred Vestments and the Liturgy* of "prophetic words" at the Liturgy "after the first supplication" (the litany), understands by them the antiphons [^755]. Other Eastern liturgies, although they lack Old Testament readings even in their most ancient manuscripts, yet in the fact that they have four entire New Testament readings, may preserve a trace of the former existence of Old Testament readings. Thus the Coptic (Jacobite) and Ethiopian liturgies have readings from the "Apostle," that is, the Epistles of St. Paul; from the "Catholic," that is, the Catholic Epistles; from Acts; and from the Gospel [^756]. In the Nestorian Liturgy there are likewise four readings and all from the New Testament; for example, for the Ascension of the Lord: 2 Corinthians 2:14–17; 3:1–3, and Acts 1:1–14; then 1 Timothy 1:8–11; 3:14–16, and Luke 24:36–53 [^757]. (The Acts, like the Gospel, is preceded by an Epistle of the Apostle!). Old Testament readings in this liturgy

=== LEAF p159 ===
were abolished only in the 7th century (by Patriarch Mar Aba c. 614 [^758]). A similar development, consequently, may be supposed also concerning the Coptic-Ethiopian liturgy.

The Old Testament readings removed from the Liturgy were not, of course, abandoned altogether, but were transferred unto other services, specifically unto Vespers, as the service which had taken the place of the former evening Liturgy and was therefore closest unto it in structure. Yet from the 4th and 5th centuries there is no information concerning the existence of readings at Vespers (excluding the obscure indication in the *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia [^759]). But there are reports that Old Testament readings were assigned predominantly unto weekdays, on which for the most part the Liturgy was not celebrated. Thus John Cassian states that in the Egyptian monasteries at weekday offices (which ones, he specifies not) there is one reading from the Old Testament and one from the New Testament, whereas on Saturday and Sunday both readings are from the New Testament (naturally, at *(Orig. p. 164)* the Liturgy, since on both these days almost everywhere the Liturgy was celebrated, as we have seen, of which readings had been an indispensable constituent since the 2nd century); likewise throughout the entire Pentecostarion period [^760]. Remarkable is the restriction which was made for weekdays in regard unto New Testament readings, an echo whereof may be seen in the requirement of the Council of Laodicea that on Saturday there should be read "(also) the Gospel with (*μετὰ*) the other scriptures" [^761]: in all likelihood on weekdays the Gospel was not read.

The *Pilgrimage* of Sylvia speaks of the reading of the Gospel only at Sunday Matins and at the services of great feasts (and of Holy Week); more frequently she speaks of "readings" in general [^762]. However, it must be noted that since reading was from ancient times attached unto the Liturgy, in certain localities, on days when there was no Liturgy, there were no readings either. Cassian calls reading at weekday worship something "supererogatory, only for those who desire it" [^763].

##### Pericopes

As regards the selection of readings, there occur in this period even more than in the 3rd century [^764] mentions of "pericopes," of "readings" (*lectiones*). But just as in that era, these pericopes were not attached unto specific days of the year, but were read continuously one after another at divine worship, excluding the great feasts and in part Great Lent, for which there were special readings. "Your Holiness remembers," says Blessed Augustine, "that we customarily examine the Gospel of John according unto the order of the readings. But since now the celebration of the holy days has arrived, for which definite readings from the Gospel are appointed, and they are annual, so that there can be no others, we must of necessity interrupt the adopted order, though we abandon it not entirely" [^765]. Certain passages in St. John Chrysostom sound, however, as if the pericopes were already distributed among the liturgical days of the year, among the Sundays. "We frequently indicate unto you many days in advance the subject (*ὑπόθεσις*) of the discourse, so that you

=== LEAF p160 ===
in the intervening time may take the book into your hands and examine the pericope" [^766]. "Let each person several days in advance on any day of the week, even on Saturday, take and read at home continuously (*συνεχῶς*) the pericope of the Gospel that is about to be read" [^767]. But Chrysostom could speak thus also in the event that the pericopes were not fixed unto specific calendar days, but followed in the continuous order of the Gospel [^768].

The presiding bishops enjoyed a certain freedom in the choice of pericopes for reading. "For this reason did we bid (*fecimus*)," says Blessed Augustine, "that there be read that very reading from the Gospel wherein the Lord was tempted with the words *(Orig. p. 165)* of the psalm which you have just heard" [^769]. Sometimes the theme planned by the preacher determined the selection of the reading: "remember our promise," says Blessed Augustine, "we ordered that corresponding readings from the Gospel and the Apostle be brought forward" [^770]. If the sermon had to be continued at the following service, the reading was likewise repeated at it: "Your charity remembers that last Sunday we conversed concerning spiritual rebirth; this reading we ordered to be read again, in order to complete that which was not stated then" [^771].

##### Euthalius

From the 4th and 5th centuries there has been preserved an attempt at dividing the biblical New Testament text for liturgical use, belonging unto Euthalius, according unto some manuscripts a deacon (of Alexandria), according unto others Bishop of Sulca (*Σούλκης*, an unknown locality in Egypt), perhaps at first a deacon and later a bishop. In his "edition" (*ἔкθεσις*) of the Acts [^772], the Epistles of St. Paul, and the Catholic Epistles, Euthalius first of all divides the biblical text into verses (*stichoi*), placing in each verse as many words as a reader is able to pronounce in a single breath, yet conforming therein unto the sense; next he divides the text into 57 readings (*ἀναγνώσεις*), indicating (in the *Ἀνακεφαλαίωσις*) with which words each reading begins and how many chapters it contains (the chapters being of nearly the present size, sometimes smaller); the Acts is divided into 16 readings, Romans and 1 Corinthians into 5 readings each, 2 Corinthians into 4 readings, Hebrews into 3 readings, James, 1 Peter, 1 John, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, 1 Thessalonians, and 1 Timothy into 2 readings each, and the remainder into 1 reading each. (In regard unto the Epistles of St. Paul, Euthalius made use of the division into chapters that existed prior to him). For each chapter its citations and parallels from other sacred books and apocrypha are indicated, and a summary of the contents is given. Today scholars dispute the attribution of this work in its entirety unto a single individual and disagree in determining the time of his life. Under Athanasius, unto whom the author dedicates his work, some understand the famous Archbishop of Alexandria, others Archbishop Athanasius II of Alexandria (490–496). Scholars find that the work could have originated in the 4th century (under St. Athanasius the Great), but that certain elements in it, and among others the division into liturgical readings, constitute a later accretion, since they correspond unto no division of the ecclesiastical year
