### THE FIRST HOUR

#### Its Relationship to the Vigil

Already from the Praises, that is, from the concluding portion of Matins, the Vigil diminishes its solemn intensity, transitioning first into the quiet and slow singing of the Great Doxology, and then into a long series of litanic petitions; the litiya after Matins with the reading that fills it stands in this regard yet another step lower, while the First Hour, completely devoid of singing, stands lower still. Both the one and the other are even transferred out of the nave into the narthex. Quiet prayer now caresses and refreshes the soul, exhausted by the prolonged ecstasy of festive hymns.

*(Orig. p. 736)*

#### Its Relationship to Matins

Falling at the very break of dawn (the first Eastern hour = 7:00 AM for us at the equinox), the First Hour stands in the same relationship to Matins as the Ninth Hour does to Vespers: both, coinciding in time, and partly in content, with these two paramount daily services, nevertheless possess each its own distinctive theological idea and do not repeat from those services a single chant or prayer.

#### Structure and Content of the First Hour

The theme of the First Hour is the thoughts and sentiments of the believer at the onset of morning; only certain passages of its psalms depicting the treachery of the enemies of the persecuted righteous one (as Psalm 5:11 [LXX 5:10]: **"There is no truth in their mouth... their throat is an open sepulchre..."**) permit also the association of the First Hour, according to the direction of the *Instructional Notice* [*Uchitel'noye izvestiye*] appended to the *Sluzhebnik*, with the commemoration of the trial of Christ before Pilate, which took place at this hour of the day.

The first psalm of the Hour, Psalm 5, **"Give ear to my words, O Lord"** [*Glagoly moya vnushi, Gospodi*], is a hope-filled morning prayer in the temple for the righteous and against the ungodly, who must be deprived of any participation in liturgical worship, wherein the psalmist turns from the ones to the others several times in balanced strophes (in all likelihood originally intended for antiphonal singing).

The second psalm of the First Hour, Psalm 89 [LXX 89; Ps 90], **"Lord, Thou hast been our refuge"** [*Gospodi, pribezhishche byl yesi

=== LEAF p893 ===

...nam"*, attributed (also by the Hebrew Bible) to Moses, depicts the brevity and arduousness of human life in comparison with the eternity of God, Who replaces generations of men upon earth like plants each morning, for our life before God is shorter than a single day; seeing the cause of this in the wrath of God against our sins, the singer prays for a swift ("in the morning") recompense by the mercy of God for former afflictions according to the number of their days and years.

In the final psalm of the Hour, Psalm 100 [LXX 100; Ps 101], **"Of mercy and judgment will I sing,"** the pious king in the form of a vow outlines the program for his conduct (at the beginning, the morning of his reign) both in private, domestic life, and in intercourse with men, toward subordinates and immediate servants, and toward the wicked, whom he will destroy every morning.[^2721]

Among the hymns and prayers of the First Hour, the Lenten troparion **"In the morning hear my voice"** [*Zautra uslyshi glas moy*] prays in general for the acceptance of morning prayer; the Theotokion **"What shall we name thee"** [*Chto Tya narechem*] constitutes a glorification of the Mother of God unrelated to the time of day, yet of the Theotokia at the Hours it is the most ecstatic, perhaps because the First Hour corresponds approximately to the time of the Savior's birth ("thou didst hold in thine arms the Son, the God of all"); this Theotokion is used nowhere else.

The verse of the Hour at the First Hour, in contrast to the other Hours, is twofold (as we shall see below, the First Hour in non-Orthodox Churches is distinguished from the others by its length and unique character); the first verse, **"Order my steps,"** taken from the midnight-matins Psalm 118 [LXX 118:133–135] *(Orig. p. 737)*, asks God for the direction of our conduct according to the commandments of God (implicitly, in the coming day) and for spiritual enlightenment; the second, from Psalm 70:8 [LXX 70:8], **"Let my mouth be filled with Thy praise,"** asks that throughout the coming day we may sing the glory of God.

The Lenten troparion after the Trisagion, of which at the First Hour, unlike the other Hours, only one is appointed, is dedicated to the Mother of God: **"The most glorious Mother of God"** [*Preslavnuyu Bozhiyu Mater'*], which serves also as a sessional hymn in Tone 6; on Wednesday and Friday to the Cross, **"Speedily anticipate"** [*Skoro predvari*]—a sessional hymn in Tone 4; on Saturday to the martyrs, **"As the firstfruits"** [*Yako nachatki*]—the kontakion to the martyrs in Tone 8. Only one troparion is appointed, perhaps in correspondence with the number of the Hour and on account of fatigue from Matins.

The exclamation at **"Thou Who at all times"** [*Izhe na vsyakoe vremya*] is the same as at the Ninth Hour: **"God be merciful unto us"** [*Bozhe ushchedri*].

The Prayer of the First Hour, attributed to St. Sabbas the Sanctified,[^2722] addressed to Christ, as is the prayer of the Ninth Hour alone besides this, asks Him, as the One Who enlightens (in baptism) and sanctifies every man coming into life, for spiritual enlightenment and correction through the intercessions of His Mother (the latter

=== LEAF p894 ===

...only in the prayer of this Hour; cf. the troparion after the Trisagion).

Liturgical practice has established that this prayer, unlike the other prayers at the Hours, is pronounced by the priest and not by the reader (in the Kyiv Caves Lavra—by the reader); and after it, in keeping with the special attention accorded at this Hour to the Most Holy Theotokos, they sing the kontakion to Her from the service of the Annunciation, **"To Thee, the Champion Leader"** [*Vzbrannoy Voyevode*], as possessing the most comprehensive laudatory and prayerful content. In the Kyiv Caves Lavra they do not sing it. As the concluding service in this group, the First Hour has a dismissal, termed therefore "complete" (*sovershennyy*), in common parlance "lesser" (*malyy*), at which among the saints only the choir of the venerable monastics is commemorated, because it is a monastic service.

#### History of the Hour

The First Hour was established in the 4th century[^2723] in Palestinian monasteries for ascetic purposes (Introductory Chapter, p. 147), wherein the concluding psalms of Matins—Psalms 50, 62, and 89 [LXX]—were introduced into its composition (*ibid.*). Concerning its subsequent development during the era when the Hours perhaps possessed a sung, musical character, nothing is known; judging from the fact that later sung offices appoint for it Psalm 5 [LXX],[^2724] one may surmise that from antiquity this psalm was part of the Hour's structure.

According to the most ancient Greek Horologion of the 8th–9th century, Sinai Library no. 863, the First Hour has after the usual beginning the troparion **"Having passed the night, I thank Thee, O Lord"** three times; 8 psalms: 5, 45, 66, 69, 89, 91, 100, 112 [LXX]; antiphons from Psalm 142:1, 2, 8–12 [LXX] with *Alleluia*; the troparion **"(O Christ,) True Light,"** preceded by its conclusion **"And direct"** (perhaps a refrain); **"Glory..."**—a troparion according to the day of the week: on Monday and Tuesday, **"Hear, O Lord, sinners who pray"**; on Wednesday and Friday, **"As having a multitude of compassions upon our humility"**; on Thursday, **"O Most Pure Theotokos, in despair at our grievous transgressions"**; on Saturday, **"O victorious martyrs"**; **"Order my steps"** (3 times), but for *(Orig. p. 738)* **"Let my mouth be filled"** no repetition is specified (as is also the case in other Hours); the Hour concludes with the Trisagion, the Creed, and the Our Father.

Horologia from the 9th century onward contain the First Hour in its present composition, differing only in the troparia after the Trisagion and the concluding prayers. Thus, the Slavonic Horologia of the Moscow Synodal Typography Library, nos. 46 and 48 (15th–16th c.), have the troparia **"Thou Who didst command light to exist throughout all creation"** and **"O Lord, we are Thy people"**; a manuscript of the Athonite St. Andrew Skete, no. 12 (11th c.), has only the troparion **"Speedily anticipate."** However, two manuscripts, namely St. Petersburg Public Library no. 57 and Sophia Library at the St. Petersburg Theological Academy no. 1052 (13th c.), appoint troparia after the psalms that differ from the present ones: specifically, on Saturday, "when there is no troparion" (for the day), **"Righteous Light"** and

...**"Truly all things are vanity."** As regards the prayers at the First Hour, in the Sung Office (*Asmatike Akolouthia*) it, like the other Hours, possessed 3 prayers of the antiphons, 1 of the dismissal, and 1 of the bowing of the heads (*kephaloklisia*); in certain monuments also a prayer behind the ambo (*opisthembonos*), and in some, prior to the latter, also a "morning" prayer (7 in total). In manuscript Horologia as well, the First Hour frequently contains several prayers; thus, in manuscripts of the Moscow Typography Library, nos. 46 and 48 (15th c.), there are 4 prayers, all differing from the present one: **"I thank Thee, O Lord my God, and I glorify Thee,"** **"Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God"** (no. 46: **"Thou Who at all times"**), **"Holy Father, Who the righteous Sun,"** and a "prayer concluding all singing: Merciful Lord, accept my sighs."

Sinai no. 12 (13th c.) and St. Petersburg Public Library no. 57 lack the third prayer. Sophia Library at the St. Petersburg Theological Academy no. 1052 (13th c.) contains of these only the first. Sinai no. 868 (13th–14th c.) and Moscow Typography Library no. 47 (15th c.) have no prayers. Sinai no. 855 (12th c.): **"Spotless, undefiled"** and **"Thou Who at all times,"** with a later hand adding: **"O Christ, the True Light"**; Sinai no. 870 (14th c.)—the present Prayer of the First Hour, with the addition of the present prayers of the Mid-Hour, wherein the latter (**"Thou Who sendest forth light"**) is read with uplifted hands.[^2725]

### THE THIRD HOUR

#### Content of the Hour

The Third Hour is always joined with the Sixth, forming with it a single service, which is placed in the same intimate relationship with the Divine Liturgy as the First Hour is with Matins and the Ninth Hour is with Vespers.

The general theme of the Third Hour is the natural prayer at the beginning of the day for the preservation of righteousness in conduct and the elimination of obstacles thereto—external ones on the part of enemies, and internal ones on the part of our own sinfulness. But in accordance with two commemorations associated for the Christian with this hour, this theme divides in two: according to the *Instructional Notice* of the *Sluzhebnik*, the Third Hour commemorates the trial of the Savior before Pilate, with the scourging and mocking, which took place precisely between 9:00 AM and 12:00 noon,[^2729] and the Descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Apostles, which occurred precisely at this hour. The aforementioned general theme of the Hour is developed by its psalms, and especially by the verse of the Hour (consequently, its central focus), while of its two particular motifs, the first is presented in the first two psalms, and the second in the third psalm and in all the hymns.

The first psalm of the Hour, Psalm 16 [LXX 16; Ps 17], **"Hear, O Lord, my righteousness"** [*Uslyshi, Gospodi, pravdu moyu*], is a prayer for help in the name of the petitioner's righteousness, and above all in the name of the marvelous mercy of God—for help against enemies seeking both directly and indirectly to destroy the righteous one, as one who, by his elevated disposition, does not conform to their entirely worldly interests.[^2730]

The second psalm of the Hour, Psalm 24 [LXX 24; Ps 25], **"Unto Thee, O Lord, *(Orig. p. 741)* have I lifted up my soul"** [*K Tebe, Gospodi, vozdvigokh dushu moyu*]—an acrostic psalm—contains a series of prayerful appeals to God, not strictly connected but ever intensifying, for protection against enemies and guidance in fulfilling the at times difficult law, in the name of the eternal mercy of God toward the faithful.

The third psalm of the Hour, Psalm 50 [LXX 50; Ps 51]—the penitential **"Have mercy upon me, O God"** [*Pomiluy mya Bozhe*]—implores forgiveness and the complete cleansing of sin (wherein reference is made to the Old Testament rite of purification through washing, which gave rise to Christian baptism) in the name of the mercy of God, but also on the foundation of profound repentance, for the cleansing of inborn sinfulness; this prayer then intensifies into a prayer for the granting of the Spirit of righteousness and purity, and concludes according to custom with a vow: to convert the lawless and to glorify God instead of offering material

=== LEAF p898 ===

...sacrifices.[^2731]

The Lenten troparion of the Third Hour, commemorating the Descent of the Holy Spirit, asks that He not be taken from us, and that He be renewed within us; the Theotokion calls the Mother of God the vine that brought forth Christ, having obviously in mind the Savior's comparison of Himself to the vine (John 15), the branches of which are the Apostles (as the first to receive the Holy Spirit), and asks for prayers from them and all the saints (this hymn frequently serves as the Theotokion for great saints).

The verse of the Third Hour from Psalm 67:20 [LXX 67:19; Ps 68:19] contains an ecstatic glorification of God (**"Blessed be the Lord God, blessed be the Lord..."**—the same phrase twice with an insignificant variation, out of the fullness of emotion), a glorification that is due unto Him daily, and consequently on this newly begun, now fully unfolded day, and which is united with hope in His help in the work of salvation, the uniquely divine work.

Among the Lenten troparia after the Trisagion at the Third Hour: the first (Tone 8), **"Blessed art Thou, O Christ our God"** [*Blagosloven yesi Khriste Bozhe nash*], glorifies the event commemorated at this Hour; the second, **"A speedy and firm consolation"** [*Skoroe i izvestnoe*], asks for the abiding presence of Christ with us, resembling His abiding presence with the Apostles (after the Descent of the Holy Spirit); the Theotokion, **"The hope and protection"** [*Nadezhda i predstatel'stvo*], expresses hope in the help of the Mother of God and asks for mercy through Her unceasing (not merely at specific hours, as with us) intercessions.

The priestly exclamation of the Third Hour (after the prayer **"Thou Who at all times"**), as also of the Sixth, **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers,"** is of a more joyful tone than at the First and Ninth Hours, in view of the commemoration linked with the Hour and the proximity of the Divine Liturgy.

The concluding prayer of the Hour, likewise in view of this commemoration, is addressed distinctly to all three Persons of the Holy Trinity, Whom it implores for salvation. The prayer is attributed to St. Mardarius, an Armenian martyr under Diocletian, and was pronounced by him under torture that culminated in his death—a torture consisting of being hung upside down and burned with red-hot iron; to his fellow martyr, St. Eustratius, belongs the prayer of the Saturday Midnight Office, **"Magnifying I magnify Thee"** [*Velichaya velichayu Tya*].

*(Orig. p. 742)*

### THE SIXTH HOUR

#### Content of the Hour

The Sixth Hour is dedicated to the commemoration of the Crucifixion of Christ itself (according to the *Instructional Notice* of the *Sluzhebnik*—of its circumstances from the bearing of the Cross until the darkening of the sun).

All the psalms of the Hour depict the persecution of the righteous one by enemies seeking his destruction.

The first psalm of the Hour, Psalm 53 [LXX 53; Ps 54], **"Save me, O God, in Thy name"** [*Bozhe, vo imya Tvoe spasi mya*], bearing the infrequent inscription "Instruction" (*Maskil*), asks God, for the sake of His very name (the preservation of its holiness), for salvation in a righteous cause from terrible enemies who forget God, expresses certainty in this, and offers a vow of thanksgiving.

The second psalm of the Hour, Psalm 54 [LXX 54; Ps 55], **"Give ear, O God, unto my prayer"** [*Vnushi, Bozhe, molitvu moyu*], is an agitated prayer to God in great affliction,[^2734] reaching even to the desire completely to abandon the city filled with violence and strife (guarded as if by an evil watch upon its walls), especially in view of a friend turned enemy;[^2735] a prayer for the destruction of the enemies, interwoven with the expression of complete certainty in salvation.

The third psalm of the Hour—Psalm 90 [LXX 90; Ps 91], **"He that dwelleth in the aid of the Most High"** [*Zhivyy v pomoshchi Vyshnyago*]—sketches in the form of an instruction all the benefits of complete trust in God (Who is called here by the most sublime titles), Who protects the one hoping in Him as with wings (perhaps an allusion to the Cherubim) from silently ambushing death (the snare of the fowlers), from all dangers of night and day that destroy thousands,[^2736] and from noxious beasts by means of Angels who do not allow him even to stumble against a stone; wherein a solemn assurance is given to us by God Himself, promising, moreover, to the one who trusts all earthly blessings and salvation.[^2737]

The Lenten troparion of the Hour, **"Thou Who on the sixth day and hour"** [*Izhe v shestyy den' zhe i chas*], commemorating the Crucifixion, asks for the forgiveness of sins and for salvation; the Theotokion **"Seeing that we have no boldness"** [*Yako ne imamy derznoveniya*] urgently implores the intercessions of the Mother of God in view of our sinfulness, redeemed by the sufferings of Her Son (the reflection on the latter being most general; in the Theotokion of the Ninth Hour it is more detailed).

The verse of the Hour (Psalm 78:8–9 [LXX 78:8–9]), **"Let Thy tender mercies speedily anticipate us,"** contains the prayer of a suffering soul for divine help and the purging of sins for the glory of the name of God.

The Lenten troparia after the Trisagion, **"Thou hast wrought salvation,"** **"To Thine immaculate Icon,"** speak of the Crucifixion, but the Theotokion is of such
