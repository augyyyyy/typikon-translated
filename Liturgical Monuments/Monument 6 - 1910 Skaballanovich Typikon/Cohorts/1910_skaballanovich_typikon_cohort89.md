...as they are filled with direct exhortations and reproaches to the brethren (beginning: **"My brethren and fathers"** [*Bratia moyi i ottsy*]). For the same reason, a "preface" (naming the author and the reading) is out of place before them, just as it is not pronounced before the Paschal Catechetical Homily of St. John Chrysostom at Paschal Matins, which corresponds exactly to the Studite catecheses. The abbot in this case represents the Venerable Father himself, and the catechesis is presented as issuing from the mouth of the saint himself, invisibly standing among the worshipers. Therefore, after the catechesis *(Orig. p. 727)* the worshipers respond to it with gratitude personally addressed to St. Theodore in the form of his troparion, Tone 8: **"Instructor of Orthodoxy"** (*"Pravoslaviya nastavniche"*), seek on November 11, "without a Theotokion," as noted in the Typikon rubric for the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee.[^2714]

#### History

Concerning the profound antiquity of the custom for the abbot or someone else from the monastery administration to offer an instruction to the brethren several times a week, chiefly on Sunday, wherein discussion of it was also permitted (in Jerome's *Rules of St. Pachomius*, *catechesis* and *disputationes*)—see Introductory Chapter, pp. 193, 202, 213.

The catechesis in the strict sense is not mentioned by the *Hypotyposis*, *Diatyposis*, Evergetis Typikon, or Western Studite recensions, but only by the Studite-Alexis Typikon, and among the Jerusalem recensions in this present place, for some reason, only the most ancient manuscript—the Georgian (see above, p. 724); yet all mention it briefly under the Sunday of the Publican and Thomas Sunday.[^2715]

Old Believer Typikon:
"And then the Studite catechesis is offered, and the abbot reads it himself, or chooses one of the priests or of the senior brethren; and upon the completion of the reading, the clerics say the troparion, Tone 8: **'Instructor of Orthodoxy'** [*'Pravoslaviyu nastavniche'*], the end being sung; and the second choir says: **'Glory... both now...'**, Theotokion, in the same tone: **'Who for our sake was born'** [*'Izhe nas radi rozhdeysya'*]."[^2716]

### NON-ORTHODOX MATINS

In comparison with our own, non-Orthodox morning offices exhibit the following principal differences:

The Armenian Matins, consisting of the Midnight Office and "Dawn," has at the former: 7 prayers, 5 instructions, 5 divisions of the Psalter (of 8 in total), and several hymns; at the latter: 7 prayers (one with kneeling), 3 instructions (one of which is an "exhortation to prayer"), several hymns, and Psalms 112, 5, 114, 141, 53, and 85:17 [LXX].

The Coptic Matins, consisting of the Midnight Office and Matins proper, has at the former, besides our Psalms 3, 117, and 118 [LXX]: Psalms 6, 69, 85, and 116 [LXX]; the Gospel of Matthew 25 (concerning the ten virgins), Luke 7:36 (concerning the creditor and debtors), Luke 12:32 (concerning the expectation of the Second Coming), penitential sessional hymns, laudatory verses with **"Rejoice"** (*Chaire*) to the Mother of God, and a prayer; at the latter, it has at the beginning 19 psalms—besides our 62 and 142 [LXX], also 1–6, 8, 11–12, 14, 15, 18, 24, 26, 66, 69, and 112 [LXX]; the Gospel of John 1:1–16; the Creed; a series of brief prayer *(Orig. p. 728)* exclamations; and 2 prayers (Introductory Chapter, pp. 299–303).

The Abyssinian [Ethiopian] Matins of the ancient type has almost no psalms (only a few psalm verses); it opens with a long series of prayer invocations listing the works of creation and providence, appeals to various saints, an enumeration of their struggles, and the virtues of the Mother of God; it has two long series of readings from the New Testament—the first of 9 readings and the second of 4 (on the resurrection, light, vigilance, baptism, and the Second Coming), separated by the Canticles of Moses, of the Three Youths, and of Simeon the God-Receiver, and concluded by lengthy hymns to the Most Holy Trinity, to Christ, and to the saints, and a paraphrase of the Lord's Prayer (with frequent *Alleluia*).

The Abyssinian Matins of the later type (with Greek influence), consisting of the "Midnight Prayer," the "Prayer at the Third Hour of the Night," and the "Prayer at Cockcrow," has at the first service, besides our Psalms 118, 50, 117, and 3 [LXX]: Psalms 133, 6, 12, 69, 85, 90, 116, and 119–129 [LXX] ("the vesperal psalms"), the Gospel of Matthew 23:1–13 (on the Second Coming) and Luke 7:36–50 (the supper in Simon's house), the troparia **"Behold, the Bridegroom"** (*"Se Zhenikh"*) and penitential troparia, and a prayer to the Savior; at the second service, besides our Psalm 50 [LXX], the Canticles of Moses and of the Three Youths, and several litanies: "the compline psalms," Psalm 135 [LXX], a prayerful paraphrase of those two canticles (following them), and the Gospel of Matthew 25:31–46 (on the Last Judgment); at the third service, besides our Psalms 148–150 [LXX], a twofold Great Doxology, the Canticles of the Theotokos, of Zacharias, of Anna, of Simeon, and of Hezekiah: the Creed, several prayers to Christ and to the Mother of God,

### THE PRACTICAL FEASIBILITY OF THE ENTIRE ALL-NIGHT VIGIL TYPIKON
*(EXCURSUS)*

The celebration of the All-Night Vigil according to all the above-expounded requirements of the Typikon—with the singing, for example, of the entirety of Psalm 103 (with refrains to every verse), all 8 psalms of the First Kathisma, the prolonged singing of the two great Polyeleos psalms or the immense Psalm 118, and especially with the singing of the 120 irmosi and troparia of the Kanon, to say nothing of the multitude of stichera, sessional hymns, troparia, etc.—the celebration of a Vigil with such a massive volume of singing, and moreover with 7 readings, may appear decidedly unfeasible. And the practice of simultaneous chanting (*mnogoglasiye*) in the Russian Church during the 16th–17th centuries, that is, in the era when the All-Night Vigil concluded its historical development, would seem historically to confirm such an opinion. And yet, as if to provide a factual refutation of an opinion so disastrous for the Typikon, an experiment of such a Vigil was recently undertaken at the Kyiv Theological Academy—in all probability the first and only one in Russia since the 17th century (it is well known that in not a single Russian monastery today is the Kanon sung in its complete form, its troparia being merely read, just as nowhere is the entirety of the First Kathisma, the Polyeleos, or the Evlogitaria / Amomos [Psalm 118] sung).

The thought of such an All-Night Vigil originated two years ago simultaneously in the minds of two Academy professors: the Professor of Liturgics, Priest V. D. Prylutsky, and the author of the present book. Having deliberated on the matter, they decided that executing this intention would require an expenditure of a minimum of 300 rubles for two choirs (which, according to the principle of antiphonal singing upon which our entire liturgical order is constructed, must be of absolutely equal competence and balance) and not less than two months of rigorous preparation (rehearsals). When the aforementioned professors shared these aspirations with their like-minded colleague in liturgical scholarship, Bishop Gabriel of Akkerman (Chepur), he reproached them for their naivety, stating that this would require 3,000 rubles and 2 years of preparation.

The idea of an ideal All-Night Vigil, thus hovering in the air, sank into the hearts of two devout fourth-year students: Priest Z. T. Saplin (a native of Kursk) and I. A. Lagovsky (a native of Kostroma; the previously mentioned individuals were natives of Ryazan, Lithuania, and Kherson). They resolved to make of this Vigil a kind of vow-offering, and found among the student-singers about 20 individuals who expressed their readiness, at no small risk to their vocal cords, to sing even the entire night through, from 6:00 PM until morning. About 7 of these 20 were

=== LEAF p886 ===

fourth-year students; next in number followed first-year students, then second-year, and finally third-year; ethnically, the performers were divided roughly equally between Great Russians and Little Russians [Ukrainians].

The All-Night Vigil was initially intended for the eve of December 5, as the feast of St. Sabbas the Sanctified; however, due to various considerations, November 10–11 was chosen—a Sunday with Tone 1, musically the richest. Rehearsals lasted about a month and a half. The site chosen for the Vigil was the small Holy Spirit *(Orig. p. 731)* Church of the Brotherhood Monastery (within whose precinct the Academy was situated). Benches were arranged along all the walls of the church. The Rector of the Academy, Bishop Innocent, who regarded the enterprise with great sympathy, consented—in order not to impede the complete execution of the Typikon requirements, which, as is well known, do not envision the hierarch's active presiding at the Vigil—merely to assume the position of the Abbot [Proestos] and restrict himself to the functions assigned to the latter in the Typikon (honor was paid to his episcopal rank in the church only upon the conclusion of the Vigil by the singing of *'Eis polla eti, Despota'* and the festive tolling of bells). He took his place in the center of the nave against the wall, wearing a monastic mantle. The celebrants of the Vigil were a fourth-year student, Hieromonk Antony (Romanovsky), and a fourth-year student, Hierodeacon Nektary (Trezvinsky).

At 6:00 PM, with the royal doors closed, the censing of the sanctuary began. Prior to this, the Rector invited those present to sit (unfortunately, there were by no means enough seats for everyone). After the censing of the sanctuary, the curtain and the holy doors opened, before which a student-novice in monastic habit holding a tall candle had taken his stand beforehand, fulfilling the duties of the paraekklesiarches. At the moment the holy doors opened, he exclaimed: **"Arise!"** [*Vozstanite*]. Through the holy doors came forth the hieromonk with the censer (without the deacon, wearing mantle and epitrachelion). In sepulchral silence, broken only by the clanking of the censer chains, an extensive censing of the entire three-aisled church was performed, during which the celebrants of the censing traced a sixfold wave-like path (along each side of the walls and pillars), the end of which brought them into the narthex; when they exited it following the censing, the paraekklesiarches exclaimed: **"Lord, bless!"** [*Gospodi blagoslovi*]. Those performing the censing proceeded to the holy doors, and having performed there the prescribed concluding censing of the principal icons, the priest made the opening blessing. Fulfilling the duties of the ekklesiarches, Priest V. D. Prylutsky sang solo: **"Amen. Come, let us worship..."** [*Amin. Priidite poklonimsya...*], and the beginning of Psalm 103 with the refrain. The psalm was sung verse-by-verse by two choirs with the prescribed refrains after each verse according to the Bakhmetev chant. Immediately after the exclamation, the royal doors

=== LEAF p887 ===

were closed, and the priest came out and took his stand at the right end of the solea before an analogion and an icon, where he remained standing for nearly the entire All-Night Vigil. The unhurried singing of the psalm, with what seemed like an endless repetition of each of its three refrains, and with attention completely undistracted by anything (not even by a censing), revealed to the mind ever new insights in every verse. At verse 24 of the psalm (**"In wisdom hast Thou made them all"**), the priest approached the royal doors to read the Prayers of Light [Lucernarium prayers]. Rapture shone upon every face. The psalm was concluded at 6:35 PM.

**"Blessed is the man"** [*Blazhen muzh*] was likewise sung to the Bakhmetev chant with a threefold *Alleluia* after every verse; yet the singing of the first *Glory* (Stasis), that is, Psalms 1, 2, and 3, occupied only about 10 minutes. This was owing to the lively antiphonal execution: the second choir began its verse while the first was still finishing its own. The second and third *Glories* of the First Kathisma, that is, Psalms 4–8, were sung to the troparion melody of Tone 1 (the ordinary chant), that is, the manner in which the Typika psalms are sung at the Divine Liturgy. In this, it seems, the choir director made an error: they ought to have been sung to the sticheric melody, since the troparic melody begins at the Vigil only after *(Orig. p. 732)* **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** [*Nyne otpushchayeshi*] and thereafter dominates unchallenged (in the sessional hymns and hypakoe) almost until the Kanon.

On the other hand, a deeply moving feature was introduced into the singing of these 5 psalms: on the basis of the ancient Typikon for singing kathismata (see above, p. 633), each psalm verse was divided in singing between the two choirs, that is, the right choir sang the first half of each verse, and the left sang the second half. Since biblical poetry is founded upon the parallelism of members—where the second half of each verse contains a parallel thought to the first, developing and completing the thought of the first—this manner of chanting positively astonished the mind with unexpected insights and juxtapositions.

For example:
*Right choir*: **"Offer the sacrifice of righteousness,**
*Left choir*: **and hope in the Lord.**
*Right choir*: **Many say: Who will show us good things?**
*Left choir*: **The light of Thy countenance hath been signed upon us, O Lord."** (Ps 4:6–7 [LXX 4:6–7; Ps 4:5–6])
*Right choir*: **"All sheep and oxen,**
*Left choir*: **yea, and the beasts of the field."** (Ps 8:8 [LXX 8:8; Ps 8:7])

The litanies after the 1st and 2nd Stases were pronounced by the priest, and after the 3rd by the deacon.
Toward **"Lord, I have cried"** [*Gospodi vozzvakh*], the candles before the icons were lit, and the church immediately took on a more festive appearance; up to this point, only oil lamps had been burning, and there had been semi-darkness. **"Lord, I have cried"**, preceded by the intonation of the Tone, was sung to the Great Znamenny

=== LEAF p888 ===

chant; portions of the first two verses were sung solo by a bass, while the entire choir responded at the appointed places (more frequently than in the ordinary chant) with **"Hear me, O Lord"** [*Uslyshi mya Gospodi*]. The remaining verses of Psalms 140 and 141 were chanted in recitative, as in the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra. The Sunday stichera were sung to the Bakhmetev chant, but the 6 Menaion stichera were sung to their appointed model melodies (*podobny*, according to the Lavra melody), on account of which the model melody was intoned before each group: before the first—**"As noble"** (*Yako dobla*), before the second—**"O most glorious wonder"** (*O preslavnago chudese*).

It is futile to describe to anyone who has not heard these melodies all their beauty, majesty, and compunction. Let us note only that upon the words of the sticheron to the Holy Martyr Stephanida: *"Bound unto two palm trees, thou wast torn asunder, / and didst fly up unto God like a sparrow,"* fell musical notes that expressed both the entire horror of this torment (the tearing asunder of the human body by two trees bent down and then released from men's hands) and the complete triumph over it achieved by the martyr's noble spirit, so that everyone capable of discerning these words and perceiving how their full horror was conveyed by the music could not help but tremble. The impression made by these melodies was heightened by the fact that the stichera, like all the chants at the Vigil, were sung without a canonarch (even in the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra, the finest idiomelon stichera [*samoglasny*], such as those for the Nativity of Christ, the Dormition, and the Annunciation, are sung without a canonarch). At the appointed place, the choirs came together in the center.

The Entrance, preceded by two immense candles borne by two monks in mantles, was performed only at 7:35 PM—that is, 1 1/2 hours after the beginning of the service, an amount of time within which, with energetic dispatch, an entire ordinary Vigil can be served. The prokeimenon, instead of being intoned, was sung solo by the canonarch (prior to its singing by the combined choirs to a modified *(Orig. p. 733)* Lavra melody), and its verses were likewise chanted by the canonarch, but in recitative (they ought to have been sung like the prokeimenon itself).

The departure for the Litiya (at 7:43 PM) was made through the north door with the royal doors closed, preceded by two candles carried by monks in mantles; the priest wore only the epitrachelion. The singers, having united their choirs and formed into ranks of four abreast, followed the priest singing **"Most glorious things today"** [*Preslavnaya dnes*], and at **"Glory... both now..."**—**"O Heavenly King"** [*Tsaryu Nebesny*] (the temple stichera). The Litiya procession advanced into the narthex itself, into which most of the worshipers also proceeded (the narthex proved to be quite commodious), including the Bishop. **"Lord, have mercy"** [*Gospodi pomiluy*] was counted by one of the singers. The Aposticha were sung (to a simple chant) by the combined choirs, who had formed a semicircle before the analogion. The royal doors were not opened even for the blessing of the loaves. Psalm 33, as later Psalm 50, was spoken aloud (following the rubric's direction: *"we say"* [*glagolem*]) by one of the choirs; the effect was exceedingly powerful.

After **"The blessing of the Lord be upon you"** [*Blagoslovenie Gospodne na vas*], the "Great Reading" took place. The analogion for the reader was placed at the left end of the solea, obliquely toward the iconostasis and the people. The continuous reading appointed for the day was the entirety of the Second Epistle to the Thessalonians. It was read by the Inspector of the Academy and Professor of the New Testament, Archpriest D. I. Bogdashevsky (without epitrachelion, of course), who sought by means of vocal inflection to invest into the text being read (the reading, like the others, was delivered in a natural conversational cadence, albeit in Church Slavonic) all the profound understanding accumulated through long years of scholarly research.

The concluding **"Amen"** of the Epistle was pronounced not by him, but, as the Typikon requires, by the reader of the Six Psalms—who, likewise according to the Typikon, in his capacity as the Abbot [Proestos], was the Rector-Bishop (who had also previously read **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** and **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"**). During the reading of the Epistle, the singers were given a prosphora and a cup of wine each, and the people received prosphora moistened with wine. With the beginning of the reading, the church was plunged once more into darkness (which lasted until **"God is the Lord, and hath appeared unto us"** [*Bog Gospod i yavisya nam*, Hebrew: "shone forth"]).

Matins began at 8:40 PM. The Great Litany was pronounced by the priest. **"God is the Lord"** was executed exactly like the prokeimenon (see above). The kathismata were read in the center of the church by a student. The sessional hymns were sung to the touchingly prolonged melody of the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra (which shares something in common with Passion chants; the Tone 2 sessional hymn being a genuine Passion chant, **"Noble Joseph"** [*Blagoobrazny Iosif*]). Chanting them while seated (*"seated we sing"* [*sedyashche poyem*]) could not be accomplished on account of the cramped space of the kliroses.

After the First Kathisma and its sessional hymns, the Rector read from the solea from Theophylact's *Blagovestnik* [Explanation of the Gospels] the commentary on the morrow's Gospel, titled: *"Homily of Blessed Theophylact, Archbishop of Bulgaria, on the Gospel according to Luke."* Due to the brevity of the Gospel reading (concerning the foolish rich man), the reading took up about two folio pages of spaced print.

The remaining readings were read by a second-year student, V. P. Afonsky (a native of Kyiv). Following the Second Kathisma, the Polyeleos, and the Third Ode of the Kanon, the commentary of the Apostle Paul upon the morrow's Epistle was read; this commentary occupied 5 folio pages of extremely fine print in two columns, and despite rather brisk reading it proved somewhat lengthy, on account of which it became necessary to abandon the original intention of reading from the *Margarit* [The Pearls] after Ode 3 of the Kanon, concerning which, moreover, the Typikon speaks very vaguely. *(Orig. p. 734)* The readings were directed by the author of the present book, for whom it required no small labor to track down the necessary Slavonic editions; and the "Studite Catechesis" required for the reading was discovered only in what was virtually a unique manuscript of the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra, the excerpting of which was delayed, making it necessary to translate it from Russian into Church Slavonic.

The pinnacle of the Vigil proved to be the Polyeleos. Sung

=== LEAF p890 ===

to the majestic, distinctly triumphant melody of the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra, these 47 verses of the Polyeleos psalms seemed to form an unending hymn to the Creator and Provider. An inexplicable mystery seemed to breathe from the twofold mention of Og and Sihon (**"And Og the king of Bashan: Alleluia, alleluia; for His mercy endureth unto all ages: Alleluia"** [Ps 135:20; LXX 135:20]). The illuminated chandelier heightened the impression. And through all this, strangely, the royal doors remained continuously closed. For the singers, the Polyeleos was an extraordinary success. By common consensus, the Vigil here surpassed the boldest expectations. The fervor of the worshipers reached its apogee.

**"The assembly of the Angels"** [*Angelsky sobor*—the Evlogitaria of the Resurrection] was sung to the jubilant, rapid Lavra melody (resembling the hurried running of the Myrrh-bearing women to and from the tomb). The Hypakoe was sung solo to the melody of the sessional hymns by a fine tenor; an intense silence settled over the church, remarkably illustrating the very name of this chant (*"attention"* [from Greek *hypakoe*], or more accurately, *"refrain"*). And once again, a reading.

With the beginning of the Gradual Antiphons [Anabathmoi], the royal doors were opened, and they remained open until the Gospel was carried back into the sanctuary during Ode 1 of the Kanon. The Gradual Antiphons were sung to the Lavra melody, each twice. It was a majestic antiphonal dialogue between the kliroses. Psalm 50 was spoken in unison by the left kliros. Everyone was especially moved by the fact that the Gospel for veneration was held "upon his breast" by the priest, flanked by two candles.

The Kanon was begun at 10:45 PM and concluded at 12:50 AM. It was sung to the Bakhmetev chant, while **"I shall open my mouth"** [*Otverzu usta moya*—the Katabasia] at the coming together of the choirs was sung to the Lavra melody. The verses of the Biblical Canticles sounded especially powerful. Each ode of the Kanon seemed like something vast and unending. When those present saw that absolutely all 14 troparia of each ode were being sung, some began to fear for the vocal cords of the singers, all the more so since their ever-growing enthusiasm seemed to cause them to sing louder and louder. One venerable listener implored the organizers to curtail this "dangerous experiment." But that would have meant abandoning the enterprise at the very end. The kontakia, ikoses, and sessional hymns after Ode 3 were sung to the ordinary troparic melody, since there were seven of them in all; likewise the Sunday Kontakion and Ikos after Ode 6.

The reading after Ode 3 once again plunged the church into darkness, which persisted until the beginning of Ode 9, when the lighted candles remained unextinguished until the dismissal. During the reading of the Prologue, which comprised 10 folio pages, the author made a circuit of the seated congregation to observe the composition of the worshipers who had "endured unto the end" [cf. Matt 10:22]. He was struck by the substantial percentage of Academy students and educated women of various ages, from dignified matrons to teenage gymnasium students. There were also many educated men. In all, there were probably about 200 worshipers.
