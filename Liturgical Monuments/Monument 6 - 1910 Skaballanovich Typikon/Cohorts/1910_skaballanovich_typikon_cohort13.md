its relation unto the Jewish Passover; hitherto Christians, apparently, had always celebrated Pascha simultaneously with or immediately following the Jewish Passover; now they do not trust the Jewish reckoning of Passover and establish their own Paschalion [^558], in consequence of which Christian Pascha at times diverges significantly in time from the Jewish. This gives rise to the need for a preliminary determination of the date of Pascha, an endeavor in which in the 3rd century St. Hippolytus, Bishop of Portus (the harbor of Rome), and Dionysius, Bishop of Alexandria, were engaged [^559]. In the 4th century this question was definitively resolved by the Church in the decree to celebrate Pascha on the first Sunday after the full moon occurring on or after March 21 [^560]. The *Apostolic Constitutions*, apparently, already know such a decree.

*(Orig. p. 124)*

Furthermore, characteristic is the view of these canonical monuments regarding Pascha as a propitiatory fast for the Jews, alongside which the very commemoration of Christ's Passion seems to recede into the background.

Alongside this latter commemoration there already emerges (as became noticeable in Tertullian) as an element in the concept of the feast also the commemoration of Christ's Resurrection. Yet this element does not yet receive in these monuments equal status with the sorrowful element of the festival. The Paschal vigil according to the monuments appears dedicated to the Passion and death of Christ, and only its conclusion receives a joyful character—in honor of Christ's Resurrection. The present Church Typikon with striking fidelity reproduces the spirit of this practice, when the Paschal vigil is composed of Passion services (the Canon of Great and Holy Saturday) and Sunday services (Paschal Matins); these two halves of the Paschal vigil even in the present Typikon are regarded as completely equal in standing; this is evident from the fact that: (a) they are of equal duration; (b) upon the coincidence of the Annunciation with Pascha, the Canon of the Annunciation is sung not only at Paschal Matins, but also with the Canon of Great and Holy Saturday.

The examined canonical monuments of the 3rd century almost agree in defining the hour at which the Paschal fast is broken. According to the *Canons of Hippolytus* this is done at dawn; according to the *Didascalia*, at the third hour of the night; according to the *Apostolic Constitutions*, at cockcrow. Yet Dionysius of Alexandria observes that in his time there was no uniformity in this regard: "Those who hasten and arise before midnight, we rebuke as disdainful and intemperate; but those who defer and manfully persevere until the fourth watch itself, we receive as noble and patient in labors; while toward those who break the fast between these terms, according as they have purpose or strength, toward them we are not harsh" [^561].

#### Pascha According to the *Testamentum*

At the same stage of development is Pascha represented also by the *Testamentum of Our Lord Jesus Christ*, whose prescriptions concerning Pascha significantly supplement the information given by the examined monuments concerning it from the 3rd century. If from the latter we become acquainted only with the general character and spirit of the feast in that era, the *Testamentum* provides also its detailed ritual. Yet many passages in this section of the *Testamentum* are exceedingly obscure. The monument speaks of Pascha in connection with baptism. Having stated that the catechumens designated for baptism are exorcised from the day of their selection, the *Testamentum* continues:

"As the days *(Orig. p. 125)* of Pascha draw near, the bishop exorcises each one individually... On the fifth day of the final week (Great Thursday) they must wash themselves and anoint the head (with oil?)... On Friday and Saturday they fast. On Saturday a general exorcism before baptism is performed over all by the bishop. (Here occurs the above-cited remark concerning forty days of fasting). On the final Saturday they keep vigil by night (the faithful), when the catechumens are exorcised until midnight of that Saturday" [^562].

There follow descriptions of baptism, chrismation, and the reception of communion; and after a brief parenthetical observation concerning the order of charity (with which the Eucharist was customarily accompanied), the monument returns to the rites of Pascha:

"On the fifth day of the final week of Pascha (Great Thursday) bread and the cup are offered, and He Who suffered for the one who offered is Himself both the One Who is and the One Who approaches (an obscure expression, rendered by the Coptic-Arabic version: 'on the fifth day in the evening the priest offers bread and the cup mixed with water and wine, for the celebration of the mystery of Pascha; likewise he does also on Saturday'). A lamp is brought into the temple by the deacon, who says: **'The Grace of Our Lord be with you all'** (*«Благодать Господа нашего со всеми вами»*), and all the people respond: **'And with Thy spirit'** (*«И со духом Твоим»*). The boys recite spiritual psalms and hymns at the lighting of the lamp. All the people, chanting with harmonious voice, respond: **'Alleluia'** (*«Аллилуиа»*). And no one kneels until the speaker (the youth?) finishes. Exactly so also if a reading is read or a word of instruction is delivered. When, however, he utters the Name of the Lord or anything else (similar), as has been said,

=== LEAF p123 ===
no one falls prostrate. The breaking of the Paschal fast takes place at midnight which follows Saturday. In Pentecost no one fasts and none bend their knees, for these are days of rest and joy. Those burdened with heavy labors rest somewhat during the days of Pentecost and the Great Day of the Lord. The bishop before offering the sacrifice pronounces in the established order that which is appointed for the sacrifice, while those clothed in white alternately with one another say: **'Alleluia'** (*«Аллилуиа»*)" [^563].

Further in the *Testamentum* there follow prescriptions concerning order at the agapes, the offering of firstfruits, memorial alms, the prayer of blessing over the loaves, again concerning firstfruits, and concerning prayer before taking food [^564]; immediately whereupon the monument continues:

"On the days of Pascha, especially the final ones—on Friday and Saturday—by day and by night [^565] there are as many prayers as hymns; the instruction is long, the readings diverse and frequent. Vigils and nocturnal matins are celebrated with beauty and solemnity. The deacons who walk among the women, lest any illicit young men be found among them, are assisted by the readers. Over this also the subdeacons preside. They do not permit the youth to sleep; for that night, preeminently that which follows Saturday, is an image of the Kingdom of Heaven. Those who labor and grow weary may become exhausted before midnight; (therefore) first the catechumens are dismissed, having received a blessing from the breaking of bread. The faithful, when they are dismissed, go in order and modestly unto their homes; and they do not abandon prayer even at their repasts. The priests, however, do not interrupt their service. The women depart, each following *(Orig. p. 126)* her husband (simultaneously with their husbands). The widows, however, remain until morning in the temple, receiving here also their sustenance. The virgins also remain in the temple, and the bishop provides them assistance and care, while the deacons minister unto them. The presbyters remain with the bishop until morning, praying and resting. Exactly so also do the newly baptized. The virgin betrothed, however, accompany their mothers and go home; this is more decorous. The bishop commands it to be proclaimed that no one taste anything until the sacrifice is offered and the body of the Church receives new nourishment. Then from the beginning of the evening those who are to be baptized are baptized after the first reading. If anyone, before he receives the Eucharistic communion, tastes anything,

=== LEAF p124 ===
he becomes guilty, and the fast wherewith he fasted shall not be accounted unto him at all. Upon the catechumens, when they are dismissed, hands are laid. If any of the faithful remains away through sickness, the deacon carries unto him the sacrifice, that is, the Eucharistic communion. If a presbyter cannot come unto church, a presbyter carries unto him (the communion). If a woman is ill through pregnancy and cannot keep the fast of those two days, let her fast the second day, and let her take bread and water at the first hour; and if she cannot approach (unto communion in church), let a deaconess carry the communion unto her. Concerning those who are sick, let them inform the bishop, that he may visit them as far as possible; for hereby great consolation is afforded unto the sick" [^566].

So far as these unsystematic and frequently digressive prescriptions permit us to judge, the feast of Pascha according to the *Testamentum* possessed the following order. The sorrowful-joyful solemnity began on Great Thursday, when there was celebrated with special solemnity and special rites (the most important of which were the lighting of the lamp and the chanting of the Alleluia) the Eucharist, yet a kind of peculiar one, perhaps without communion, as may be suggested by the obscure expression: "He Who suffered (Christ) for the one who offered is Himself both the One Who is and the One Who approaches"; communion was perhaps not received in view of the fast. From that same day, apparently, genuflections were discontinued in view of Pascha and throughout the whole of Pentecost (just as today they are discontinued from Wednesday evening). Friday and Saturday were spent in the strictest fast and unceasing day and night services, performed, as the monument notes, with special magnificence, wherein there were exceptionally lengthy readings (*paroimiai*), yet they were interspersed with an equal number of hymns (cf. the present manner of reading the Old Testament lessons on Great and Holy Saturday). On Saturday, after a solemn exorcism, the catechumens were baptized. Marked by exceptional duration was the vigil in the night following Saturday. At this vigil or immediately following it, the Paschal Eucharist was celebrated, which—in view of the fact that it was prepared for by a two-day fast, and in view of the fact that during it the newly baptized received communion for the first time—acquired exceptional significance: it had to be tasted before the reception of any other food whatsoever, and was administered decisively to "the entire body of the Church," for which reason the Holy Gifts were sent out even to the absent and the sick. (This latter circumstance well explains *(Orig. p. 127)* the present Communion Verse (*Koinonikon*) at the Paschal Divine Liturgy: **"Receive the Body of Christ"** [*«Тело Христово приимите»*]).

#### Pentecost

=== LEAF p125 ===
In connection with Pascha, almost all writers and monuments of the 3rd century speak also of Pentecost, the first mention of which is found, as we have seen [^567], in a fragment of St. Irenaeus (2nd century). Here, however, one may observe that earlier writers, such as Tertullian, know Pentecost as the fifty-day interval of time that followed Pascha and was accounted as joyful days; whereas later writers, such as Origen, assign the name of Pentecost unto a single festival day. Proving the superiority of Christianity over paganism, Tertullian remarks: "Every pagan feast hath for itself one single day in the year; but for thee the resurrection is renewed every seven days; gather all pagan feasts together, arrange them in succession, and they will not be able to fill out Pentecost" [^568]. Discussing the most suitable time for baptism and pointing unto Pascha as the most solemn day for baptism, Tertullian adds: "An exceedingly significant interval of time for the administration of baptism is presented by Pentecost, when both the Risen Lord conversed among the disciples, and the grace of the Holy Spirit was given, and the hope of the Second Coming of the Lord shone forth; for then, upon His Ascension into heaven, the Angels said unto the Apostles that He would come in the same manner as He ascended into heaven—namely, (He will come) in Pentecost" [^569]. Having mentioned in another place the impermissibility of fasting and genuflection on Sunday, Tertullian adds: "with that same exemption (*immunitate*) we rejoice (*gaudemus*) from Pascha unto Pentecost" [^570]. Finally, in one passage Tertullian states that the "fifty days" from Pascha "we spend in all exultation" (*in omni exultatione*) [^571]. In Origen, on the other hand, the juxtaposition of Pentecost with Pascha and Friday presupposes for the former a single day of festival [^572]. The *Testamentum*, as we have seen, knows Pentecost both as a period and as a single-day feast with a solemn Liturgy equal unto that celebrated on Saturday, Sunday, Pascha, and Epiphany [^573]. Characteristic is the silence concerning Pentecost in the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*.

#### Epiphany (Theophany)

In the 3rd century the feast of the Baptism of the Lord, or Epiphany (Theophany), had its beginning. It is first mentioned by Clement of Alexandria [^574]. Speaking *(Orig. p. 128)* in the *Stromata* (*Miscellanies*) concerning various attempts and methods of establishing the chronology for the Incarnation and death of Christ, he remarks: "There are also those who, determining even more inquisitively not only the year, but also the day of the Nativity of our Savior, say that it occurred in the years of Augustus on the 25th day of Pachon (December or May); the followers of Basilides celebrate also the day of His Baptism, passing the entire preceding night in readings (*prodianyktereuontes anagnōsesi*, προδιανυκτερεύοντες ἀναγνώσεσι); they say that it occurred in the 15th year of Tiberius on the 15th of Tybi (January), while others say that it was on the 11th of the same

=== LEAF p126 ===
month" [^575]. Consequently, in Alexandria in Clement's time the Orthodox did not yet possess this feast. It is possible that the Basilidians arrived earlier than the Orthodox at the idea of celebrating the day of the Baptism of the Lord and named it Epiphany (Theophany), since according to the view of these heretics Christ became the Son of God precisely from the time of His Baptism and the descent upon Him of the Holy Spirit. Among the Orthodox the feast could have been established in counteraction unto the heretical festival, and into it, of course, a different meaning was invested. The mention of Epiphany in the *Testamentum* [^576] demonstrates that in the 3rd century the feast obtained wide dissemination. Unto these specified three feasts was the Church year of the 3rd century restricted: the feast of the Nativity of Christ becomes known later [^577], in the 4th century; the Ascension in the 5th century [^578]; others still later.

#### Commemorations of Martyrs

The veneration of *martyrs* in the 3rd century bore the same local character as in the 2nd century. The monuments of the 3rd century do not mention a single universal feast of the whole Church in honor of any saint or martyr. Yet, of course, reverence for the martyrs grew and prepared the idea of universal ecclesiastical commemoration for certain among them. Such martyrs as St. Laurence († 258) must have appalled the entire Christian world by the tortures they endured. The level reached by the veneration of martyrs and saints in the 3rd century is well demonstrated by the interpolation of the passage of the Syriac *Didascalia* concerning martyrs in the *Apostolic Constitutions*:

##### The Syriac *Didascalia*
"Therefore, blessed are the martyrs and pure from all sins and temptations, and removed from every unrighteousness, as is spoken of Christ in Isaiah: 57:1–2" [^579].

##### The *Apostolic Constitutions*
"And concerning the martyrs we say unto you, that they should be held by you in all honor, as also by us (the Apostles) are honored the blessed James the Bishop and our holy fellow-minister Stephen" [^580].

*(Orig. p. 129)*

These are the only saints whose (universal) commemoration is mentioned by the *Apostolic Constitutions*; consequently in the era of the *Didascalia* there did not yet exist even in their honor a universal commemoration.

=== LEAF p127 ===
Apparently, in the 3rd century no overly sharp boundary was yet drawn between the commemoration of martyrs and the remembrance of the ordinary deceased. Tertullian says: "we make oblations (*oblationes*) for the deceased (*pro defunctis*) on the anniversary day of their suffering (*pro natalitiis annua die*)" [^581]. "We celebrate," says Origen, "the commemoration of the saints and of our parents; likewise do we honor the memory of friends who die in the faith, both rejoicing over their repose and entreating for ourselves a righteous end in the faith. When their commemoration is performed," an agape takes place [^582], "so that our celebration may serve for the remembrance and repose of the soul whose memory is kept, and for us before the face of God may be a sweet-smelling savor" [^583]. Here the commemoration includes not so much prayers for the repose of the departed as it is permeated by a firm and joyful assurance of such repose. In the *Didascalia*, however, the character of the commemoration is already closer unto our own: "Do ye, however, according unto the Gospel and according unto the power of the Holy Spirit, assembling in the cemeteries (*memoriis*), perform the reading of the Holy Scriptures, and send up unceasingly unto God *prayers*, and offer the Eucharist, received in the likeness of the royal Body of Christ, both in all assemblies and in the burying-places and at the passing of the dying" [^584].

Making a brief survey of that which was accomplished by the 3rd century in the domain of divine worship—more particularly for the future Church Typikon—we find that, in regard to liturgical orders, there was now developed for the first time, in certain churches at least, a rather complex order of *Matins*, in some respects coinciding with our present service, as well as the order of the *introductory part of the Liturgy* directly adjoining it. In comparison with the 2nd century, attention is drawn in these services unto: (a) the formulation of prayers; (b) the establishment of a definite order for the reading of Holy Scripture; (c) the selection of specific melodies for ecclesiastical use from among the series of existing Greek chants. As regards the evening service, it was reduced entirely unto the *agape*, for which, in some churches at least, there was likewise a precisely elaborated rite; yet regulation here extended unto the ceremonial aspect of the service (the breaking of bread and distribution of the cup), but not unto its internal content: prayers, hymns, and instructions at the agape (in contrast unto Matins and the Liturgy), apparently, were left entirely unto the discretion of the participants under the oversight of the president, who in the absence of a bishop or presbyter could be a deacon and even a layman, if the agape was not conjoined with the Eucharist. Yet the agape was a comparatively rare and occasional service (if only for the sole reason that it was by no means inexpensive). Usually, however, the *evening* was sanctified by the same kind of semi-private, semi-public prayer as the other notable hours of the day, the worship *(Orig. p. 130)* of which, although no less than the agape left unto the freedom of each, consisted in the majority of cases in the chanting of psalms with diverse refrains,

=== LEAF p128 ===
predominantly with the Alleluia, and with the addition, presumably, of improvised prayers, which served, however, merely as an appendage unto the Lord's Prayer. As regards the *ecclesiastical year*, first of all the *feasts* are noticeably distinguished from weekdays; from among the days of the week *Saturday* begins to stand out in certain places; *Pascha* acquires for itself the most prominent, exceptional position in the calendar, altering also its former character—that of a fast in honor of Christ's Passion and death—unto a dual character of sorrow over them and joy over Christ's Resurrection. In quantitative terms, the Church year increases by only one feast in comparison with the 2nd century—*Epiphany (Theophany)*.

*(Orig. p. 131)*

### The 4th and 5th Centuries

The cessation of persecutions, the revivification of theology under the influence of the Christological controversies, and the flourishing of monasticism could not fail to exert the most salutary influence upon the development of divine worship in the 4th and 5th centuries. If already by the close of the 3rd century there was established in certain churches a firm and complex order—in addition to the Liturgy—also for the morning service as the most essential worship, then in the two subsequent centuries such an order was created for well-nigh the entire present cycle of daily offices. And although this order differed among individual churches, and unto subsequent times transmitted by no means everything from its composition, nevertheless by the beginning of the 6th century throughout the entire Christian East there was elaborated that rather uniform and stable type of divine worship—or more accurately, several such types—from whose modification and confluence by the beginning of the 9th century the present order of the daily cycle of worship was delineated in its essential features. At the same time, in these centuries—the 4th and 5th—there arose for the first time the Menologion (Calendar) of Saints, and the cycle of the Great Feasts expanded from the three or four of the preceding century unto almost their present number. Being the most fruitful in theology, with the most vigorous pulse of ecclesiastical life, these centuries were also the most creative in Church worship: it was fashioned now in the (immortal) foundations of its structure that have survived to this day, in its present form and schema, which the 6th through 8th centuries merely filled out with hymnographic content, and the 9th through 12th centuries polished unto aesthetic elegance.

#### The Abolition of the Agape

In the realm of liturgical orders, the 4th and 5th centuries were marked above all by *the abolition of the agape*.

The immediate cause of this abolition was the vast outward expansion of the Church, wherein there now appeared many unworthy members; among the latter the agape was transformed into that which it had once been for

=== LEAF p129 ===
the Gentile-Christians of the days of the Apostles Paul and Jude who were insufficiently imbued with the ideals of the new faith; and whereas such Christians were then few, and they gradually rose unto worthy participation in the agape—this "banquet of the Word" (to use the expression of Clement of Alexandria)—in the 4th and 5th centuries such Christians, tares among the wheat of the Church, grew ever more numerous, and the Church was compelled to renounce for a long time, if not forever, this ancient and exalted institution of hers. Furthermore, the agape did not entirely harmonize with that ascetic spirit wherewith, under the influence of an asceticism full of youthful freshness and vigor, Christian worship now began to be permeated.

The first half of the 4th century already requires an apology for the agape and an artificial preservation of it. The Emperor Constantine the Great mentions "the exceedingly modest banquets (*sōphronestata de kai symposia*, σωφρονέстата δὲ καὶ συμπόσια) instituted in memory *(Orig. p. 132)* of the martyrs by many, out of a feeling of pity for the indigent and for the relief of strangers and the poor," and remarks: "whoever considers them superfluous thinks not according to divine and most sacred doctrine" [^585]. The Council of Gangra (c. 340 or later) anathematizes those "who despise those who make agapes in faith and gather the brethren in honor of the Lord, and will not take part in these invitations, on the ground that they esteem this matter of no account" [^586]. Yet how vital and useful an institution the agape still was is evident from the fact that the Emperor Julian considers it an institution well-nigh the most dangerous in Christianity for paganism: he says that just as those who traffic in children entice them onto ships with dainties, so also Christians "by the same method begin (in their seduction of the pagans) with what are called among them agapes, feasting, and ministry at tables; and both this institution and its very name (agape—love) mean much among them (*esti poly*, ἔστι πολύ); and both the one and the other have drawn men most of all into godlessness" (into Christianity) [^587].

From the second half of the 4th century we encounter for the first time restrictive measures in relation to agapes and a struggle against them by pastors of the Church. The Council of Laodicea (c. 360 or earlier) forbids both "to offer the oblation (the Eucharist) in private houses" [^588], which was conjoined with the agape, and "in places belonging to the Lord (*kyriakois*, κυριακοῖς) or in churches to hold so-called agapes and to eat in the house of the Lord and spread couches (*akoubita strōnnyein*, ἀκούβιта στρωννύειν)" [^589]. St. John Chrysostom († 407) reckons, apparently, with an already fading habit of his hearers regarding agapes. Insisting that the celebration in honor of the martyrs should be preeminently spiritual, consisting of a purely prayerful assembly in church, he adds: "Yet dost thou desire to take part also in a bodily table? It is permitted, upon the conclusion of the assembly, here beside the shrine of the martyrs, having reclined beneath a fig tree or a vine, both to give rest unto the body and to deliver the conscience from condemnation. The martyr, seen nearby, standing close at hand, and presiding over the table itself, will not permit pleasure to turn into sin"

=== LEAF p130 ===
[^590]. Blessed Augustine relates concerning the Church of Milan in the episcopate of St. Ambrose († 397): "according to the custom of the African churches, my mother here also on the days of the commemoration of the saints brought offerings into the churches for the common meal from diverse meats; once, when she appeared with such offerings, she was stopped by the doorkeeper of the church, who told her that the bishop had forbidden such offerings" [^591]. And "everywhere," in Blessed Augustine's time, according to his testimony, "in the greater part of Italy and in all or most other churches, agapes either are not held at all, or, if instituted according to custom, were brought to an end and discontinued after the loving *(Orig. p. 133)* exhortation of the bishop" [^592]. Nevertheless in Africa the statutory prohibition of agapes took place more than half a century later than in Asia Minor. Only the Third Council of Carthage in 418 [419] decrees: "let bishops or presbyters feast not in church, unless during a journey out of necessity they have rest there; and unto the laity, as far as possible, let such banquets be forbidden" [^593]. In the West, however, the agape survived even longer. For the Council of Arles the agape was still something inseparable from the Eucharist or near unto it in sanctity: "Whoever by priestly authority is excommunicated from communion must not be invited unto the colloquy or banquet (*colloquio atque convivio*) [printed: *collquio atoqne convivio*] not only of clerics, but of the entire people" [^594]. The agape was first prohibited in the West by the Council of Auxerre in 584 [^595].

Of special importance was the abolition of the agape for the later liturgical Typikon—apart from the influence of its ritual upon the structure of Vespers—in the respect that it definitively established the custom of receiving the Eucharist before the taking of food. Already in the 3rd century this custom enjoyed universal dissemination; yet the opposite practice met with no censure either. The cause of this latter circumstance was that the agape, an evening service, was still frequently united with the Eucharist and maintained the view of it as physical no less than spiritual nourishment, not distinguishing the Eucharist sharply from food in general. Therefore we see that in the African churches, where the agape persisted longer than elsewhere, the custom of celebrating the Divine Liturgy in the morning before the midday meal took root later than in other places. According to Socrates (5th century), "the Egyptians neighboring upon Alexandria, and the inhabitants of the Thebaid, hold their religious assemblies on Saturday, but do not participate in the Holy Mysteries as is customary among Christians; but having dined and eaten all kinds of food, toward evening they partake of the Holy Gifts" [^596]. The tone of condemnation in which the historian speaks of this custom points unto a universal practice of another sort. Nevertheless this latter practice still required statutory measures for its confirmation and admitted of an exception: "The holy mysteries of the sacrifice," decrees the Third Council of Carthage (419), "must be celebrated by men in no other manner than upon condition that they have not eaten, with the exception of one day in the year, whereon"
