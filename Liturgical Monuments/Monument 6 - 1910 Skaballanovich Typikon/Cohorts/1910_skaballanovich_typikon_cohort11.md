#### Constituent Parts of Divine Worship

In order that the picture of the daily cycle of divine worship in the 3rd century may be complete, it is necessary to make several further general observations concerning the character of the services of that era and their internal content—that is, to examine more closely the common and indispensable constituent elements of every divine service: the reading of Holy Scripture, prayer, and singing in the 3rd century. Then, in addition to the structural composition of the services of that time, we shall fully comprehend their entire content.

#### The Reading of Holy Scripture

Frequent though the aforementioned testimonies of 3rd-century monuments concerning the reading of Holy Scripture at divine worship may be, they do not leave everything clear on this point. Above all, they do not specify the cycle of sacred books that were read. One must bear in mind that the New Testament canon of sacred books was not yet fully defined at that time. Not all the sacred books currently regarded as divinely inspired were recognized everywhere, in all Churches, as such even in the 4th century, as shown by the classification of certain of these books by St. Athanasius and Eusebius of Caesarea into the category of *antilegomena* (ἀντιλεγόμενα, disputed) or merely *anagignōskomena* (ἀναγιγνωσκόмеνα, readable—that is, permitted to be read at divine worship as edifying alongside the divinely inspired books). Such hesitation in the 4th century existed regarding the Epistle to the Hebrews, the majority of the Catholic Epistles, and the Apocalypse. Judging from the fact that into the category of such books "disputed" with regard to their canonicity in the 4th century were placed also certain venerable ancient writings subsequently decisively recognized as uninspired—such as the Epistle of Barnabas, the *Shepherd* of Hermas, and the like—one may think that in the 2nd and 3rd centuries the scope of Holy Scripture read at divine worship was broader than ours: that is, that alongside the present canonical books of the New Testament there were read and not sharply distinguished from them the writings of the Apostolic Fathers, and in certain Churches probably even apocrypha. A trace of this practice hath been preserved unto the present day in the Abyssinian Church, where as Holy Scripture at divine worship there is read, for example, the Book of Enoch [^469]. In our divine worship, a trace of this

=== LEAF p102 ===
practice is the reading of the non-canonical books of the Old Testament: the Wisdom of Solomon and the Wisdom of Jesus son of Sirach as paremias alongside and on a par with the canonical books, as well as, in part, the reading of the Acts and Apostolic Epistles as it were on a par with patristic readings at the All-Night Vigil before the Six Psalms.

#### Pericopes

For the first time from the 3rd century we possess testimonies regarding the liturgical division of the New Testament for reading. The *Arabic Didascalia*, as we have seen [^470], makes mention of pericopes (*zachala*). The testimony of this canonical monument, *(Orig. p. 105)* regarding whose attribution unto the 3rd century doubt is possible, is confirmed by writers of the 3rd century. Origen makes mention of pericopes in his commentary upon the Book of Kings: "that which hath been read is very extensive; and since it is necessary to speak of that which is divided into two pericopes, and there hath been read in order concerning Nabal..." [^471]. It is noticeable at times how the daily pericope compelleth Origen in a homily to dwell upon sections of Scripture little conducive unto edification, such as genealogies or the enumerations of the Hebrew encampments in the wilderness [^472]. All this likewise permitteth the conclusion that the division of Scripture for liturgical reading must have been continuous, without omissions. The poverty of the church year (in sacred commemorations) excluded the need for any departures from the continuous readings. Nevertheless, for existing commemorations, special readings must have been selected; this is evident from the fact that the same Origen in his commentary upon the Book of Job speaketh of reading it on fast days in honor of Christ's Passion: "in the church assembly the suffering of Job is read on holy days, on days of fasting, on days of abstinence, on days wherein those fasting and abstaining as it were suffer together with that marvelous Job, on days wherein by fasting and abstinence we follow the holy Passion of our Lord Jesus Christ... Not without reason (*merito*) even now on days of suffering, on days of sanctification, on days of fasting is the suffering of the blessed Job read; upon it do they meditate, and it do they unceasingly investigate" [^473].

#### The Reader

At the beginning of the 3rd century the reading of all Holy Scripture without distinction was assigned unto the reader, the view of whose office was of the highest order: it was accounted honorable even for a confessor. "So also was it meet," writeth St. Cyprian regarding the appointment as readers of the confessors Aurelius [printed: Avraamiy] and Celerinus, "for them to begin from the office of reader. Nothing is more becoming for a voice that hath confessed the Lord with a glorious proclamation than to resound in the performance of divine readings; after the lofty words which martyrdom for Christ hath uttered, to read the Gospel of Christ, through which martyrs are made" [^474]. "What else ought to have been done for one who hath come unto us with such a commendation (*dignatione*) from the Lord,

=== LEAF p103 ===
who was renowned for the admiration of the very one who persecuted him, than to elevate him unto the ecclesiastical ambo (*tribunam*), that, standing upon the height of the elevated place, visible unto the entire multitude by the glory of his station (*honoris*), he might read the precepts and the Gospel of the Lord, which this confessor steadfastly and faithfully followeth? Let that voice which hath confessed the Lord be heard daily in that which the Lord hath spoken. Let this confessor see whether there existeth a higher degree which he might attain in the Church. There is nothing wherein *(Orig. p. 106)* a confessor could be so profitable unto the brethren as that, when the Gospel reading is heard from his mouth, everyone who heareth may imitate the faith of the reader" [^475]. These passages clearly demonstrate that in Carthage in the middle of the 3rd century the reader read the Gospels as well (although already the 2nd century singled out the latter as a pre-eminently sacred book). But the *Testamentum* already entrusteth the reading of the Gospel unto a presbyter or deacon [^476].

#### Homilies

With regard to homiletics, the 3rd century is important in that now for the first time those homilies are committed unto writing which were delivered in church in explanation of the read sections of Holy Scripture (Origen, Hippolytus). Thereby was laid the foundation of that patristic literature which subsequently yielded such rich material for liturgical patristic readings. Yet from among the writers of the 3rd century our Typikon hath selected none for liturgical use.

#### Prayers

With regard to the prayers at the daily cycle of divine worship, the 3rd century likewise takes a significant step in comparison with the 2nd. There, formulas of Eucharistic prayers appear for the first time (in the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* / *Didache*); here, similar prayer formulas appear for the other principal daily services, at least for Matins, and at least themes for the prayer of the Hours.

St. Cyprian reproacheth a heretic in that, "having despised the bishops and forsaken the priests of God, he dareth to set up another altar, to offer another prayer with unauthorized words" [^477].

#### Melodies

Liturgical singing, as we have seen, received significant development in the 2nd century in comparison with the 1st, if one crediteth the tradition that St. Ignatius the God-Bearer was the initiator of antiphonal singing in the Christian Church. In the 3rd century it takes a no less significant step: the necessity is recognized of regulating the very melody (not the order alone) of Christian chant. Consequently, in the matter of chant the 3rd century penetrateth deeper than the 2nd. We have in mind the considerations expressed by Clement of Alexandria regarding music appropriate for Christians and their divine worship. Above all, Clement directeth attention unto

=== LEAF p104 ===
the distinction of Christian divine worship from Jewish and pagan worship in musical terms: "we for the glorification of God employ exclusively the peaceful word, and no longer employ either the ancient psaltery, or the trumpet, or the timbrel, or the flute" [^478]. Yet not all genres even of vocal singing, in Clement's view, may be accepted by Christians and *(Orig. p. 107)* were accepted by the Church of his time: "Music must be resorted to for the embellishment and education of morals. For even at a banquet (the agape?) we drink for mutual singing, chanting our cherished (hope) and glorifying God for the ungrudging gift of human enjoyments and for the constant provision of everything necessary for the growth of both body and soul. That music must be rejected which is superfluous (*perittē*, περιττὴ), which breaketh the soul, which runneth into variety, now mournful (*thrēnōdē*, θρηνώδη), now unrestrained and voluptuous (*akolaston kai hēdypathē*, ἀκόλαστον καὶ ἡδυпаθῆ), now frenzied and raving (*ekbakcheuomenēn kai manikēn*, ἐкβαкχευομένην καὶ μανικὴν)" [^479].

Dwelling upon the genres of vocal Greek music existing in his time—which had captivated the attention of the entire world of that day, had long since penetrated Eastern peoples, and must have everywhere displaced national melodies—Clement findeth several of these genres utterly unacceptable for a Christian and the Church: "We ought to choose melodies imbued with dispassion and chastity; melodies that enervate and relax the soul cannot harmonize with our manly and magnanimous frame of mind and disposition. The art expressed in vocal modulations through diverse flourishes is a false art; it fostereth a propensity toward an indolent and disordered life. By austere and sober melodies, however, shamelessness and drunkenness are averted in the very bud (presumably the agapai are meant). One must surrender chromatic harmonies (*chromatikas harmonias*, printed *χρωατικὰς ἁρμονίας*) unto shameless (*achrōmois*, ἀхрώμοις) carousals and the music of bouquet-bearing courtesans" [^480]. "The diverse venoms of enervating songs and mournful verses of the Carian muse are equally destructive unto morals, captivating them through intemperate and artless (*kakotechnō*, κακοτέχνῳ) music into the passion of such a revel" [^481]. Yet concerning certain genres of contemporary Greek music Clement speaketh approvingly, apparently finding in them something in common with the sacred music of the ancient Hebrews: "David may furnish a model for music, singing and prophesying, praising God melodiously (*emmelōs*, ἐммелῶς). For the Dorian harmony precisely there is most suitable such an enharmonic mode (*genos enharmonion*, γένος ἑναρμόνιον), and for the Phrygian—the diatonic (*diatonon*, διάтоνον), as Aristoxenus saith; and thus the harmony of the barbarian Hebrew psaltery, revealing within itself something venerable as the most ancient, becometh, especially in Terpander (the Greek musician), a model for the Dorian harmony, when he praiseth Zeus in the following manner: Zeus is the beginning of all, the leader of all; this beginning of hymns do I raise unto thee, Jupiter" [^482]. The next of the Greek melodies,

=== LEAF p105 ===
the Carian, Clement characterizeth as too plaintive and somber for Christian use; further concerning it [printed: *o nam*] nothing is known unto us. As regards the chromatic, enharmonic, and diatonic melodies, however, *(Orig. p. 108)* their very structure is known unto us. Their difference depended upon the magnitude of each of the three intervals in the tetrachord (a system of 4 strings or notes) which formed the foundation of Greek music. The total magnitude of the intervals in a tetrachord equaled 2 1/2 tones, and the simplest structure of a tetrachord was that in which it consisted of two whole intervals or tones and one half-tone; such a structure was called diatonic (through tone). A more artificial structure was that in which the tetrachord consisted of a tone and a half and two semitones; such a structure was called chromatic (colorful, varied). Still more artificial was the structure in which the tetrachord was composed of a double tone and two quarter tones: "Such a tetrachord was probably not used in vocal singing practice owing to the impossibility of precise execution by the voice and distinction by the ear of quarter tones, but was practiced in the realm of instrumental music" [^483]. This latter structure of the tetrachord was called enharmonic. But Clement, apparently, by an enharmonic melody understandeth some other (unison?), since he regardeth it as simpler than the diatonic and assigneth it unto Dorian melodies, which subsequent writers class with the diatonic genre of music on a par with the Phrygian.

All these remarks of Clement concerning music were evidently prompted by the lack of discrimination among Alexandrian Christians (naturally, highly educated in general and musically in particular) toward diverse genres of music. Clement was thus the first among the Teachers of the Church to arrive at the thought of regulating the liturgical singing of Christians with regard unto its very melody, and he declared himself decisively against the admission into the Church of the most artificial and passionate melodies. Subsequent organizers of church chant—in the West, Saints Ambrose and Gregory the Great, and in the East, St. John of Damascus—excluded the chromatic chant from their system of Tones, completely in accordance with Clement's indications, and adopted the Dorian and Phrygian chants of a diatonic character.

#### The Development of Hymnody

Writers of the 3rd century consider themselves entitled to boast before pagans of the abundance of hymns in their divine worship and to compete with them in this regard. Clement of Alexandria saith unto a pagan that upon the "God-beloved, cleanly shaded mountain" (in the Church) they "abandon themselves unto rejoicing (*bakcheuousi*, βακχεύουσι); yet not Maenads (priestesses)..., but daughters of God, fair lambs, celebrating (*theaspizousai*, θεασπίζουσαι) the venerable solemn mysteries (*orgia*, ὄργια) of the Word,

=== LEAF p106 ===
forming a sober-minded (*sōphrona*, σώφρονα) choir; the choir here is the righteous; here is singing, here is a hymn unto the King of all; maidens sing, Angels glorify, prophets speak" [^484]. Tertullian saith: "If dramatic art delighteth anyone, we have an abundance of Scrip-*(Orig. p. 109)*tures, an abundance of verses, an abundance of maxims, an abundance of songs (*canticorum*), an abundance of singing (*vocum*); and not fable, but truth; not strophae, but simplicity" [^485]. Origen, in response to Celsus' observation that Christians would honor God if they sang praises unto the Sun and Minerva, saith: "We utter (*legomen*, λέγομεν) hymns only unto the one God of all and unto His Only-Begotten Word God; we chant praises (*hymnoumen*, ὑмνοῦмен) unto God and unto His Only-Begotten, even as the sun, moon, stars, and all the heavenly host sing praises unto Him" [^486].

The great growth of liturgical singing literature encountered even a reaction among certain bishops. In the encyclical letter directed against Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch (who was carried away by the heresy of Artemon), in the second half of the 3rd century, the bishops denounce him, among other things, because "he abolished the psalms unto the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ, as being of recent origin (*neōterous*, νεωτέρους) and the compositions of modern men; whereas in the midst of the church, on the great day of Pascha, he commanded songs to be sung in honor of himself (*psalōdein eis heauton*, ψαλωδεῖν εἰς ἑαυтὸν), and for this purpose appointed women, whom hearing one could not but shudder" [^487].

#### Hymnographers and Hymns of the 3rd Century

Notwithstanding so rich a hymnographic activity in the 3rd century, scarcely any compositions of this genre, or even the names of their authors, have reached us from that era. This is explained by the destruction of Christian books by pagans and by the fact that Christians concealed their teaching—which was contained, among other things, in hymns (*hymnoi theologountes*, ὕμνοι θεολογοῦντες), especially the dogma of the Holy Trinity, brought forward principally by the 3rd century—not only from pagans, but also from catechumens (*disciplina arcani*), on which account Christians presumably feared to write down their hymns (as also the Creed) [^488]. Nevertheless, from the 3rd century there have survived not only several names of hymnographers, but also several of their works. Eusebius citeth from the work of Dionysius of Alexandria *On the Promises* a passage wherein this hierarch, reproving Nepos, bishop of the Egyptian Pentapolis († 246), for chiliastic views, praiseth him for "an abundance of spiritual songs, wherewith even unto now many of the brethren are delighted" [^489]. — Clement of Alexandria concludeth his *Paedagogus* with a hymn unto the Shepherd—that is, unto the Savior; it is possible that this hymn had a liturgical use, and in any case it *(Orig. p. 110)* can provide an idea of the character of the ecclesiastical poetry of that time. The hymn, beginning with the words: "Tamer of untamed colts," consisteth entirely of a long series (up to 30) of rapturous and tender titles (epithets) for Christ (for example: wing of nestlings, King, Word, Bestower of wisdom, Savior, husbandman, rudder, bridle, fisher of men), and a supplication unto Christ to gather His children for His praise and to lead them along His path. The conclusion of the hymn is accounted the most poetic: "O heavenly milk, poured forth from the glorious breasts of the graced Virgin—that is, of Thy Wisdom! We, Thy children, nourished with tender lips, filled with the gentle breath of the maternal bosom, sing all together simple songs and innocent hymns unto Christ the King, as a holy reward for the doctrine of life; we sing simply the mighty Child." The hymn consisteth of four strophes; it is written in anapests, spondees, and dactyls, with verses not everywhere regular and uniform. The hymn is consequently characteristic as the first attempt at applying Greek versification unto Christian song.

— The allegorical dialogue of Hieromartyr Methodius, bishop first of Patara (in Asia Minor) and subsequently of Tyre († 311), *The Banquet of the Ten Virgins* (presumably written in counterpoise to Plato's *Symposium*, demonstrating the superiority of virginity over marriage), concludeth with a "Hymn of the Virgins unto Christ God" in 24 strophes (in imitation, perchance, of the alphabetical psalms), with a refrain wherewith the choir responds unto the address of each virgin unto Christ. In the hymn, which begins: "From above, O virgins, a voice resoundeth...", the virgins summon one another, in view of the approaching voice that awakeneth the dead, to hasten forth unto the meeting of the approaching Bridegroom;

=== LEAF p108 ===
they express their self-forgetting devotion unto Christ, animating themselves by the example of "the Forerunner, who suffered for love of the truth," and of the Mother of the Bridegroom, "the graced, blameless, unapproachable, pure Virgin who was subjected to suspicion regarding her virginity"; the hymn concludeth with a prayer unto the Father with the Son to receive the virgins "into the portals of eternal life" [^490]. The hymns of Clement and St. Methodius resemble also the subsequent Akathist—the former by its multiplicity of epithets and acclamations, and the latter by its 24-strophe division. Yet in spirit and content both represent something inimitable for subsequent times: so much immediacy of religious feeling is in them, as yet untouched by theological reflection, such intimacy, so to speak, in communion with God, and such power of love for Christ, possible only in those centuries.

From among our present liturgical hymns, the most akin unto these hymns is the canticle **"Gladsome Light"** (*Svete tikhiy* / *Phōs Hilaron*). Judging from the fact that St. Basil the Great speaketh of it as "ancient" [^491], its composition cannot be placed later than the 3rd century. It is remarkable that all these monuments of liturgical poetry of the 3rd century possess an exclusively or predominantly (**"Gladsome Light"**) Christological *(Orig. p. 111)* content: of such a character, as we have seen, were the ecclesiastical hymns in the 2nd century as well [^492]. In this respect prayers differed from hymns: following the model of the Lord's Prayer, and in view of the constant mention by the Savior, in His precepts concerning prayer, of the Father (that prayer ought to be directed unto Him), the prayers that have survived from the 3rd century are for the most part addressed unto the Father. In the 4th century this even prompted a special decree of the Council of Hippo: "When they stand before the altar, prayer must always be directed unto the Father" [^493]. This custom and decree could have been prompted also by the endeavor to protect the dogma of the Trinity from a tritheistic interpretation and to emphasize in this dogma the concept of monarchy (*monarchia*).

From among our present liturgical hymns one may assign unto the 3rd century (judging from the fact that it is found in 4th-century codices of Holy Scripture) the **"Great Doxology"** (*Slavosloviye velikoye*). Just as the hymns of Clement, Methodius, and the hymn **"Gladsome Light"** are models of original 3rd-century Christian creativity, so this hymn is a model of skillful compilation from Holy Scripture. Its versification is Hebrew: parallelism of members. The first three hymns may serve as models of those songs which, according unto the testimony both of the Apostle Paul and later of Tertullian, were sung by individual persons at divine worship (being improvised or composed beforehand) by turn; the latter, perhaps, is a model of hymns for that congregational performance which, according unto the statement of the Neocaesareans unto St. Basil the Great, was introduced into their church by St. Gregory the Wonderworker († 270) [^494], and of which 4th-century writers make increasingly frequent mention. An echo of this distinction between the two genres of hymns (for individual and for congregational use) hath been preserved in our present

=== LEAF p109 ===
Typikon in its requirement that **"Gladsome Light"** be pronounced by the superior, whereas for the singing of the **"Great Doxology"** both choirs assemble together. By the special character of the first genre of hymns is explained, perhaps, the fact that not all of them entered into divine worship even from subsequent centuries (neither the poems of Gregory the Theologian nor the hymns of St. Ephrem the Syrian entered into liturgical worship, just as the hymns of Clement and Methodius did not).

#### The Church Year of the 3rd Century: Feasts and Weekdays

Regarding the church year in the 3rd century in comparison with the 2nd, one must note above all a greater distinction and setting apart of feast days from weekdays. Although the Alexandrian school of theology did not approve of such separation [^495], *(Orig. p. 112)* it is manifested, for example, in that on Sunday and during Pentecost work, fasting, and genuflections are now for the first time prohibited: "We," saith Tertullian, "as we have received by tradition, on the day of the Lord's Resurrection alone ought to refrain not only from kneeling, but from every manner of anxiety and toil, deferring our ordinary business lest we give place unto the devil" [^496]. "On the Lord's day we account it unlawful (*nefas dicimus*) to fast or to pray on bended knees" [^497]. "The non-bending of knees on the Lord's day," saith Pseudo-Justin, "is a symbol of the Resurrection, through which by the grace of Christ we have been delivered from sins and from death slain by Him; this custom received its beginning from apostolic times, as saith the blessed Irenaeus, martyr and bishop of Lyons, in his treatise on Pascha" [^498].

#### Sunday

Tertullian is thus the first to make mention of rest or freedom from labors as a mark of a Christian feast, and in particular of Sunday. Yet the Alexandrians—Clement and Origen—in agreement with writers of the 2nd century (especially Saints Justin and Irenaeus), speak only of rest from worldly pursuits and insist that a Christian feast ought not to resemble the Jewish Sabbath with its absolute idleness [^499]. Characteristic is the view of Origen regarding the commemoration associated with Sunday, expressed in the following passage of his: "Now, since there is much people (in church) on account of Friday, and especially on the Lord's day, which is a commemoration of Christ's Passion (for the Lord's Resurrection is celebrated once a year, and not always after seven days), let everyone pray unto God that His word may come unto us" [^500]. Such a view of Sunday is reflected also in our Typikon, when it appointeth for Sunday, besides the Sunday Canon, also the Canon of the Cross and Resurrection, and when it appointeth heirmoloi for the canon of general edifying content rather than dedicated unto the historical event, and the like.

#### Saturday

Although in this manner there continueth the polemic begun in the 2nd century against the Jewish Sabbath, and writers of the 3rd century like those of the 2nd all agree in the conviction regarding its complete non-obligatoriness for Christians [^501], nevertheless from the very beginning of the 3rd century, with the waning of antagonism toward Judaism, there ariseth a tendency toward some manner of honoring Saturday, distinguishing it from the series of ordi-*(Orig. p. 113)*nary days; and this tendency toward the end of the century and the beginning of the 4th century leadeth unto Saturday being honored in certain Churches almost equally with Sunday. Thus Tertullian, immediately preceding the aforementioned passage concerning the honoring of the Resurrection, saith: "In the matter of genuflection also prayer suffereth variety in view of those few who on Saturday abstain from kneeling. Since this exception is stubbornly defended in the Churches, may the Lord grant His grace that they may either give it up or follow their opinion without causing scandal unto others" [^502].

It is remarkable how those passages of the *Syriac Didascalia* where it speaketh of honoring the Lord's day are interpolated by the compiler of the *Apostolic Constitutions* in the sense of celebrating Saturday.

1. *The Didascalia:*

On the Lord's day, leaving all things aside, gather together in church. For whereby shall he be justified before God who goeth not to hear the saving word and to be nourished with divine food which abideth unto the ages?

2. *The Apostolic Constitutions:*

Assemble thither ("into the Lord's house") especially with greater diligence on the Sabbath day and on the day of the Lord's Resurrection, to send up praise unto God, Who created all things through Jesus and sent Him unto us, Who permitted Him to suffer and raised Him from the dead. For whereby shall he be justified before God who will not go to hear the saving word concerning the Resurrection on such a day, when we also perform three prayers standing in remembrance of Him Who arose on the third day, and when there take place readings of the prophets, the proclamation of the Gospel, the offering of the sacrifice, and the gift of sacred food [^503].
