=== LEAF p211 ===
having the same organization as Chariton's, thanks to the donations of Empress Eudokia and the attention which St. Euthymius devoted unto the labor of the monks, soon attained prosperity and at the same time became a higher school of ascetical endeavor for the Palestinian monasticism of that epoch: into it entered men from the Lower Coenobium and other monasteries of cenobitic structure, which had by that time gained significant expansion throughout the cities and wildernesses of Palestine. Unto his lavra St. Euthymius gave rules which touched upon diverse minutiae of daily life (for instance, on the observance of silence during divine worship and at meals), resembling in this the rules of St. Pachomius, yet apparently lacking penances. Deviations therefrom, according to tradition, were punished by God Himself through illnesses, from which St. Euthymius healed the afflicted. [^1116] In his views upon monasticism St. Euthymius must have adhered unto the Egyptian anchorites: it is known that in his life he strove to imitate St. Arsenius of Scetis, and with pleasure listened unto narratives concerning the Egyptian fathers from his own monks who had entered under him from the monasteries of those parts, such as Martyrius and Elias. [^1117] Consequently, in the arrangement of monastic life as well, St. Euthymius must have inclined toward the anchoritic type. Yet prior unto his death he foretold that his lavra would soon be converted into a cenobium, and gave unto his successor Elias instructions concerning the site and plan for this cenobium, as well as rules for it. Five years after the death of the venerable father, Patriarch Martyrius entrusted unto the deacon Fidus (unto whom the apparition of Euthymius repeated his instruction) the reconstruction of the lavra into a cenobium: the cells were demolished; upon the site of the burial of St. Euthymius was erected the building of the cenobium with a wall and tower; the former church was converted into a trapeza (refectory), and a new temple was built. [^1118] This narrative, so clearly distinguishing the concepts of lavra and cenobium, must also point in general unto a temporary decline of lavriote-celliotic life in Palestine after St. Euthymius and its suppression by cenobitic life. The former demanded leaders of powerful spirit, who were not to be found after the death of St. Euthymius. The emergence of such a figure as St. Sabbas the Sanctified (whose activity belongs rather unto the VI century and inaugurated a distinct epoch in the development of the liturgical Typikon) was required for this type of monastery to revive once again. With its conversion into a cenobium, the Lavra of St. Euthymius lost its significance and gradually became deserted. [^1119]

### St. Gerasimus

Palestine of this period (V c.) possessed, apparently, purely anchoritic monasteries as well. Such must have been the monastery of St. Gerasimus († 475) on the Jordan, a native of Asia Minor who had received his ascetical training in the Thebaid desert. Nourishing themselves upon bread and water and occupying themselves with basket weaving, the monks of Gerasimus's monastery remained in reclusion within their cells six days a week, assembling only on Saturday and Sunday for the Liturgy. St. Gerasimus himself the Forty Days Fast

spent entirely without food. He was renowned for his power over wild beasts ("whom the lion served"). [^1120]

### Origins and Characteristics of the Palestinian Typikon

As we see, the lives of all these founders of Palestinian monasticism speak very little concerning their purely liturgical activity. Nevertheless, it is beyond doubt that, having created a distinct type of monastic life, they could not fail to impress a distinctive mark upon the "prayer rule" of their monks. Tradition, although comparatively late, regards precisely the enumerated Palestinian abbas as the forebears of the Jerusalem Typikon. The most ancient among the manuscripts of this Typikon surviving unto the present day (XIII c.) admonish the hegumen:

> "unswervingly and immutably to observe the ecclesiastical sequence, order, and constitution (*καταστάσεως*), even as it hath been handed down unto us and enacted by the holy fathers who were before us in their holy monasteries and lavras, that is to say, by Euthymius the Great, Sabbas the Sanctified († 532), Theodosius the Coenobiarch († 529), Gerasimus of the Jordan, whom the wild beast served, Chariton the Confessor, and Cyriacus the Anchorite († 556); which Typikon Sophronius, great among the patriarchs († ca. 640), committed unto writing for subsequent generations. And when the barbarians burned the Lavra of the great Sabbas, and the written record made by blessed Sophronius fell victim unto the flames, all-wise John Damascene, like unto an industrious bee, once again delivered it unto his successors, and it is preserved even unto this day." [^1121]

The same thought is expressed by St. Symeon of Thessalonica (XV c.):

> "our divine father Sabbas set forth the Typikon, having received it from SS. Euthymius and Theoctistus, and they received it from those who were before them and from Chariton the Confessor. And when this Typikon (*διατύπωσις*), as is well known, was destroyed upon the devastation of that land by the barbarians, then our father among the saints Sophronius, Patriarch of the Holy City, laboriously set it forth, and after him our divine father John Damascene, experienced in theology, renewed and transcribed it." [^1122]

One may, of course, doubt all the particularities of this genealogy, especially if it intends to assert that the Jerusalem Typikon in the entire compass which it possessed in the XIII–XV centuries, and which is very close unto

=== LEAF p213 ===
its present composition, proceeds from *(Orig. p. 225)* SS. Chariton, Euthymius, Theoctistus, and Gerasimus; yet at the foundation of this tradition, in order for it to arise at all, an historical kernel must have lain. The liturgical orders of the enumerated Palestinian lavras must have differed noticeably from the practice of other monasteries, corresponding unto their entire distinctive constitution. And St. John Cassian, who observed the life of Palestinian monasteries in the present epoch, as well as several other accounts concerning them from the same era, permit us not only to determine the general character of their divine worship at that time, but also to perceive what elements in it were held in common with the later and present Jerusalem Typikon. Having forewarned that in general the rules of Egyptian monasteries are impracticable in the West (in Gaul) "on account of the inclemency of the climate or on account of the difficulty and diversity of customs," and that he would replace their rules in his instructions on cenobitic life with "the rules of Palestinian or Mesopotamian monasteries," [^1123] St. Cassian observes with regard unto Palestinian divine worship as well that it tempers the "perfection and inimitable rigor (*rigorem*) of the Egyptian rules." [^1124] The most significant distinction of Palestinian monastic worship from that of Egypt, according to Cassian, was that in Palestine, as in Mesopotamia and throughout the entire East, the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours were celebrated at their appointed times, whereas in Egypt they were not celebrated at all, being replaced by unceasing prayer during handiwork in order not to detach the brethren therefrom; yet so as not to impede necessary labors, these "prayer offices" in Palestine "are celebrated in due moderation," and precisely with this in view "for each hour (only) three psalms are appointed." [^1125] This report of Cassian does not stand alone. "The abba of a certain monastery situated in Palestine reported unto blessed Epiphanius, Bishop of Cyprus († 403): by thy prayers we do not abandon our rule, but diligently perform it at the third, sixth, and ninth hour." [^1126] The inalienable obligation of the office of the Hours for all days of the year subsequently remained an enduring peculiarity of the Jerusalem Typikon (the Stoudite suppresses the Hours on feast days). On the part of the Palestinian monasteries there was both a strict fidelity unto ancient customs [^1127] and an unwillingness to depart far from the practice of secular churches. [^1128] Unto Palestine of that epoch the liturgical Hours owe also an enrichment of their structure—the emergence of the distinct office of the First Hour. [^1129]

### The Spoudaioi

In general, the unwillingness to sever entirely the bonds with the customary forms of divine worship consecrated by antiquity and preserved in secular *(Orig. p. 226)* churches may be recognized as a characteristic feature of Palestinian divine worship at that time. We see from the *Pilgrimage* of Silvia what an active part "monastics and virgins" took in the services of the Church of the Holy Resurrection (Anastasis): together with

=== LEAF p214 ===
the presbyters they began the service prior unto the arrival of the bishop, and sometimes continued it after the bishop's departure, together with the most zealous among the laity. [^1130] It is possible that by virtue of this the monks of Jerusalem or of its environs (for the majority of the lavras enumerated above were situated near Jerusalem), or an offshoot thereof, received the name of the Spoudaioi (*οἱ σπουδαῖοι*, "the Zealous Ones"). Concerning the existence of monks bearing this designation in the Jerusalem of that era, there is testimony in the *Life of St. Sabbas the Sanctified* composed by Cyril of Scythopolis (VI c.): "Patriarch Elias (494–517) built a monastery near the episcopal residence and gathered into it the Spoudaioi of the Holy Resurrection, who hitherto had been dispersed across diverse places near the Tower of David, giving unto each of them a cell with every material provision." At this new monastery a church was arranged in the name of the Mother of God, named *ἡ Θεοτόκος τῶν σπουδαίων* (the Theotokos of the Spoudaioi). [^1131] Modeled upon the Jerusalem Spoudaioi, perhaps already in the V century, a monastery of the Spoudaioi was founded in Constantinople as well. [^1132] It is striking that according unto the Holy Sepulchre Typikon in a manuscript of 1122, portraying the practice of the IX century, the role of the Spoudaioi during divine worship in the Church of the Resurrection is entirely identical unto that in the *Pilgrimage* of Silvia: they celebrate the office in the "Resurrection" prior unto the arrival of the patriarch, after which they withdraw into their monastery to finish the service, while on Sunday the clerics of the Holy Sepulchre chant. [^1133] These monks could serve as mediators and unifiers between the purely monastic and the cathedral secular liturgical practice, and it is possible that their influence upon the shaping of the Jerusalem Typikon was exceedingly significant. [^1134]

Proximity unto secular liturgical practice did not, however, exert an adverse effect upon the prayerful zeal of Palestinian monasticism (as the very name of the *Spoudaioi* implies), nor did their greater contact with the world, in comparison with Egyptian monasticism, lower the level of their ascetical endeavor. The finest representatives of this monasticism astonish by their feats of prayer no less than those in Egypt. Thus St. Adolius, a monk of a certain Jerusalem monastery, "from eventide until the morning hour when the brethren gathered once again in the chapels (*εὐκτηρίοις*), stood upon the Mount of Olives, upon the hill of the Ascension whence Jesus ascended, chanting psalms and praying. And this he did at all times: whether snow fell, or rain, or hail, he remained motionless. Having fulfilled the customary prayer rule (*κανόна*), with a waking hammer (*τῷ ἐξυπνιασ*(Orig. p. 227)*τικῷ σφυρίῳ*) he knocked upon all the cells, summoning the brethren into the chapels for the morning doxology (*δοξολογίαν*), chanting at (*καθ᾽*) each cell one or two (*δεύτερον*) antiphons with them, and having prayed in this manner with them, withdrew before dawn into his cell." [^1135]

### Syrian-Mesopotamian Monasticism

Nor did the Syrian-Mesopotamian ascetical movement pass without influence upon our liturgical Typikon, which in character of course differs as greatly

=== LEAF p215 ===
from Egyptian and Palestinian as these three lands differ in every respect, and whose spiritual experiences were utilized by the principal "Typikon-framers" of Byzantium and the West—St. Basil the Great and St. John Cassian. Undoubtedly, only an inadequate acquaintance with this monasticism on the part of St. Cassian, who had not visited Mesopotamia, permitted him to regard Mesopotamian asceticism alongside Palestinian as "more moderate" than Egyptian. [^1136] Yet precisely "the characteristic feature of the former is an austere mode of life; in this respect Syrian monasticism leaves behind it original Egyptian monasticism; in this measure of it was manifested the distinctive spirit of the Syrian with his ardent, fiery, impetuous character." [^1137] It invented the most arduous and bizarre forms of ascetical feats. Here ascetes lived in chambers smaller than the human stature, wherein one had ever to maintain a bent posture of body, [^1138] or on the contrary entirely beneath the open sky in summer and winter, [^1139] in huts without windows and doors, [^1140] in suspended and swaying circular *(Orig. p. 228)* cages; [^1141] Syria precisely invented also the ascetical feat of stylitism (St. Simeon Stylites, † 459). It is not surprising that in the matter of fasting as well Syrian monasticism surpassed other lands. Thus here existed a class of ascetes termed *boskoi* (*βοσκοὶ*, shepherds, or more precisely "grazers"), because "they made no use of bread, cooked food, or wine, and when the hour arrived to partake of food, each of them, taking a sickle, went forth to wander upon the mountain like unto a grazing beast, and nourished himself upon plants." [^1142] Nowhere did men attain unto such a degree of contrition (*compunctio*, *katanyxis*) as here. Concerning St. Domnina, Blessed Theodoret recounts: "often, having taken my right hand and applied it unto her eyes, Domnina released it so wet that it seemed as though the hand itself poured forth tears." [^1143] St. Ephrem the Syrian recounts concerning his fellow ascete Julian: "Throughout the entire service he stood, not daring to lift his eyes, as though before the very judgment seat of our Lord Jesus Christ. One day I said unto him: 'Who spoileth the books here? For where there was written "God," or "Lord," or "Jesus Christ," or "Savior," there the outlines of the letters have been effaced.' The blessed man replied unto me: 'I will hide nothing from thee... as I read, as soon as I discover the written name of my God, I bedew it with my tears.'" [^1144] St. Ephrem the Syrian himself prayed that God would temper contrition within him ("relieve me the waves of Thy grace," *«ослаби ми волны благодати Твоея»*). [^1145]

### Syrian-Mesopotamian Monastic Divine Worship

It is understandable that through all this was created the most fertile soil for the development of divine worship as well. In the majority of monasteries and monastic communities, which Syria and Mesopotamia in the IV–V centuries counted in vast numbers, divine worship, apparently, did not differ from that customary in secular churches. Even concerning the *boskoi*, Sozomen observes that they, "dwelling in the mountains, ever give glory unto God with prayers and hymns according to the ordinance of the Church (*κατὰ θεσμὸν τῆς ἐκκλησίας*)." [^1146] St. Publius, having founded a monastery near the city of

=== LEAF p216 ===
Zeugma on the Euphrates, erected therein a church wherein he gathered the monks "at the beginning and at the close of the day to offer unto God the morning and evening doxology"; and because there were Syrians and Greeks in the monastery, they, "dividing into two sides within the church, performed the psalmody *(Orig. p. 229)* alternately, now in the one and now in the other tongue." [^1147] For the midnight service (Matins) the superior himself roused the brethren in certain monasteries. [^1148] Communities possessing no temple went daily in the morning and at eventide unto neighboring churches, as, for example, the female companions of St. Domnina. [^1149] Yet, of course, such divine worship was insufficient for the Syrian ascetes, and they supplemented it with their own, which consisted of an alternation of psalmody and prayer; it doth not appear that this alternation consisted, as in Egypt, in accompanying each psalm with a prayer; rather, after a certain quantity of psalms followed a lengthy prayer or a series thereof: concerning St. Marcian it is recounted that he "replaced prayer with psalmody, psalmody with prayer, and both the one and the other with reading of the Word of God"; [^1150] the same is said concerning St. Publius; [^1151] St. Abraham "performed at night by turns forty psalmodies, filling the intervals between them with twice as great a number of prayers." [^1152] St. Julian Saba in his monastery near Edessa "commanded the brethren to offer unto God common psalmody in the cell, and after dawn to withdraw two by two into the desert, and there, the one kneeling, to offer unto God due adoration, while the other, standing, chanted at that time fifteen Psalms of David; then changing positions: the second standing to chant, while the other, prostrate upon the earth, prayed"; [^1153] and this they did continually from morning until twilight; before sunset, having rested a little, coming from here and from there, from all directions they assembled in the cells and together chanted the evening hymn unto the Lord. [^1154] These reports demonstrate also that in the prayer rule, as among the Egyptian ascetes, a certain importance was attached unto the number of psalms, and that the number taken in this case was sacred (forty) or round (fifteen = one tenth of the Psalter); yet the twelve-psalm office so widely adopted in Egypt is not mentioned here. Apparently, more significance than in Egypt was attached unto bows and prostrations: in the cited report the bow is intensified unto prolonged prostration upon the earth. In other instances this intensification found expression in a great quantity of short metanoias: St. Simeon Stylites "performed prayer now motionlessly, now making bows; many of the visitors sought to count their number; once someone counted 1,244 of them, but then grew weary and ceased counting; when the elder made bows, his forehead approached unto the very tips of his toes; partaking of food once a week and even then in small measure, his hollow belly afforded his back the ability to bend easily." [^1155] No less than in Egypt were ecclesiastical feasts and fasts venerated. The anchorites dwelling near Carrhae (= Harran) gathered into that city on Pascha and on the feast day *(Orig. p. 230)* of the holy martyr Elpidius for divine worship. [^1156] SS. Marana and Cyra received female visitors only on the days of Pentecost. [^1157] St. Simeon Stylites "on the days of church feasts from sunset until

=== LEAF p217 ===
its very rising stood the whole night through with hands uplifted unto heaven, forgetful of sleep and weariness." [^1158] He likewise passed the entirety of the Forty Days Fast without food, arriving at times by reason of this at a state of syncope. [^1159]

### The Typikon of St. Basil (Asia Minor Monasteries)

The monastic Typikon of St. Basil the Great, if it be comprised by his 55 "Rules Expounded at Length in Questions and Answers" and 313 "Rules Expounded Briefly in Questions and Answers," and his other ascetical writings (*Discourse on Ascetical Discipline*, three *Ascetical Discourses*, *Ascetical Constitutions for Those Laboring in Cenobia and Monasticism*, etc.), represents in itself not a codification of diverse minutiae of monastic life like unto the *Rules* of St. Pachomius, but a general theory of ascetical endeavor; for this reason it could not exert a detailed influence upon our Typikon. What was important in this Typikon was that it disregarded those minutiae and particularities of monastic governance unto which the *Rules* of St. Pachomius of the later recension attached so much weight. Perhaps by virtue of this trait the Typikon of St. Basil proved to correspond unto the spirit of Palestinian monasticism more than that of Pachomius: under Bishop John of Jerusalem (386–417) the Typikon of St. Basil was introduced into the Carmel monastery; [^1160] St. Theodosius the Coenobiarch († 529) "frequently offered unto the brethren instructions from the counsels of the great Basil, unsurpassed in ascetical feats and invincible in doctrines of truth," and especially loved to read unto the brethren the introduction unto the *Longer Rules*. [^1161] Yet the monastic Typikon of St. Basil provided by his designated writings is of too general a character to impart a definitive organization unto a monastery. In all likelihood, under the name of the Typikon of St. Basil was understood not only his ascetical writings, but also the organization which he imparted unto his own monasteries. When St. Basil resolved to begin an ascetical life, he "desired to find a brother who had chosen this manner of life" [^1162] to serve as a guide for himself. In the homeland of St. Basil in Cappadocia there were already not a few anchorites, but they were in constant communication with the world and lacked close communion among themselves (like the Egyptian anchorites); for this reason they did not satisfy the ideal of St. Basil, and he undertook a journey into Mesopotamia, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt in order to acquaint himself with genuine ascetical endeavor. Here he arrived at the conclusion that cenobitic *(Orig. p. 231)* monasticism leads more surely unto the goal than solitary eremitism—a thought which is unfolded throughout all his ascetical works. This thought he resolved to realize in practice as well. At first he founded a monastery upon the bank of the Iris River (in Pontus), near the city of Ibora, where a convent of his mother and sister already existed. Subsequently, "traversing the Pontic cities (for the contest against Arianism), he founded there many monastic communities." [^1163] Unto the first monastery he invited his friend St. Gregory the Theologian. In the epistles addressed unto him on

=== LEAF p218 ===
this occasion he set forth his ideal of monastic life, developed by him only in greater detail later in the *Rules*. The day of the monk must begin with prayer:

> "the honoring of the Savior with songs and hymnody; then, when the sun hath fully shone forth, setting about works and having prayer everywhere present with oneself, it is needful to season one's labors with psalmody, as with salt." [^1164] "Prayer hath two forms: the first is doxology with humility of mind, and the second and lower is petition; therefore when praying do not immediately proceed unto petition, [^1165] lest otherwise thou show that thou prayest solely out of necessity." [^1166] Alongside prayer must go the readings and study of the divinely inspired Scriptures: "if prayers follow upon readings, then the soul, moved by love toward God, approacheth them more maturely and vigorously." [^1167]

Although, as observed, prayer according unto St. Basil must be unceasing, ever accompanying the labor of the monk as well, nevertheless certain hours of the day and night are worthy of distinct prayerful veneration; as such hours St. Basil designates: morning, the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours, the conclusion of the day (Vespers), the onset of night (Compline), and midnight, indicating wherein each of these hours is significant. [^1168] An especially propitious time for prayer Basil the Great considers midnight: "that for which the morning is employed by others serveth the ascetes of piety at midnight, because the nocturnal silence more than all else bestoweth freedom upon the soul, when neither eyes nor ears convey harmful sights or rumors unto the heart." [^1169] It is unknown what "prayer" at the designated hours Basil the Great hath in view: private or public. Communion Basil the Great counseled daily for all men, and even more so for monastics, with the Reserved Gifts. [^1170]

No small significance doth Basil the Great attach unto the outward appearance of the monk:

> "Unto a humble and contrite manner of thinking there correspond a sorrowful gaze cast down unto the ground and a disregard for outward appearance." [^1171] It is not advised to seat oneself beside an elder or superior, but rather lower down, nor to cross one leg over another when sitting. [^1172] "It is necessary that the tunic be gathered upon the body with a girdle; and the girdle must not lie higher than the loins, for this would be effeminate, nor be drawn so loosely that the tunic might flutter, for this *(Orig. p. 232)* would be frivolous. The gait must be neither slow, which would betray a spiritual slackness, nor yet swift and hurried, which would disclose frenzied agitations of the soul." [^1173] The necessity is demonstrated

=== LEAF p219 ===
of using a girdle. [^1174] "In food, bread will satisfy necessity, water will satisfy thirst in a healthy man, and cooked preparations of seeds or legumes can sustain strength in the body for necessary requirements." [^1175] "The ascete must by no means strive after variety in foods, nor even under the pretext of abstinence demand an alteration of the offered provisions... On the contrary, even if for eating with bread salted fish be offered, which the holy fathers judged right to add unto foods in small quantity in lieu of any other condiment, mixing it into food consisting exclusively of water or greens, the ascete must not, under the pretext of a vainglorious, self-willed piety, refusing this as though it were flesh-meat, demand other provisions (though non-fish, yet more costly and choice); but without special attention unto this, dipping a morsel into the broth made from a small piece of salted fish, let him partake with all thanksgiving; for this small quantity of fish, cast into such a volume of water, or if it so happen, into a broth of seeds or legumes, will not constitute non-fasting food, but genuine and the most strict fasting food of an ascete." [^1176] "Nor ought one to abhor wine, if it be taken for healing, yet neither ought one to seek after it without need." [^1177] Prayers are prescribed prior unto partaking of food and after it, wherein the second "let it contain within itself both thanksgiving for that which hath been bestowed and petition for that which hath been promised." [^1178] "For the taking of food there must be appointed one definite hour, and that the same one throughout the course of a given period, [^1179] so that of the twenty-four hours in the day only that single one be expended for the body, while all the remaining hours the ascete would spend in noetic labor (*умном делании*). Sleep must be light, from which one can awake without difficulty and such as naturally followeth upon a small intake of food; it should intentionally be interrupted by concerns regarding weightier matters." [^1180]

Basil the Great likewise imparteth instructions concerning the governance of a monastery, concerning the oversight over the brethren on the part of the superior, and concerning the reception of postulants into the monastery; all these instructions are likewise of a general character and do not enter into such details as the Rule of St. Pachomius, touching only occasionally upon specific matters; for instance, it is advised "in the event of perplexities in the brotherhood to choose one brother, that he might either propose the doubts of certain ones unto the brotherhood for common deliberation, or bring them unto the knowledge of the superior." [^1181]
