In completely identical fashion the rite of forgiveness following the meal is performed unto this day on Mount Athos.

The titles placed above the Order of the Panagia and distinct prayers of the rite in certain manuscripts demonstrate that it was performed outside monasteries as well, on the following occasions: upon someone’s entrance into service or office, or upon embarking on a journey, particularly by water.[^2829] This rite was likewise performed at the Byzantine court after dinner. According to the description of Pseudo-Kodinos (14th century), *“after the dishes are cleared from the table, the domestikos brings the bread upon the panagiarion; the emperor arises; the first of those present at table, having received the panagiarion, sets it upon the table, and having elevated the bread, hands it unto the domestikos, and the latter unto the emperor; as soon as the emperor brings the bread unto his lips, all loudly chant unto him: **‘Many years’**.”*[^2830]

*(Orig. p. 789)*

Casting a single glance over the entire development of the order of the monastic meal, one cannot fail to note in its history, as the most characteristic phenomena, certain particularities. Even prior to Christianity, the necessity of prayerful blessing of the meal was recognized. The most important of the refectory prayers in its present form was already known to the 4th century. The agape, and following it monasticism, imparted unto the meal a genuine liturgical character. The origin of the Order of the Panagia itself, which currently constitutes the focal point of the refectory meal, is hidden in the depths of history. Yet the circumstance that it is unknown to the Studite Typikon widely diffused throughout the Greek East—possessing here in its stead the elevation of the bread fragments (*ukrukhi*)—this circumstance compels one to presume that this order appeared

=== LEAF p953 ===

for the first time in the Jerusalem Typikon and could have originated within the bosom of the Jerusalem Church at a very early date. As soon as the Mother of God received in the Church all Her glory and honor, there must have arisen the idea of a certain eucharist in Her name as well. In order to distinguish this eucharist more sharply from the true one, the elevation is entrusted unto a deacon and a simple monk (among the Greeks even unto a reader). Remarkable is the similarity of the order of the meal in the Studite and Jerusalem Typika, reaching at times literal identity in everything except this detail (the elevation of the bread fragments instead of the panagia), which is, to be sure, the most important. It is very characteristic that the Studite Typikon commemorates the Mother of God only at the evening meal. It is remarkable that the Studite Typikon lacks the Lord’s Prayer at the beginning of the meal, *(Orig. p. 790)* replacing it with another prayer, which naturally yielded its place later unto the greatest of prayers, which so fittingly suits the meal by its fourth petition.

Hymns in honor of the Mother of God gradually increase in number. Later typika have from ancient times attempted to omit the concluding psalms, the result of which in the present Typikon is the disappearance of Psalm 83 [84]. In compensation for this, these typika exhibit a tendency toward multiplying the number of brief prayer formulas. Later typika restrict the participation of the brethren in favor of the superior and generally complicate the rite excessively (the Greeks and the Old Believers).

The venerable antiquity of the rite, demonstrating how continuously it was considered an indispensable component of every piously festive meal, might plead—alongside its indisputable compunction—for its greater practical application at present and for the desirability of its dissemination beyond monastery walls. Yet even within the latter, it appears, it is not everywhere observed.

*(Orig. p. 791)*

### On a Saint Having a Vigil on Sunday

#### Content of the Chapter

Nothing speaks so powerfully unto the heart of a Christian as the Resurrection of Christ, and nothing more deeply causes our soul to tremble with awe than the celebration of this event, however frequently it occur.[^2831] Yet at times even this seemingly impossible thing is possible—an augmentation of the joy of the Sunday feast. This occurs when with Sunday there coincides a commemoration worthy in itself of an equally or nearly equally solemn celebration as the Resurrection. Having set forth in Chapters 1 and 2 the order of the customary Sunday service, the Typikon naturally occupies itself in Chapter 3 with such exceptional Sundays. It passes over in this place cases of the concurrence with Sunday of the Twelve Great Feasts—which augment Sunday joy still further—on account of the comparative rarity of such occurrences (not even every year) and because the most important among them entirely displace the Sunday service. Reserving the regulation of these latter cases for the Menologion section, the Typikon in this present section treats solely of concurrences with Sunday of the commemorations of great saints, and begins—in Chapter 3—with the greatest saints for whom a Vigil is appointed. Such saints are very few. Unconditionally, without the reservation *“if the superior so desire”*, a Vigil is appointed only for the following saints: John the Forerunner (June 24 and August 29), the Apostles Peter and Paul (June 29), John the Theologian (September 26 and May 8), and the Three Hierarchs (January 30). Yet in addition to these, a Vigil is permitted by the Typikon for a whole series of other saints. It is admissible under special circumstances for any saint (on royal days, or for locally venerated saints). On the other hand, even for the aforementioned greatest saints, *“for the sake of great necessity”*, a Vigil may be omitted, just as it may be omitted even on a Twelve Great Feast (excluding the Nativity of Christ and Theophany). The order provided in Chapter 3 of the Typikon holds force for any saint, on whatever occasion a Vigil be appointed for him, if his commemoration fall on a Sunday. It is not entirely applicable only unto the Vigil of the patron saint of a temple, who possesses, however, in comparison with every other great saint, merely a single prerogative when combined with a Sunday Vigil (his own Matins Gospel and the sticheron following it).

In combining such Vigils—of the Sunday and of the saint—the rule endeavors, first of all, without expanding the customary volume of the Vigil, to grant unto each of the two offices an approximately equal place,

=== LEAF p956 ===

achieving this by placing the Res*(Orig. p. 793)*urrectional hymns before the hymns of the saint in each group, yet assigning the former in smaller quantity.

At Small Vespers, the stichera at **“Lord, I have cried”** are exclusively Resurrectional, since their small number (4) would be inconvenient to divide between two commemorations; only the *Doxastikon* (*Slavnik*) belongs unto the saint; the Theotokion of the Tone on account of its importance (the Lesser Dogmatikon); on the other hand, the Aposticha are assigned almost entirely unto the saint: following the Resurrectional sticheron, there are chanted here the Aposticha of Great Vespers, which cannot be chanted in their own proper place because of the Resurrectional stichera; the *Doxastikon* of the saint is already taken from Small Vespers (since the Aposticha *Doxastikon* of Great Vespers will be in its proper place), and the Theotokion (in contrast to **“Lord, I have cried”**) is according to the Tone of the *Doxastikon*, that is, according to the direction of the Menaion. Following **“Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart”**, the Resurrectional troparion, *Glory*, of the saint, *Both now*, Theotokion according to the Tone of the troparion.

At Great Vespers, **“Blessed is the man”** is chanted as the entire kathisma, the first section of which has application also unto the commemoration of the saint. At **“Lord, I have cried”**, the stichera from the Octoechos are Resurrectional 3, Anatolian 1, and of the saint 6; *Glory*, of the saint; *Both now*, Dogmatikon of the Tone. Old Testament readings (*Paroimiai*) of the saint. At the Litiya, according to custom, the sticheron of the temple and the Litiya stichera of the saint; *Glory*, of the saint; *Both now*, Resurrectional Theotokion according to the Tone of the *Doxastikon*. At the Aposticha, Resurrectional stichera; *Glory*, of the saint; *Both now*, Theotokion according to the Tone of the *Glory*. Following **“Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart”**, **“O Theotokos Virgin, rejoice”** twice, and the troparion of the saint once. The reading is not specified, since it is taken for granted that it is the “great reading” from the Acts and Epistles.

At Matins, at **“God is the Lord”**, the troparion of the saint occupies the same position as for a minor saint, in view of the impossibility of a more solemn chanting thereof owing to lack of space. Following the kathismata, the sessional hymns are exclusively Resurrectional with their Theotokia, for homogeneity of chant and because the sessional hymns of the saint are more appropriate at the Polyeleos, which is almost entirely dedicated unto his memory. In view of this, the readings following the kathismata also pertain unto Sunday—*“in the Gospel”*, that is, from the commentaries upon the Gospel by St. John Chrysostom or Theophylact. The Polyeleos is appointed in any case (even in that part of the year when for an ordinary Sunday it is omitted). Under the Polyeleos is understood also the Megalynarion (*Velichaniye*)[^2832] with the selected psalm, but without the refrain *“Alleluia (3), glory to Thee, O God”*, which occurs after the **“Assembly of the Angels”** [*Angelsky sobor* / *Evlogetaria*]. Following the Megalynarion follows the **“Assembly of the Angels”**, which constitutes, as it were, the Resurrectional Megalynarion. Following the Little Ektenia comes the Hypakoe. Since the Hypakoe represents a variety of sessional hymn, unto it are joined all the sessional hymns of the saint, including those from the kathismata, but naturally only once each (consequently, if at each kathisma there be two sessional hymns, only the first of each). The “reading of the saint”, since everything here is dedicated unto him. The Hymns of Ascent (*Anabathmoi* / *Stepenny*) of the Tone, applicable according to

### THE ORDER OF THE BLESSING OF KOLIVA (*CHIN BLAGOSLOVENIYA KOLIVA*)

Just as unto Chapters 1 and 2 of the Typikon there is subjoined the order of the refectory meal, so unto this chapter is joined the Order of the Blessing of Koliva. By this it is made understood that the partaking of koliva is an attribute and distinction of the commemoration of a saint with a Vigil, just as the refectory meal is for every day. This is the meal on the day of the commemoration of a saint. What koliva is,[^2834] is explained in the very title of the present rite: it is *kutiya*, or boiled wheat with honey, adorned with sweet fruits and brought into the church in honor and memory of *“Dominical (that is, Twelve Great) feasts or the saints of God”*—great saints, as is evident from the placement of this rite precisely here. As the content of the prayer over the koliva demonstrates, it possesses, on the one hand, the same symbolic significance as that offered in remembrance of the departed, that is, it prefigures the resurrection, likened in Holy Scripture unto the germination of seed from the earth; on the other hand, the koliva has as its purpose, like the bread and wine blessed at Vespers, the sanctification of the festive *(Orig. p. 796)* meal, which serves as an image of the enjoyment of eternal blessings. This rite is performed either at Vespers following **“Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart”**, the Trisagion, and the troparion with its Theotokion—consequently, at Vespers that is not combined with Matins into a Vigil, as is evident also from the rubric concerning the conclusion of this Vespers (**“Wisdom”**, **“Bless”**, **“He Who is blessed”**)—or at the Liturgy after the prayer behind the ambo.

The rite, which is provided in full in the *Sluzhebnik* (Hieratikon), is reduced unto a consecratory prayer over the koliva, which is preceded by the glorification of the saint through a troparion—at Vespers customarily appointed at its end, that is, with the (Resurrectional) Theotokion; at the Liturgy through a troparion expressly inserted into this place of the Liturgy, combined (naturally, via *Glory... Both now...*) with the kontakion. The prayer is preceded also by the censing of the deacon, naturally around the koliva, in token of the sanctifying grace of the Holy Spirit. Beginning with the customary invitation: **“Let us pray to the Lord”**, with **“Lord, have mercy”**, and addressed unto God the Father, the prayer over the koliva, **“O Lord, Who hast accomplished all things by Thy word”**, entreats the Creator of all, and in particular of plants—Who miraculously showed forth the full power of their nourishment in Daniel and the Three Holy Youths—to bless that which is set forth and to sanctify those who partake thereof, since this is an offering of the servants of God unto the glory of God and in honor of the foremost servants of God—the commemorated saint and those who have fallen asleep in the pious faith; moreover, the prayer entreats for the providers of the offering and for [all] who celebrate the commemoration of the [saint] over it, that He grant petitions directed toward salvation and eternal good things, through the prayers of the Theotokos, of the commemorated saint, and of all

=== LEAF p960 ===

the saints. Following the prayer, the service concludes customarily: Vespers, as indicated above; the Liturgy with **“Blessed be the name of the Lord.”**

In the practice of the contemporary Russian Church, koliva in honor of saints is offered only in honor of the holy Great-Martyr Theodore the Tyro on Friday of the First Week of Great Lent, in remembrance of his well-known assistance unto Christians during the fast. Yet the customary prayer over the koliva in this instance is preceded by a special moleben unto the Great-Martyr.

#### History of the Order

The rite originated, naturally, from the agape, and represents an inheritance from antiquity just as significant as the prayers for the catechumens at the Liturgy and much else in Orthodox divine worship. Among the described manuscripts, it is provided by MS Moscow Synodal Library No. 335/391, fol. 408. The rite is performed unto this day in the South Slavic Churches, especially on patronal feasts.
