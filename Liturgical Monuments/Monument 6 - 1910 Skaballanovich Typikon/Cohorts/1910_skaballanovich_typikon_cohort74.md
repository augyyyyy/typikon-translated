# THE GREAT READING (*Chtenie Velikoe*)

There is now introduced into the structure of the vigil a new element of divine worship—the reading (*anagnōsis* [Gr. ἀνάγνωσις]). Such a reading is performed at the Sunday all-night vigil 7 times, and at a festal vigil 6 times: after Vespers, after the 1st and 2nd Kathismata, after the Polyeleos, after the 3rd and 6th Odes of the Canon, and after Matins—the last being only on Sunday. Having as their purpose the instruction and edification of the worshippers, these readings serve at the same time to refresh attention and afford rest for the body, since they are listened to while seated. They are of diverse content: whereas the first, at the Sunday vigil, is taken from the Holy Scriptures, the next four are taken from Patristic homilies upon the New Testament, the sixth from the lives of the saints, and the seventh is moral-ascetical—the *Catecheses* of St. Theodore the Studite. Consequently, the most important of these readings is the first, which is therefore called the "Great Reading" [*chtenie velikoe*], although in volume it is smaller than all the rest. From the Holy Scriptures there is taken for this reading that portion which, while being most important and most profitable for a Christian, is rarely known to anyone with the desired thoroughness—namely, the Acts, *(Orig. p. 609)* the Apostolic Epistles, and the Apocalypse, that is, the entire New Testament with the exception of the Gospel, which, in addition to being more or less familiar to everyone, would be inconvenient to hear while seated and with the freedom necessary for rest (for we are bound to hear it standing upright ['Let us attend / Stand upright' — *prosti*]). For each reading there falls 4–5 chapters. The Acts of the Apostles are required to be read only during the Paschal season [from Pascha unto Pentecost]; concerning the Epistles, however, on the Sunday of All Saints it is appointed that their reading should begin on that Sunday with the General Epistle of the Apostle James; consequently, they are read in the Biblical order, concluding with the Apocalypse (during Great Lent) [^2373]. On Palm Sunday and Cheesefare Sunday other readings are appointed, as also at the concurrence of the Twelve Great Feasts. On the Sunday before the Nativity of Christ there is appointed "a reading in the Apostolic commentaries".

## Its Origin

The custom of reading consecutively through the whole of the Holy Scriptures at services, at least of the New Testament, derives its origin from the most ancient times of the Church and was borrowed by her from the synagogue, where the entire divine service was reduced almost exclusively unto reading. In the 1st–6th centuries no distinction was made in importance between the reading of certain sections of Holy Scripture and others, and every reading, with the possible exception of the Gospel, was listened to while seated; from the 4th century they began to select for

=== LEAF p732 ===
readings shorter sections suited unto the day, imparting unto their reading a solemn setting. Owing unto this, many portions, especially of the Old Testament, for lack of time were left unread at divine services. The ascetics returned the Church in this regard unto the former practice: deprived of temple services, they replaced them with the singing of the Psalter and the consecutive reading of the whole of Scripture, reading through at a single sitting entire large books, such as, for example, Isaiah and the Epistle to the Romans. With the foundation of the first monasteries, in those of them that followed the order of the cathedral-parochial service (the monasteries of Palestine and Syria), alongside the traditional short readings adapted unto the day, they endeavored, imitating the practice of the ascetics, to conduct also a continuous reading of the Bible in large sections, entire epistles, for example on Sinai (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 59–61, 104, 105, 161, 167, 189–191, 295). In ancient Western worship, reading was allotted scarcely less, if not even more, space than in the East: there not one of the services was devoid of at least a short reading from the New Testament under the name of *capitulum* or *capitellum*, while at Matins reading occupied as much space as the chanting of the Psalter; furthermore, two special services were assigned unto reading: one after the 1st or 3rd Hour under the name of *capitulum*, and the other after Vespers before Compline—*(Orig. p. 610)* the *collatio* [^2374] (Introductory Chapter, pp. 304–307, 309–319); the latter, evidently, corresponds completely in placement unto the reading under consideration at our Vespers, but its subject matter consisted of Patristic homilies.

## Order of the Great Reading

Concerning the order of this reading the Typikon states:
"The reader [says]: **Amen** (following 'The blessing of the Lord...'), and announces the beginning of the reading. And the priest: **Through the prayers of our holy fathers**. And the priest and the deacon divest themselves of their sacred vestments and depart. And thus all sit down, each in his own place."
By the "beginning of the reading", which is pronounced by the reader prior unto the exclamation of the priest: **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers"**, there is evidently understood the title of the book being read, in the form, for example: "The Reading from the Acts of the Holy Apostles" or "The Reading from the General Epistle of Peter" (as at the Paremias). The priest's exclamation serves as a fitting reminder that the practice of this reading traces back unto the holy ascetics. It goes without saying, and as is stated in the practice—rarely observed anywhere, however—

=== LEAF p733 ===
of these readings (in Russia—at Solovki Monastery, at Valaam, and in a few other monasteries), that this reading must be performed not in a chanting tone (*naraspév*), like the Epistle and Gospel, but in an ordinary, even conversational voice, with the necessary intonations: it replaces a sermon, an instruction. The reader stands facing or half-turned toward the people.

## Ancient Typika on the Great Reading

According unto the Evergetis Typikon, this reading took place between the pannychis and Matins, "amounting unto 8 folios" [^2375].
The Georgian MS:
"there is a reading of the text (a Georgian word signifying the original text in distinction from commentaries and paraphrases) of the Acts of the Holy Apostles; it is meet to know that from Pascha unto the Sunday of All Saints the Acts of the Holy Apostles are read, and on the remaining Sundays of the year the 7 General Epistles and 14 Pauline Epistles and the Apocalypse."
Likewise, with the exception of the word "text", Greek and Slavic MSS, Greek printed editions, and the Old Believer Typikon.
The Order [*Diataxis*] of Patriarch Philotheus:
"when the reading is set forth, the priest pronounces the verse: **Through the prayers of our holy fathers**." The Moscow *Sluzhebniki* do not speak of the reading, except that "when they begin to ring for the third hour (at the 3rd tolling?), then the reader leaves off the reading, and upon the conclusion of the third ringing, the psalm-reader says: **Amen**, and **Glory to God in the highest**."
The *Sluzhebnik* of Peter Mohyla:
"And the reader, having uttered: **Amen**, immediately having read the inscription of the reading, says: **Bless, father**. The priest: **Blessed is our God always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages**. The reader: **Amen**. And he reads as much as is required. And thus the priest and the deacon divest themselves—" [^2376]. *(Orig. p. 611)*

# FRACTION AND CONSUMPTION OF BREAD AND WINE (*Khlebolomlenie i pocherpanie*)

During this reading the worshippers not only restore their strength for the further vigil by sitting, but are also refreshed by partaking of the blessed bread and wine. In the church there thus takes place, as it were, a supper, accompanied, like every monastic meal, by reading, and that of an especially lofty nature. Taking place within the temple, unto the sound of the divinely inspired Scriptures, this is the holiest of meals. It is truly the agape of antiquity.
"The cellarer (*kellaritēs* [Gr. κελλαρίτης], the supervisor of the storeroom), having taken from the blessed loaves and broken them into pieces (cut them into slices), distributes them upon a dish unto the brethren, and ladles out a cup (*potērion* [Gr. ποτήριον]) apiece of (blessed) wine unto all equally, from the superior unto the lowest who are in the monastery. — And when the cellarer has distributed unto the brethren the bread and a cup of wine apiece, on account of the toil (*dia ton kopon* [Gr. διὰ τὸν κόπον]) of the vigil (because of fatigue from the vigil), as we have received from the beginning from the holy fathers, he then departs and makes the customary prostration together with those who served with him (who assisted him in the distribution), two in a rank (the refectory servers who assisted the cellarer stand in pairs before the solea, where they make prostrations) before the holy doors (thanking God for the completed ministration) and unto both choirs one apiece (asking forgiveness for shortcomings), and they depart (unto their places)."
Following the partaking of the blessed bread and wine, the Typikon further observes, it is forbidden to consume any food ("to taste anything") in view of the morrow's communion. In view of this also, this partaking occurs at vigils only from September 1 unto March 25; at summer vigils, however, the blessed loaves are not tasted, but are eaten at the morrow's meal before other food (this is in view of the fact that the Vespers of the vigil, beginning after sunset, in summer, owing unto the brevity of the night, may end too late at night).
"And the blessed loaves have diverse gifts (*charismata* [Gr. χαρίσματα]), that is to say (i.e.), they stay (*pauei* [Gr. παύει]) burning heat (*pyreton* [Gr. πυреτόν], high fever) in those who drink them with water, and shaking ague (*phrikas* [Gr. φρίκας], chills/ague), and heal every infirmity (*malakian* [Gr. μαλακίαν], weakness) and every disease; and in addition unto these, they drive away mice (*sētas* [Gr. σῆτας], which signifies also 'moths') from crops (*gennēmatōn* [Gr. γεννημάτων], granary grains) and expel other noxious pests."

In the *Sluzhebnik* this notice is abridged:

and there is added a warning unto the priest, that he bless not the blessed substances a second time at another vigil, but distribute the bread unto the people and eat it himself at the table before other food, consume the wine with reverence, anoint with the oil at the end of Matins or put it into food, and sow or grind the wheat.

The Georgian MS:
"The cellarer breaks one of the blessed loaves and gives it unto the brethren, as well as a cup apiece of wine on account of fatigue, as this has been handed down unto us by the fathers; the brethren bow unto the sanctuary, unto the right and unto the left, and go forth from the church. From this time forth, in view of the communion of the Holy Mysteries, they must not drink water. ## Historical Foundation of the Evening Blessing of Loaves and Wine

The prototype of this "meal", as has long since been observed by many, was the agape. At the latter the food was frequently as simple as that offered by the present Typikon for consumption at the vigil (Introductory Chapter, pp. 51–52, 75–77). Probably oil and wheat were also added unto the substances being blessed (and they were added, as we have seen, not without hesitation and not all at once) not without thought of the ancient agape, and likewise, probably under the influence of the very ancient practice of blessing oil. In the liturgical monuments of the 4th and neighboring centuries there are prayers for the blessing of water and oil, which were brought into the temple, apparently, alongside the bread and wine for the Eucharist, on which account prayers for their sanctification were placed following the order of the Liturgy. Such a place they occupy in the Syriac *Testament of our Lord Jesus Christ* (*Testamentum Domini Nostri Jesu Christi*), where they are cited with the note: "if the priest sanctifieth oil for the healing of those who suffer, then, placing its vessel before the altar, he saith with compunction"; the prayer beseeches God, Who hath granted us the Holy Spirit, Christ, Who hath sanctified us, made us wise, given us consciousness of sins, the power of His Spirit, the Healer, Who hath given unto the worthy the grace of healing, to send down upon the oil the fullness of His beneficent mercy, that it may relieve the laboring, heal the sick, and sanctify those who come unto faith; "this prayer is said also over water". The *Apostolic Constitutions* beseech the Lord of Sabaoth, Who hath given water for drinking and purification and oil to make the face cheerful, to sanctify these substances through Christ, to grant them "power effective for health, banishing diseases, driving away demons, and expelling every deceitful snare". The prayer in the Euchologion attributed unto Serapion, Bishop of Thmuis (4th c.), "concerning those who offer oil and water", "blessing" these substances "in the Name of Christ", likewise entreats for them "healing power", so that through the drinking of them and anointing with them "every burning heat (*pyretos* [Gr. πυρετός]), everything demonic, and every disease may be repelled" [^2378]. Mention of blessed loaves is made in the *Spiritual Meadow* [*Pratum Spirituale*]: Abba *(Orig. p. 613)* Sergius the Sinaite, having given such bread unto a lion lying upon

=== LEAF p737 ===
the road and hindering his mules from passing, commanded him to get off the road, which the beast duly performed [^2379].
The second part of our Vespers, by its proximity unto the Liturgy, resembles the 2nd part (from the Entrance onward) of Chanted Vespers (Introductory Chapter, p. 342), only there the assimilation was unto the beginning of the Liturgy, whereas here it is unto the end.

# NON-ORTHODOX VESPERS (*Inoslavnye vecherni*)

Instituting a comparison of our Vespers according unto the themes of its hymns and prayers with non-Orthodox evening services, we find in the latter the following most significant differences (the points of agreement have been indicated in the exposition of the rite).
 
Armenian Vespers hath at the beginning Psalm 54:17–18 [LXX; Masoretic Ps. 55:16–17]: **"As for me, I cried unto God, and the Lord heard me; evening and morning and at noon will I tell and declare it, and He shall hear my voice"**, Psalm 85 [LXX; Masoretic 86] **"Bow down Thine ear, O Lord"**, Psalm 139 [LXX; Masoretic 140] **"Deliver me, O Lord"**, Psalm 120 [LXX; Masoretic 121] **"I have lifted up mine eyes"**, Psalm 90 [LXX; Masoretic 91] **"He that dwelleth in the help of the Most High"**, Psalm 122 [LXX; Masoretic 123] **"Unto Thee have I lifted up mine eyes"** (in our rite, at Daily Vespers), Psalm 53 [LXX; Masoretic 54] **"Save me, O God, in Thy name"** [^2380]; thus, all the psalms of our Sixth Hour, one of the Ninth Hour, and one from the Midnight Office entered into Armenian Vespers.
 
Coptic Vespers: Psalm 130 [LXX; Masoretic 131] **"Lord, my heart is not exalted"**, 131 [LXX; Masoretic 132] **"Remember, O Lord, David"**, 136 [LXX; Masoretic 137] **"By the waters of Babylon"**, 137 [LXX; Masoretic 138] **"I will give thanks unto Thee, O Lord"**, 145 [LXX; Masoretic 146] **"Praise the Lord, O my soul"**, 146 [LXX; Masoretic 147:1–11] **"Praise ye the Lord, for a psalm is good"**, 147 [LXX; Masoretic 147:12–20] **"Praise the Lord, O Jerusalem"**, the Gospel of Luke 2:25–30 on the Meeting of the Lord, the Creed [*Credo*], the 1st prayer with thanksgiving for the past day and with a petition to accept the evening doxology, to deliver from the snares of the evil one, and to grant a peaceful evening and night; the 2nd prayer for the forgiveness of the sins of the day, restful sleep, a guardian angel, and deliverance from evil and the torment of the devil [^2381].
 
Abyssinian [Ethiopian] Vespers: Psalm 117 [LXX; Masoretic 118] **"Give thanks unto the Lord"** and the "Songs of Ascents"—Psalms 119–128 [LXX; Masoretic 120–129] (**"Unto the Lord in mine affliction"**, in our rite the customary Kathisma at Daily Vespers); the Gospel of Mark 1:29–34 on the healing of Simon's mother-in-law and the evening healings of the Savior; **"Glory to God in the highest... goodwill"**; a petition for forgiveness through the prayers of the Theotokos; **"We worship Thee, O Christ, with Thy Good Father and the Holy Spirit, for Thou hast come and saved us"**; our 4 penitential stichera: **"I would fain blot out with tears"** [*Khotekh slezami*]; from the service of the Great Canon the stichera: **"My whole life"** [*Vse zhitie moe*], **"With the weight of heedlessness"** [*Tyazhestiyu nebrezheniya*], **"I have become an offense"** [*Bykh pretknovenie*]; our Theotokion **"Rejoice, O Pure One"** [*Raduisya Chistaya*]; **"It is good to give praise"**; penitential sessional hymns from the Psalter after the 17th Kathisma: **"I have sinned against Thee, O Savior"** [*Sogreshikh k Tebe Spase*], **"I cry unto Thee"** [*Zovu k Tebe*]; the Martyr's hymn [*martyrikon*] **"They who desired not earthly pleasures"** [*Izhe zemnyya slasti ne vozzhelevshe*]; the Theotokion **"O Virgin Theotokos Mary, preserve Thy flock"** [*Bogoroditse Devo Marie sokhrani stado Tvoe*]; penitential troparia: **"As the prodigal son"** [*Yako bludnyi syn*], **"I pray Thee like the publican"** [*Molyutisya yako mytar*]; a prayer unto the Lord Jesus with confession of the sins of the day and a petition for amendment, with

Amen"** [^2385]. *(Orig. p. 615)*
