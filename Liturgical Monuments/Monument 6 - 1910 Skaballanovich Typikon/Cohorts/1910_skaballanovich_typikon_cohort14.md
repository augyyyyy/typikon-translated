#### The Daily Office in the 4th and 5th Centuries

The abolition of the agape imparted unto the cycle of the daily offices a thoroughly spiritual, "noetic" character, depriving Christian worship of the last element that made it somewhat resemble the cultus both of the Old Testament and of natural religions.

Owing unto this abolition, the various daily offices could not fail to receive, at the outset at least, an approximately identical structure, which they retained throughout the course of the two centuries under consideration. Only the subsequent period in the history of divine worship (the 6th to 8th centuries) engaged in the differentiation of the daily offices from one another and gave unto each of them its own distinctive character.

At the present time we possess rather detailed information concerning the daily divine services of the 4th and 5th centuries from three different lands and parts of the Christian world of that day, each of which elaborated its liturgical ritual independently, although—owing unto the constant close communion of the churches—not without external influence. We have in view the liturgical data contained: (1) in the *Apostolic Constitutions*; (2) in the *Pilgrimage to the Holy Places* (*Peregrinatio ad Loca Sancta*) of the late 4th century, attributed unto Sylvia of Aquitaine; and (3) in the ancient monastic rules. The first monument, being, as we have seen, of Syrian origin [^598], acquaints us with the divine worship of the Syrian Church of the 4th and early 5th centuries; the second, with the divine worship of the Church of Jerusalem of the late 4th century; and the third, with the rule of prayer of the Egyptian and Palestinian ascetics of the beginning of the 5th century. The first two monuments present the type of cathedral-parochial church services for the laity; the last constitutes the first and most ancient typikon of monastic divine worship. Both types of church service could not fail to exert an influence upon the liturgical practice of the Laura of St. Sabbas the Sanctified—a disciple of the disciples of the Egyptian ascetics, who founded his monastery in immediate proximity unto Jerusalem—the founder of the present Church Typikon, whose activity belongs already unto the 6th century.

#### Vespers and Matins According to the *Apostolic Constitutions*

The *Apostolic Constitutions*—a monument, as we have seen, of diverse dates of origin—speak of morning and evening worship in three places: in Book II, and in the appendices to Books VII and VIII.

=== LEAF p132 ===
The first passage belongs unto the most ancient portions of the monument, specifically representing a redaction of the 3rd-century Syriac *Didascalia* made in the 4th to 5th centuries [^599]. The redaction in this instance expressed itself in the fact that unto the general exhortation of the *Didascalia* to pray frequently *(Orig. p. 135)* in church, the *Apostolic Constitutions* add the requirement of morning and evening public worship and appoint for each a specific psalm. (The additions of the *Constitutions* unto the *Didascalia* we print in italics).

"O bishop, command the people and exhort them to assemble constantly (*endelechizein*, ἐνδελεχίζειν) in church every day, morning and evening, and not to forsake the assembly (*synolon*, σύнолон), but to frequent it continually... that no one by his absence may maim the Church and sever into parts the Body of Christ. *Every day assemble ye morning and evening, chanting and praying in the churches* (*psallontes kai proseuchomenoi en tois kyriakois*, ψάλλοντες καὶ προσευχόμενοι ἐν τοῖς κυριακοῖς), *in the morning reciting* (*legontes*, λέγοντες) *Psalm 62, and in the evening Psalm 140*" [^600].

The first of these psalms (**"O God, My God, Unto Thee Do I Watch from the Dawn"** [*«Боже, Боже мой, к Тебе утренюю»*, Ps. 62]), which enters into the composition of our present Six Psalms (in third place), is appointed for morning worship from that same era by Pseudo-Athanasius in the treatise *On Virginity* (*De Virginitate*) [^601] and, as we shall see, by Chrysostom. The former adds: "and at daybreak (recite): **'Bless the Lord, All Ye Works of the Lord'**" (*«Благословите, вся дела Господня, Господа»*, that is, the Song of the Three Holy Children). Concerning the use of Psalm 62 at the morning service St. John Cassian speaks [^602], and somewhat later (at the beginning of the 6th century) St. Benedict [^603].

In the appendix unto Book VII of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, without any connection with what precedes, and in certain codices even without title, is placed a "morning prayer" (*proseuchē*, προσευχή), beginning with the words **"Glory to God in the Highest"** (*«Слава в вышних Богу»*) and almost verbatim identical with a portion of the present Great Doxology; then an "evening" (*hesperinos*, ἑσπερινός)—presumably an evening hymn—**"Praise the Lord, Ye Children"** (*«Хвалите, отроцы, Господа»*), close unto the present **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*«Сподоби Господи»*) with the **"Now Dismissest Thou"** (*«Ныне отпущаеши»*, *Nunc Dimittis*) in conclusion; and finally a "prayer at breakfast" (*euchē ep' aristō*, εὐχὴ ἐπ' ἀρίστῳ): **"Blessed Art Thou, O Lord, Who Dost Nourish Me from My Youth"** (*«Благословен еси, Господи, питаяй мя от юности моея»*), similar unto the prayer in the present Order of the Panagia [^604]. Of the first of these prayers (**"Glory to God in the Highest"**) Pseudo-Athanasius also makes mention [^605] and cites its beginning; it is likewise read in the Alexandrian Codex of Holy Scripture (5th century) and others as an appendix unto the Psalms, with slight variation from its present form. The last prayer in almost the same form as in the *Apostolic Constitutions* is cited by Pseudo-Athanasius [^606] and by St. John Chrysostom [^607].

Finally, in the appendix unto Book VIII, which is not present in all codices, the *Apostolic Constitutions* provide the order and even the text of evening and

=== LEAF p133 ===
morning worship. The very structure of both is completely identical, yet the content, naturally, is different—adapted unto the time of day. Inasmuch as the *Apostolic Constitutions* attribute each *(Orig. p. 136)* of their sections unto one of the Twelve Apostles, so also the present section, which occupies the final place in the book and constitutes an appendix unto it, the monument attributes unto an Apostle, not however from among the Twelve, but the primate of the remaining (seventy) Apostles—James the Brother of the Lord. Inasmuch as this Apostle according to tradition was the first Bishop of the Church of Jerusalem, the placement of his name over the order of worship may suggest a Jerusalem origin for this order. Indeed, the descriptions of Jerusalem divine worship at the end of the 4th century in Sylvia of Aquitaine [^608] speak of a significant similarity between this practice and that one, well-nigh of an identity in the fundamental features of their structure.

"When evening cometh, thou, O bishop, gather the Church; and after the evening (*epilychnios*, ἐπιλύχνιος) psalm hath been recited (*rhēthēnai*, ῥηθῆναι), the deacon proclaims (*prosphōnēsei*, προσφωνήσει)—(that is, pronounces the appointed prayer petitions, corresponding unto the present litany)—for the catechumens, for the tempest-tossed (*cheimazomenoi*, χειмаζομένων) [^609], for those being illuminated, and for the penitents, as we have said above (that is, in the order of the Liturgy). And after their dismissal the deacon says: **'As Many as are of the Faithful, Let Us Pray to the Lord'** (*«Елицы вернии, помолимся Господу»*); and after he proclaims that which is contained in the first prayer (*ta tēs prōtēs euchēs*, τὰ τῆς πρώτης εὐχῆς, meaning the prayer of the deacon in the order of the Liturgy, corresponding unto the present Great Litany and close unto it), he says..."

There follows under the heading "Evening Proclamation" (*prosphōnēsis epilychnios*, προσφώνησις ἐπιλύχνιος) a series of petitions, close unto the present Litany of Supplication [^610], beginning: **"Save and Raise Us Up, O God: Having Arisen, Let Us Pray..."** (*«Спаси и возстави нас, Боже: вставши помолимся…»*), which points unto a preceding kneeling prayer [^611]. After the petitions (the Litany of Supplication) of the deacon there follows the prayer of the bishop, beginning: **"O God Without Beginning and Without End, Maker of All Things"**, which entreats God, Who created day and night, the dawn and the sun, and Who hath brought us through the day unto the beginning of night, to receive the evening thanksgiving, to grant us a peaceful evening and a sinless night, and to vouchsafe us eternal life (close unto the third part of the first kneeling prayer of Pentecost). After this there is pronounced by the bishop yet another prayer of the laying on of hands, preceded by the exclamation of the deacon: "Bow yourselves down for the laying on of hands"; this prayer entreats God, Who fashioned man, placed him at the head of earthly inhabitants, and established among men rulers (*archontes*, ἄρχοντες) and priests, to incline Himself unto those who have bowed the necks of their hearts unto Him, to manifest His face unto them, and to bless them through Christ.

=== LEAF p134 ===
The service concluded with the exclamation of the deacon: **"Depart in Peace"** (*«Изыдите в мире»*).

*(Orig. p. 137)*

Matins of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, being, as observed, of the same structure as Vespers, differs from it only in the content of the prayers. It begins with the "morning psalm," by which is understood, as is evident from what precedes, Psalm 62. The diaconal petitions at it (the Litany of Supplication) are adapted unto the morning (the words "evening" and "night" are replaced by "morning" and "day"; there are also other minor differences in the text of the petitions). The first prayer of the bishop for the faithful (after the departure of the catechumens, etc.), **"O God of Spirits and of All Flesh"**, entreats God, Who created the sun, moon, and stars, mercifully to look down upon us, to receive our morning thanksgivings, and to vouchsafe us eternal life for our fidelity unto Him ("for we have not stretched out our hands unto a strange god"). The second prayer, of the bowing of heads, **"O Eternal and True God"**, entreats God, Who art merciful unto thousands and myriads that love Him, the Friend of the humble and Protector of the poor, in Whom all things have need and Whom all things serve, to look upon those who have bowed their heads unto Him, to bless them with a spiritual blessing, to preserve them in piety and righteousness, and to vouchsafe them eternal life.

In comparison with the only complete order of the daily office from the 3rd century that has come down to us—namely, Matins in the *Testamentum of Our Lord Jesus Christ* [^612]—this order of worship, belonging unto approximately the same church, if not shorter and simpler, is, apparently, in no wise longer or more complex. Yet it must be taken into account that the present order almost exclusively (if we except the initial psalm, which could not fail to be mentioned) indicates the prayers of the sacred ministers at the service (like the present *Sluzhebnik*). It is incredible that throughout an entire Vespers or Matins not a single hymn of Christian composition was sung, if such hymns already in the 2nd century (according to Pliny) and even in the Apostolic age constituted the principal content of divine worship. The same *Apostolic Constitutions* above cite morning and evening hymns, but here they do not indicate a place for them in the order of Matins and Vespers. It is likewise scarcely probable that at an entire service only one single psalm was sung, as also that there were no readings of Holy Scripture or instruction. Thus, the order of Matins and Vespers given by the *Apostolic Constitutions*, being intentionally incomplete and restricting itself unto prayers alone, does not permit of a detailed comparison with the daily office of the 3rd century, for example in the *Testamentum*. Such a comparison is possible only with respect unto the prayers of the two liturgical orders. The prayers at the divine service of the *Apostolic Constitutions*, in comparison with the prayers of the *Testamentum*, are distinguished by a greater generality of content and, as it were, less warmth of feeling. Furthermore, noticeable is an intensification of the ceremonial aspect in the prayers: not to speak of an entire gradation of worshipers into classes—instead of the verbal blessing of the sacred minister

=== LEAF p135 ===
(with the words: "The Grace of the Lord... be with you") in the *Testamentum*, the present monument introduces the laying on of hands by the bishop, applied in the *Testamentum* only unto the catechumens, imparting unto it a special significance, making it the focus and *(Orig. p. 138)* principal moment of the prayer. In the arrangement of the prayers in comparison with the *Testamentum*, one cannot fail to notice a greater regularity, proportion, and sequence. This arrangement closely approaches the present one: a portion of our Vespers, beginning with the Litany of Supplication, with respect unto the prayers of the sacred ministers is given here completely, having finished its development: unto the first prayer of the bishop following the "proclamation" of the deacon corresponds the present exclamation after the Litany of Supplication, which is a remnant of the former prayer here (which was later joined unto the seven prayers of the Lucernarium); after this exclamation there now follows among us, as in the *Apostolic Constitutions*, the prayer of the bowing of heads. But the most important step in comparison with the 3rd century is that Vespers receives an equal place in the rank of daily offices alongside Matins (a consequence of the abolition of the agape and the definitive transference of the Liturgy unto the morning).

#### The 4th-Century Jerusalem Liturgy According to the *Pilgrimage* Attributed to Sylvia

If the *Apostolic Constitutions* have preserved for us models of prayer at the daily office of the 4th and 5th centuries and therefore acquaint us with the inner content of the latter, the *Pilgrimage to the Holy Places* (*Peregrinatio ad Loca Sancta*), attributed unto Sylvia of Aquitaine [^613] and describing the divine worship of the Church of Jerusalem of the late 4th century, acquaints us with the outward structure and *(Orig. p. 139)* composition of divine worship of that same epoch and approximately of the same region or one neighboring that wherein the *Apostolic Constitutions* arose.

The pilgrim describes first the weekday services, then the Sunday services, and finally the festal services (the latter without beginning or end). But the fundamental structure of each of these kinds of divine services is the same; Sunday and festal worship differ from weekday worship more in the place of celebration than in composition. The cycle of the daily offices consists of Matins, the hours of Terce, Sext, and None, and Vespers (not counting the Divine Liturgy); yet of these Terce was celebrated only in the Great Fast, while Sext and None, apparently, were celebrated only on weekdays.

The divine worship described by the pilgrim is so closely bound in its structure unto the place of its celebration that for an understanding of her description a clear conception is necessary of all the temples unto which it is attached. Usually (excepting feasts) it is celebrated in three temples, built by Constantine the Great upon the site of the Crucifixion and Burial of Christ and constituting, perhaps, a single complex of edifices: (a) the Church of the Resurrection, called by the pilgrim directly

=== LEAF p136 ===
the "Resurrection" (*Anastasis*); in it or beside it was the cave (*spelunca*) of the Sepulchre of the Lord, separated from the church by a lattice (*cancelli*) and constituting the sanctuary (altar) of this church; (b) the Church at the Cross (*ad Crucem*), that is, upon the site of the finding of the Cross of Christ or for its preservation; from the first church it was separated by a large courtyard, called "Before the Cross" (*ante Crucem*) [^614]; the place behind this church was called "Behind the Cross" (*post Crucem*), and here also (*post Crucem*) stood (c) the third, greater church (*ecclesia major*), called the Martyrium [^615], because it was situated upon Golgotha itself, the site of the "passion" (Crucifixion) of Christ [^616]. In these three churches the daily and Sunday divine services are celebrated, passing from one church unto another. Festal divine worship, moreover, proceeded in solemn litanies unto other churches of Jerusalem and its environs, such as: the church on Sion, upon the site of the house wherein the Savior appeared unto the Apostles after the Resurrection; the churches on the Mount of Olives (one over the cave wherein Christ customarily taught the Apostles, and another upon the site of the Ascension, called the Imbomon); the church in Gethsemane; the church near Bethany; and unto Bethlehem over the cave of the Nativity of Christ [^617].

*(Orig. p. 140)*

#### Matins and the Hours According to Sylvia

The daily divine worship of the Church of Jerusalem of that day, according unto the description of the pilgrim, consisted of Matins, Sext, None, and Vespers. Weekday Matins, not designated by this name nor indeed by any name in the pilgrim, was celebrated entirely in the Church of the Resurrection (*Anastasis*). It began by night, "before cockcrow"; but prior unto dawn there took place its less solemn part, celebrated without the bishop by the priests and deacons in course for the monastics and virgins (*monazontes et parthenae*), and also for the more zealous among the laity of both sexes (*et laici praeterea—viri aut mulieres*). This part of Matins (corresponding unto our Midnight Office and the portion of Matins prior unto the Kathisma hymns / Exaposteilarion) consisted of hymns, psalms, and "antiphons," wherein the chanting of psalms was responsorial, that is, with the whole people chanting the concluding words of each part or each verse of the psalm (*dicuntur ymni et psalmi responduntur, similiter et antiphonae*); the psalms and antiphons, the pilgrim observes in another place, at divine worship always corresponded in content unto the time of day; at Matins they were not the same as at Vespers and the Hours—"always so suitable, so meaningful (*rationabiles*), that they relate unto that which is being performed" [^618];

"at each hymn (*cata singulos ymnos*)," continues the pilgrim her description of Matins, "there is a prayer, which a presbyter pronounces. When dawn breaks, they begin the morning hymns; and behold, there arrives the bishop with the clergy, and immediately (differently from Vespers) he enters

=== LEAF p137 ===
inside the cave (the cave of the Sepulchre of the Lord, which served as the altar) and from within the lattice (which separates the cave from the temple—corresponding unto the iconostasis) he first utters a prayer for all and commemorates the names of those whom he desires, and then (*sic*) blesses the catechumens; thereafter he utters a prayer and blesses the faithful; and upon the departure of the bishop from behind the lattice, all approach unto his hand, and he, having come forth, blesses each one individually; then already at daybreak the dismissal takes place (*fit missa*) [^619]. At the sixth hour (at noon) all likewise go down unto the Resurrection; psalms and antiphons are recited (*dicuntur*), until they notify the bishop: he likewise comes down and sits not (differently from Vespers), but immediately enters inside the lattice (as at *(Orig. p. 141)* Matins) into the Resurrection, that is, where he had been also in the morning, and from thence in like manner he first makes a prayer, then (*sic*) blesses the faithful, and when he comes forth from behind the lattice, all likewise approach unto his hand.—The same is done at the ninth hour as at the sixth" [^620].

Thereafter the pilgrim describes the last office of the day—Vespers. Thus the pilgrim does not speak of the celebration of the Liturgy on weekdays, unless the latter is designated by the words *missa fit* (on Sunday she does speak of it). The distinction between the church services in the description of the pilgrim does not stand forth at all: the hours of the day (Sext and None) possess, apparently, entirely the same composition as Matins (Vespers, as we shall see, was more complex than Matins); if this were indeed so, if the similarity of the services here ought not to be attributed unto the generality and imprecision of the description or unto insufficient observation, then the Jerusalem divine worship of this epoch resembled the later so-called chanted office (*asmatike akolouthia*) [^621], which imparted unto the hours, at least in certain instances (for example, in Passion Week or generally in the Great Fast), a structure identical with Matins and Vespers: with chanting, litanies, the reading of the Apostle and the Gospel, and the like. Yet the expression of the pilgrim concerning Sext does not permit, however, an entirely complete identification of its structure with Matins, since she does not mention (possibly on account of the brevity of the description) the hymns (*hymni*) and the prayers accompanying them at the hours; nor does the pilgrim mention a special prayer at the hours for the catechumens. The principal and central moment of each service is the prayer of the bishop inside the lattice in the very cave of the Sepulchre of the Lord (the altar of the temple) and, as it were, the blessing brought forth by him from thence unto the people. Thus that prayerful presence of the bishop at each of the sacred hours in the temple, which the 3rd-century monument *The Testamentum of Our Lord Jesus Christ* only

=== LEAF p138 ===
desires [^622], here finds realization, although for this purpose the number of prayer hours is reduced.

#### Vespers According to Sylvia

A completely distinctive and the most solemn service in the course of the day in the Church of Jerusalem of the 4th century, according unto the testimony of the monument under consideration, was Vespers. This service was so solemn that it did not change its composition (and perhaps its content) even for Sunday and for the greatest feasts (only in Passion Week and on Pascha did Vespers differ from the weekday office in that it had a Gospel reading).

"At the tenth hour, which is called *licinicon* (read *lychnikon*, λυχνικόν), and which we call Vespers (*lucernare*), in like manner all gather in the Resurrection; all the lamps and tapers (*candelae et cerei*) are kindled, and there is made *(Orig. p. 142)* an immense (*infinitum*) light. And fire is not brought in from without, but is provided from the interior of the cave, that is, from behind the lattice, where day and night a lamp burns. The evening psalms and more prolonged antiphons (than at Matins or the Hours?) are sung (selected verses of the psalms?). Then they notify the bishop, who comes down and sits above (upon the cathedra); the presbyters also sit in their places (evidently for hearing the readings, which in later typika were called 'sitting above' or 'sitting below' [^623]). Hymns and antiphons are sung (not psalms, as previously). And when they have brought them unto an end (*ad ubi perducti fuerint*), the bishop arises and stands before the lattice; and one of the deacons commemorates all individually, as is customary; and when the deacon pronounces each one's name, there stand many boys (*pisinni*), who constantly respond: **'Kyrie eleison'**... their voices are multitudinous [^624]... The bishop pronounces a prayer and prays for all, after which all pray, both the faithful and the catechumens together. Then the deacon proclaims that each of the catechumens, as he stands (*quomodo stat*) [^625], should bow his head, and the bishop, standing, pronounces a blessing over the catechumens. Then a prayer is read, and the deacon again proclaims (*mittit vocem*) that each of the faithful, standing, should bow the head, and the bishop blesses the faithful, after which the dismissal takes place (*fit missa*) in the Resurrection; and all begin to approach unto the hand of the bishop. And after this, (going) from the Resurrection unto the Cross, they sing a hymn (*ymnus dicitur*); the bishop goes together with all the people. When they arrive (as is evident from what follows,

=== LEAF p139 ===
at the courtyard 'Before the Cross'), first the bishop reads a prayer and blesses the faithful. After this the bishop and all the people go Behind the Cross (presumably the courtyard behind the Church of the Cross—by analogy with the preceding), where again the same is performed as Before the Cross (the conclusion of Vespers—for the third time). And in like manner they approach unto the hand of the bishop, as in the Resurrection; so do they both Before the Cross and Behind the Cross. Everywhere hang multitudinous huge glass lamps (*candelae vitreae ingentes plurimae*) and multitudinous candlesticks (*cereofala*), both before the Resurrection, and Before the Cross and Behind the Cross. All this concludes with twilight (*cum crebris*). Such a service (*operatio*) is celebrated daily throughout six days at the Cross and in the Resurrection" [^626].

Consequently, the distinction of Vespers from Ma*(Orig. p. 143)*tins, imparting unto it greater solemnity, was: (a) the sitting of the sacred ministers, that is, the readings at it (*paroimiai*); (b) the litany of the deacon; and (c) the Litiya unto the nearest sacred localities, at which the final most important prayers and rites of Vespers were repeated, as though Vespers were celebrated again in miniature. The *paroimiai* and the Litiya even today constitute the principal distinction of Vespers. The Litiya, characteristically designated by ancient Slavonic typika as the "departure" (*iskhozhdenie*) [^627], was evoked in the practice of this church, evidently, by the desire to pray in all the places marked by Christ's Passion, at the very site of the Crucifixion (for which reason on feasts it is directed also unto distant localities outside the city), and by the memories bound up with them to rekindle prayerful fervor. It is remarkable that the Litiya does not enter into the other church (into the Martyrium), but is performed before it and behind it (as today—in the narthex).

#### Sunday Worship According to Sylvia

Sunday Matins differed from the weekday office above all in that its first part, celebrated without the bishop, was conducted not in the Church of the Resurrection, which on that day was opened not earlier than cockcrow, but in the basilica beside this church. The bishop arrived for Matins not at dawn, as on weekdays, but with the first crowing of the cock. Only with his arrival was the Church of the Resurrection, abundantly illuminated, opened unto the people, and in it Matins began as it were anew (on weekdays the bishop merely continued or rather concluded it with his prayers); there were sung responsorially three psalms—the first by a presbyter, the second by a deacon, the third by a cleric, after each of which a prayer followed; at the third prayer the commemoration of all was made. Then censers were brought in, and the church was filled with fragrance; the bishop, entering behind the lattice, took the Gospel and

=== LEAF p140 ===
from the doors (in the lattice) read of the Resurrection of the Lord. After the Gospel they went with hymns unto the Cross; there a psalm was sung, a prayer was read, the bishop blessed the people, and the dismissal took place (*missa fit*); the bishop went home, but the service continued: the monastics with the priests and deacons in course and with the more zealous among the faithful returned unto the Resurrection, where until dawn psalms and antiphons were sung with a prayer after each of them [^628]. That is, into Sunday Matins, in comparison with the weekday office, was introduced the reading of the Gospel and a Litiya.

"At daybreak in the Martyrium," continues the pilgrim, "all is performed according unto the custom observed everywhere on the Lord's Day," that is, there takes place, as is evident from her subsequent description, the Divine Liturgy (*missa*); inasmuch as it is preceded by the instruction of several presbyters and of the bishop, it is prolonged until the fourth or fifth hour (10:00–11:00 a.m. according unto our reckoning). After the Liturgy the monastics escort the bishop with songs unto the Church of the Resurrection, whither together with him enter only the faithful, without the catechumens; the bishop enters behind the lattice; thanksgiving is rendered (*aguntur gratiae*) unto God, there takes place the prayer of the bowing of heads, the blessing of the bishop from within the lattice, and all is *(Orig. p. 144)* concluded (*protrahitur missa*) at about the fifth or sixth hour (11:00 a.m.–12:00 noon). Vespers is celebrated daily (*item et ad lucernares similiter fit juxta consuetudinem cotidianam*) [^629]. Thus there were, apparently, no Hours on Sunday; as it were in their stead the Liturgy received at the conclusion a special supplementary part—a Litiya for the faithful alone (inasmuch as the catechumens did not remain until the end of the Liturgy).

The pilgrim notes the exceptional concourse of people at Sunday Matins and the contrition wherewith the people listened unto the Sunday Gospel [^630].

Comparing this liturgical practice with the practice of the *Apostolic Constitutions* and taking into account the intentional incompleteness noted above wherewith the latter is depicted, we find that the most important daily offices—Matins and Vespers—here and there possess a well-nigh literally identical structure. To speak of "psalms," "hymns," and "antiphons," as we have seen, lay outside the scope of the task of the *Apostolic Constitutions*; as regards the prayers of the sacred ministers, which constituted the second and most important part of Vespers and Matins, they follow in both monuments one and the same order, close unto our present one. The chief distinction of the Jerusalem practice (in the *Pilgrimage*) from the Syrian (in the *Apostolic Constitutions*) is that unto Matins was imparted less solemnity in comparison with Vespers (and this, presumably, was a novelty and a reform—cf. the *Testamentum*), for which reason in the former the diaconal litany is omitted, while unto the latter is added the Litiya, tripling the conclusion of Vespers. A difference purely incidental from a liturgical perspective is that the Church of Jerusalem did not know such an elaborate gradation in the ranks of worshipers as appears in the *Apostolic Constitutions* (catechumens, the tempest-tossed, those being illuminated, penitents), but divided them
