Such are the *litania major* on April 25 on the feast of the Apostle Mark, and the *litania minor* or *rogationes* (supplications) on the three days before the Ascension. The designations "greater" and "lesser" do not correspond to the comparative solemnity of these litanies, but are explained historically: the former was instituted earlier (probably by Pope Liberius in the fourth century) in place of the very solemn pagan procession *ambarvale* (from *arva*, field—a procession into the fields, as distinguished from the *amburbale*, a city procession) to propitiate the goddess Robigo, upon whom depended the rust (*robigo*) on grain crops (for which reason April 25 was called the *Robigalia*); Pope Gregory the Great regulated the Christian litany that replaced this pagan procession, directing seven distinct corporations of Rome (clergy, men, monks, handmaids, women, widows, and the poor with children) to set forth in litany from different churches (hence the name *litania septiformis*; *Epistle* II, 2). Concerning the origin of monasticism on Athos there is no reliable information; Athonite traditions trace the appearance of monasteries here back to pagan times (the Emperor Caracalla, 211–217 [printed: 219]) or at least to the flourishing of Egyptian-Palestinian asceticism; the Arab devastations of Athos circa 670 and then circa 830 do not permit anything reliable to be affirmed concerning Athos prior to the ninth century, although the foundation of almost all present-day Athonite monasteries ascends to this most ancient period. The first historically known Athonite solitary ("hesychast") was Venerable Peter (who lived on Athos 681–734); in the era of Iconoclasm many monks sought refuge here; Venerable Euthymius (circa 840) restored monasticism on Athos after the second Arab devastation; his disciples established the first cenobium on Athos—Vatopedi; and John Kolobos built the first stone monastery on the isthmus at Ierissos and obtained from Emperor Basil the Macedonian in 872 a chrysobull by which Athos was granted into the exclusive possession of monks. But at this point enmity began between cenobites and anchorites; the latter separated and established their own administration in Karyes (the "Protaton") with a protos at its head. After a new Saracen devastation circa the middle of the tenth century, monasticism on Athos was restored by Venerable Athanasius, who founded the Great Lavra on Athos through the donations of his friend, Emperor Nicephorus Phocas. Upon the complaint of the anchorites concerning the dominance of cenobitic life—foreign to ancient Athos—Emperor John Tzimiskes by the typikon (*τυπικόν* or *τράγος*) of 971 recognized equal rights for both modes of ascetic life on Athos. To this period belong the first authentic testimonies concerning the majority of present-day Athonite monasteries, such as Iviron (founded by Georgians), Docheiariou or Daphne, Panteleimon or Thessalonian, Esphigmenou (founded by inhabitants of the Thessalian village of Sphigi), Hilandar (named after the family of the ktetor), Zographou, founded by the Greek George the Iconographer (in Greek *ζωγράφος*), Koutloumousiou (after the surname of the ktetor), Xiropotamou [printed: *Skiropotamos*] ("dry torrent"), Dionysiou, founded by Alexios Komnenos [printed: *1118*, recte Alexios III Komnenos] at the request of Dionysius who labored there. These conflicting accounts are reconciled by the supposition that the monk Michael possessed an incomplete copy of the rule, perhaps the *Hypotyposis*, for which reason ten years later Venerable Theodosius felt the need for the complete ("entire") rule.—Prior to the appearance of the Studite Typikon and afterward, until the Tatar invasion, in cathedral and parish churches, it is thought, people in Russia were guided by the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople. The Council of Vladimir in 1274 and that of Constantinople in 1276 establish the existence in the Russian Church of certain customs of the Great Church, such as the performance of the proskomedia ("proskomisanie") by a deacon (apparently such may have been the most ancient custom generally), the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts on Cheese-fare Wednesday and Friday and on Great Friday, the reading of the Gospel by the hierarch four times a year (the first Passion Gospel, at the Paschal Liturgy and Vespers, and at the Litiya on September 1), the Exaltation of the Cross with features of the Great Church: some of these customs are confirmed by councils, while others are abolished (A. Pavlov, *Russian Historical Library*, St. Petersburg, 1880, VI, nos. 6, 12), yet in places all were maintained down to the fifteenth and even seventeenth centuries, especially in the Novgorod territory, which occupied the entire north of Russia. In addition, in relatively late manuscripts of the fourteenth–fifteenth centuries there has survived the translation of whole rites of the Great Church into Church Slavonic, for example, the Rite of Washing the Altar Table on Great Thursday, the Rite of the New Year on September 1, and the litany according to the Typikon of the Great Church. The same is attested by Church Slavonic manuscripts of asmatika (refrains to the psalms in the Sung Office, for example, *Tin ikoumenin* = **"The universe"** [Greek: *Τὴν οἰκουμένην*]), the expression in ancient typika "chanting at the trithekte" indicating the existence of this office, and finally even a fragment of the Typikon of the Great Church in the Slavonic translation of Archbishop Clement of Novgorod († 1299) in the Moscow Khludov Library (A. Popov, *Supplement to the Description of Manuscripts of the Khludov Library*, Moscow, 1875, p. 117), etc. See concerning this in more detail and with citations in the work now in press by Archpriest M.
