=== LEAF p81 ===
the Strengthener and Protector of our souls, and unto His Word, Who resteth with His saints, the uncreated Artificer, the Expounder of invisible and pure holy things, Who hath revealed unto us the mysteries of wisdom and promised the light of immortality; in the second section of the prayer, addressed at first likewise unto God the Father and His Son, our Creator and Redeemer, a petition is offered that the Son of God would grant the ability unceasingly to praise Him; in the third section of the prayer, addressed already solely unto the Son of God, He is glorified for various specific benefits, for instance, for help unto those sailing upon the sea, those imprisoned in dungeons, those tortured, those crucified upon the cross, and a petition is offered that He would grant us to walk in His commandments and visit in His mercy the small and the great, the prince and his people, the pastor and his flock. Unto each of the first two sections of the prayer the people respond: **"Thee we praise, Thee we bless, unto Thee we give thanks, O Lord, and we pray unto Thee, our God"** (*Tebya slavim, Tebya blagoslovim, Tebe ispoveduyemsya, Gospodi, i molimsya Tebe, Bozhe nash*) [^415]; and unto the last: *(Orig. p. 86)* **"Amen."** Then:

"They sing psalms and four songs: one from Moses, one from Solomon (perchance Psalm 71 [LXX 71 / MT 72], inscribed with the name of Solomon), and one from the prophets (that is, from the canticles of our present canon). Boys, two virgins, three deacons, and three presbyters sing (it is not indicated whether together, or alternately—antiphonally). And the doxology is pronounced by the bishop and presbyter in the following manner: **"The grace of our Lord be with you all."** The people say: **"And with thy spirit."** The priest saith: **"Let us praise our Lord."** The people: **"It is meet and right."** The priest: **"Let your hearts be lifted up."** The people: **"We lift them up unto the Lord."**"

— Then in the monument there followeth, under the title: "Final Doxology" (*collaudatio finalis*), a prayer in three parts; in the first God the Father is glorified for granting us light bodily and spiritual—faith—and for His continual benefits, and a petition is contained that the Lord would grant us unceasingly to glorify Him; in the second the Lord Jesus is glorified pre-eminently for His grace-filled operations within our souls; in the third, after the glorification of the "Holy Lord" for the blessing of redemption and reconciliation with the Father, there is contained a series of specific petitions unto Him: "help the orphans, have mercy upon the possessed who are tormented by unclean spirits and cleanse them; teach the unwise; convert those who err; set free those imprisoned in dungeons; and preserve us all." Unto the individual parts of the prayer the people respond in the same manner as in the preceding prayer (**"Thee we praise, Thee we bless..."**). At the conclusion of the prayer:

"the reader reads the prophets and the other (readings); the presbyter, however,

=== LEAF p82 ===
or the deacon reads the Gospel; in conclusion the bishop or presbyter instructs in that which is proper and profitable. Then a prayer takes place and the catechumens receive the imposition of hands (it is understood: the catechumens are dismissed). The bishop instructs the people in that which constituteth the mystery. If, however, there be no bishop, the presbyter speaketh, that the faithful may know unto Whom they approach, and Who is their God and Father."

The monument setteth forth also the very text of this "mystical teaching," "mystagogy," comprising an exposition of the most important truths of doctrine: concerning the two natures in Christ, concerning the mystery of the Incarnation and redemption, concerning the victory of Christ over death and hades (in our present divine worship the Creed correspondeth unto this mystagogy). Unto the mystagogy the people respond: **"Amen."** "When the people have been instructed in the mystagogy, the Eucharist is offered. However, the mystagogy is not always imparted, but only at Pascha, on the Sabbath day, the Lord's day, and on the days of the Epiphany and Pentecost" [^416].
The remark that immediately after the mystagogy the Eucharist is offered demonstrateth that the expounded service is at the same time also the preparatory portion of the Liturgy, the Liturgy of the Catechumens. In it, as we see, there is both the prayer for the catechumens and that reading of Holy Scripture which as early as Justin Martyr formed part of this section of the Liturgy. For this reason, in setting forth in another place, above, the rite of the Eucharist, the *Testamentum* begins this rite directly with the offering of the Gifts and the prayer over them, without touching upon the preparatory portion of the Liturgy. This portion of the Liturgy in the *Testamentum* thus mergeth with Matins.
*(Orig. p. 87)*

#### The Proclamation of the Deacon (Deaconal Matins)

Yet it must be noted that the expounded order of Matins, wherewith the Liturgy of the Catechumens merged, is not complete: inasmuch as it is placed in the section concerning the duties of the bishop, it provideth only the prayers and exclamations of the bishop. In another section of the *Testamentum*, devoted unto the duties of the deacon, there is cited that which is there designated the "Proclamation of the Deacon" (*Proclamatio diaconi*), that is, a series of exclamations which the deacon makes during divine worship. At which divine service the deacon makes them is not stated; it is noted only that a part of them precedeth the prayer of the bishop or presbyter, while a part followeth after it. But these exclamations are distinct from those placed in the rite of the Eucharist and correspond unto the exclamations of the deacon at the Liturgy of the Catechumens in the *Apostolic Constitutions*. Consequently, approximately the same place in the order of divine service must they occupy according unto the present monument as well, that is, they must have entered into the composition of the morning

=== LEAF p83 ===
divine service, which consisted of Matins in the proper sense (the laudation at dawn) and the preparatory portion of the Liturgy. This "Proclamation of the Deacon," which appeareth as it were an independent service (Matins) of the deacon—just as the *Testamentum* hath a distinct episcopal Matins, a distinct presbyteral Matins, and even a distinct "Matins of the widows"—consisteth of 12 preparatory exclamations, 22 petitions, and 1 concluding exclamation. The preparatory exclamations are: **"Let us stand. — Let each know his place. — Let the catechumens depart. — Watch, lest there be anyone impious, lest there be anyone slothful. — Watch, whosoever believeth not, let him depart. — With one accord let us pray. — Let there be no fornicator, let there be no angry person. — Whosoever is a slave of sin, let him depart. — Watch, as children of light let us make entreaty. — Let us pray unto the Lord God, our Savior Jesus Christ."** "Then, when the presbyter or bishop begins the prayer (it is not known which)"—the *Testamentum* continueth immediately—"the people pray and bend their knees. The deacon, however, speaketh thus." There follow 22 petitions in such form: "For such-and-such let us pray, that the Lord...". The 1st petition—concerning heavenly peace (the peace from on high); 2—concerning faith; 3—concerning concord; 4—concerning endurance; 5—concerning the apostles (concerning the granting of power to imitate them); 6—concerning the prophets; 7—concerning the confessors; 8—concerning the bishops; 9—concerning the presbyters; 10—concerning the deacons; 11—concerning the presbyters (again) [^417]; 12—concerning the subdeacons, readers, and deaconesses; 13—concerning the faithful laity; 14—concerning the catechumens; 15—concerning the kingdom; 16—concerning the supreme authorities; 17—concerning the whole world; 18—concerning those who sail and those who travel; 19—concerning the persecuted; 20—concerning the departed; 21—concerning the fallen; 22—concerning all of us. The series of petitions concludeth with a general exclamation: **"let us pray and beseech the Lord, that He may accept our prayers."** "Upon the completion of the exclamations of the deacon,"—the *Testamentum* continueth—"the bishop makes a sign with his hand, and the deacon saith: **"Let us arise in the Holy Spirit, that, having already become wise, we may increase in His Grace, glorifying ourselves in His name, being edified upon the foundation of the apostles, and praying let us pray, that the Lord may graciously *(Orig. p. 88)* accept our prayers."** The bishop then concludeth (makes the dismissal). The people say: **"Amen"**" [^418].

#### The Preparatory Portion of the Liturgy and Its Connection with Matins

Judging from the fact that the proclamation of the deacon dismisses the catechumens from the church, it is to be thought that this proclamation followed after the prayer for the catechumens, the laying on of hands upon them, and their dismissal, all of which took place after the readings and homily [^419] (prior unto the mystagogy). In the *Apostolic Constitutions* the mentioned litany of the *Testamentum* is made already a litany for the faithful, after the expulsion from church of the catechumens, the energumens, and those preparing for illumination [^420]; but in another place in the *Apostolic Constitutions* an analogous litany is placed even after the kiss of peace immediately before the very

=== LEAF p84 ===
offering of the Eucharist [^421]. Other ancient liturgies have the same or an analogous litany. Thus, the Chaldean liturgy divideth such a litany into two parts, placing one part after the Gospel, and the other after the Creed; the Armenian liturgy hath one part of the litany before the reading, and the other after the Creed [^422]; finally, our Liturgy (of the Basilian-Chrysostomic recension) hath placed this litany (in abridged form) at the very beginning of the preparatory portion of the Liturgy, while after the readings it introduced another litany, no less solemn (the Augmented Litany); this was done also by analogy with the Liturgy in our Matins and Vespers.
Nor was the practice of readings identical in the 3rd century at the morning service which merged with the preparatory portion of the Liturgy. As we have seen, the *Testamentum* prescribeth for this service readings from the prophets and "the others," and thereafter the Gospel. The *Apostolic Constitutions* contain already a more complex and definite practice of readings: they indicate two Old Testament readings: from the Law and the Prophets, and two or three New Testament readings from the Acts, the Epistles, and the Gospels [^423]; the Syrian and Chaldean liturgies sometimes replace readings from Acts with readings from the Catholic Epistles; the Armenian hath one prophetic reading before the apostolic epistles; the Coptic and Ethiopic liturgies have no Old Testament readings, but before the Gospel have three readings from the New Testament in the following order: from the Pauline Epistles, the Catholic Epistles, and the Acts [^424]. From the mutual influence and combination of these types of practice there arose our present order, according unto which the Old Testament read-*(Orig. p. 89)*-ings are assigned exclusively unto Vespers, the New Testament ones unto the Liturgy, while Matins, as a service close in time unto the Liturgy, is left without readings, excepting occasionally the Gospel.
It should be noted that, excluding Tertullian, other monuments of the 3rd century, in speaking of the composition of the morning divine service, present the preparatory portion of the Liturgy, which in the *Testamentum* merged with Matins, in approximately the same form as the *Testamentum*. Thus, the Arabic *Didascalia* (that is, the Arabic translation of the *Testamentum* for the Coptic Church) speaketh of the preparatory portion of the Liturgy in the following two passages:

"the place for baptism ought to be at the side of the church, so that the catechumens who are in it may have the opportunity to listen unto the pericopes and spiritual hymns and psalms which are recited in the church" [^425].

The bishop
"begins to celebrate the liturgy according unto the order and precisely thus. First he offers thanksgiving, then, when all the people are seated, he makes for them an explanation of the word, that is, of the sacred books, and instructs them unto repentance (conversion), as is proper, and in the path

=== LEAF p85 ===
of honesty. Then let there be uttered psalmody from the book of psalms, from their poets of maxims, from the wise men of grace; and all the people with understanding and fear listen unto this, and with a contrite heart follow this. Then the presbyters bring the bread and the cup of the Eucharist" [^426].

The preparatory portion of the Liturgy is presumably had in view likewise by Tertullian when he speaketh concerning a certain Montanist woman, that she "according as the scriptures are read, the psalms are sung, discourses are delivered, and prayers are offered up, receiveth ever new material for visions" [^427].

Making now a general survey of the morning divine service according unto the *Testamentum* and comparing this service with the account of Tertullian, we obtain the following picture. According unto the *Testamentum*, Matins consisteth of five principal parts: 1) two prayers of the bishop, 2) Old Testament psalms and canticles between them, 3) readings from Holy Scripture, 4) two homilies: one for all, including the catechumens, and one for the faithful alone, 5) solemn prayers between these homilies: one over the catechumens with the imposition of hands and their dismissal, and one for the faithful with the exclamations of the deacon and genuflection. This is consequently already a very complex rite, more complex even than Matins possesseth in certain monuments of the 4th century; yet this complexity oweth much unto the circumstance that the Liturgy was directly conjoined unto Matins, and its first portion merged therewith. Not everywhere even in the 3rd century did such a merger take place, and in the 4th and especially in the 5th century the majority of liturgies already possess a preparatory portion, the Liturgy of the Catechumens; consequently, this merger was a local phenomenon of that Church wherein the *Testamentum* arose (the Syrian), and perchance of certain others, and at all events a temporary phenomenon. Yet it is important as witness that into Matins certain elements began to be introduced from the Liturgy (as likewise into Vespers, owing unto the former connection of the Liturgy with the Agapes), for instance, prayers *(Orig. p. 90)* for the catechumens. — In the account of Tertullian the morning divine service appeareth shorter, but of the parts indicated by the *Testamentum* it lacketh only the canticles, which were perchance omitted by chance. The "judgment" whereof Tertullian speaketh involuntarily recalleth the exclamations of the deacon: **"let there be no fornicator..."**. These exclamations are presumably a ritual survival of the former excommunication of grave sinners, under the form of removal from the solemn worship assemblies. Thus, in the Matins of the Syrian Church of the 3rd century there was already given the germ both of the present prayers during the Six Psalms and at the end of Matins (the prayer of the bowing of heads), and of the present Six Psalms (psalms), and of the Great Litany, and of the canon (the canticles of Moses and the prophets), and of the Gospel.

#### Presbyteral or Daily Matins according to the Testamentum Domini

The presented order of the morning divine service according unto the *Testamentum* is properly its episcopal order. Apparently, it was celebrated only on feast days, since the *Testamentum* provideth also another order of this divine service, entitled: "Daily Doxology" (*collaudatio quotidiana*) and intended for celebration by a presbyter alone. This Matins could be performed even without the people, as is evident from the following preliminary remark unto it: "The daily doxology the presbyters recite in church, by themselves at the prescribed time." This daily doxology, that is, daily or priestly Matins, representeth an abridgment and modification of the first, solemn, festive episcopal Matins. It consisteth only of a single three-part prayer with an introductory exclamation: **"The grace of our Lord be with you all."** The people: **"And with thy spirit."** The priest: **"Praise ye the Lord."** The people: **"It is meet and right."** The priest reads a prayer addressed exclusively unto God the Father, the first part whereof containeth glorification for the general benefits of God and especially spiritual ones granted by redemption, the second—for redemption, and the last—for the specific benefits of God unto the suffering and unto the Church. The prayer is twice as short as the episcopal one, but coincideth with it at times in expressions. Unto each of its three sections the people respond with the same exclamations as at episcopal Matins. Therewith the divine service concludeth, but a remark is added: "if, however, anyone speaketh prophetic words (subjoineth a reading from the prophets), he receives a reward" [^428]. In this order of the morning divine service there is for the first time given the type of a daily service in distinction from a festal one. At the same time, this order is close unto the later type of the Hours and services homogeneous with them (the Midnight Office and Compline), consisting of a single prayer and a single reading, that too non-obligatory and Old Testament.

#### The Midnight Office and Matins of the Widows according to the Testamentum Domini

In assigning for every order of the clergy its own service, more specifically—its own Matins, the *Testamentum* provideth a special order of such Matins also for the widows, the chief *(Orig. p. 91)* representatives ("female presbyters," *presbyterae*) of female ecclesiastical ministry, and at the same time of the asceticism of that Church wherein this monument arose. The widow was here appointed unto this dignity by a special prayer of the bishop; in the hierarchical ladder she stands below the deacon and above the subdeacon and reader; she was bound unto continual prayerful solitude and was required to celebrate, especially on feast days, special services *at night* and *in the morning*, at dawn. These services the widows performed singly; but, remarketh the *Testamentum*, if the widow hath like-minded companions—virgins, it will be very good if they with her

=== LEAF p87 ===
shall pray, so as to respond: **"Amen."** The service the widow could perform either at home or in church, but during the period of purification she was not permitted to approach the altar. Both services of the widows consist of a single prayer. In the "nocturnal doxology" the widow at first glorifieth the holy, blameless God of the forefathers, prophets, and apostles with all her soul, strength, and heart for His care over the poor and the weak and for that He hath deigned to make her His servant, and entreateth Him to strengthen her heart for perfection in the Holy Spirit and to restore unto them, the widows, youth for the edification of the Church; turning then unto the Son, the Word and Mind of the Father, and confessing all that He hath accomplished for our salvation, the widow entreateth Him to help the widows, to guide their thoughts in steadfast faith. The "morning doxology" is intended (by its heading) already "For the widows who enjoy the presidency at assemblies." The doxology is addressed unto God the Father, and after general glorification of His Providence and mercy includeth a prayer that He would perfect in His handmaid the spirit of counsel, piety, and right-mindedness, after which there followeth praise unto God Who hath removed from "our" (the widows') poverty all turmoil, discord, evil custom, doubt, fear, and weakness; praise unto God Who hath changed the manner of thought of the widows, correcting their thoughts for upright service unto Him [^429]. These "widows'" services recall our present cell "rules" by the simplicity and uniformity of their content. It is characteristic that they are prescribed only for female ascetics, and not for men. The *Testamentum*, as a monument which arose in Syria, can speak only concerning the practice of that Church. Yet if such complex rites of Matins existed in the 3rd century in one Church, then they were possible in any other as well.

#### Hours and Days of the Celebration of the Eucharist

The enumerated monuments of divine worship clearly demonstrate that at least by the end of the 3rd century the Eucharist was definitively transferred unto the morning, following immediately upon the morning divine service, which began with dawn. Such was the case not only in the homeland of the *Testamentum*—in Syria—but judging by the testimony of Tertullian in other Churches as well: "the sacrament of the Eucharist,"—saith he—"we receive in pre-dawn assemblies and from no other hands than the hands of the president" [^430]. It is possible *(Orig. p. 92)* that in certain places the former practice continued to hold sway—the evening celebration of the Eucharist; concerning such practice there is testimony not only from the first half of the 3rd century (Clement of Alexandria) [^431], but also from the second (Cyprian of Carthage [^432], the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* [^433]),—yet by a strange coincidence these testimonies concern only Egypt, wherein the custom of the evening celebration of the Eucharist existed even down unto the times of the historian Socrates (5th cent.) [^434].
And with respect unto the days on which the Eucharist was celebrated, there was no

=== LEAF p88 ===
complete uniformity across the Churches. The *Testamentum* makes it rather clear that the Eucharist was celebrated only on Saturdays, Sundays, and feast days, whereof there were three: Pascha, Pentecost, and the Epiphany [^435]. Unto these days, as we shall see subsequently, must be added also the commemorations of (local) martyrs. Such, consequently, was the situation in Syria in the second half of the 3rd century. But in Rome and Africa, apparently, daily celebration of the Eucharist took place. Tertullian, in explaining the Lord's Prayer, understandeth under the daily bread the Body of Christ, and remarketh: "Therefore, in asking for daily bread we ask for perpetuity (*perpetuitatem*) in Christ and indivisibility from His Body" [^436]. From this one may infer a daily celebration of the Eucharist, especially if one compareth these words with the interpretation of the 4th petition of the Lord's Prayer in St. Cyprian: "we ask that this bread be given unto us daily, so that we who are in Christ and daily receive from Him the Eucharist as the food of salvation, may not be deprived of heavenly bread and separated from Christ's Body, if we should be barred from communion upon the commission of some very grave sin" [^437]. Origen saith: "Christians eat each day the flesh of the Lamb, that is, the Flesh of the Word of God, they listen unto the scriptures, each day they receive Pascha, sacrificed for us, that is, Christ" [^438].

#### The Presanctified Gifts

Yet it is possible that in all these testimonies the discourse is not concerning the daily *celebration* of the Eucharist, but only concerning daily *communion*. From Tertullian we learn that there already existed at that time the custom of taking the Eucharistic gifts home for daily partaking of them. Tertullian presenteth unto a Christian woman also such an argument against marriage with a pagan: "will not (thy husband) notice that thou tastest of something secretly before supper? And when he knoweth that it is none other than bread, what will he think regarding thee in his ignorance?" [^439] St. Cyprian relateth that a woman who "attempted with unworthy (according unto other MSS.—'unclean') hands to open her coffer (*arcam suam*), wherein was preserved the holy *(Orig. p. 93)* thing of the Lord (*Domini sanctum*), was scorched by fire issuing forth therefrom" [^440]. It is possible that the reservation of the Gifts was occasioned at times by the desire not to break the fast by partaking of them, concerning which apprehension, as we shall see, Tertullian makes mention [^441]. In view of this very thing, presumably, there began to be employed in certain places the custom denounced by St. Cyprian of using at the morning Eucharist water instead of wine [^442]. All this prepared the idea of the Presanctified Liturgy.

#### The Hours of Prayer according to Writers of the 3rd Century

The morning divine service and the agape, with the addition unto one or

=== LEAF p89 ===
the other of the Eucharist (the Liturgy), were, as we see, in the 3rd century, as in the 2nd and the 1st, not only the principal church services, but also the sole public services. And they did not exclude the existence of special private services performed by the clergy either alone, or with the participation of a few zealous believers, both in church and at home. The apostolic custom of sanctifying by prayer the three most significant and important hours during the course of the day was maintained, as we have seen, in the 2nd century as well, and passed into the 3rd century. In the 2nd century, this custom is witnessed only by the *Didache* (*Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*), without indicating which precise hours were sanctified by prayer. The testimonies of the 3rd century upon this question are both more frequent and more definite. It is evident that the custom of sanctifying by prayer certain hours of the day is now increasingly spreading and becoming established.

For *Clement of Alexandria*, the hours of prayer are a firmly established and widespread custom.

"If some assign (*taktai aponemousi*, τακταὶ ἀπονέμουσι) for prayer definite hours, as for example the 3rd, 6th, and 9th, the man endowed with knowledge (*ho gnōstikos*, ὁ γνωστικὸς) prayeth his whole life, striving by prayer to abide with God. He hath left behind, in short, all useless things, having attained unto that place where he hath received perfection, as one acting according unto love. Yet even the setting apart of three hours for honoring with equal prayers (*tais isais euchais tetimēmenas*, ταῖς ἴσαις εὐχαῖς τεтиμημένας) is universally recognized in view of the three blessed states of the saints" [^443].

Thus, upon the hours of prayer Clement looketh as upon a good means toward moral advancement, non-obligatory only for one who hath attained perfection and is capable of unceasing prayer. In another passage Clement speaketh of the 6th hour of the day as one wherein "there was manifested (*emphainei*, ἐμφαίνει) something of the economy of salvation—namely, man was made complete (*eteleiōthē*, ἐτελειώθη)" (there is meant, presumably, redemption [^444]).
*(Orig. p. 94)*
*Tertullian* speaketh concerning the hours of prayer in approximately the same spirit:

"As regards the time of prayer, the external observance even of certain hours will not be superfluous, those, I mean, generally known hours which mark out the intervals of the day: the 3rd, 6th, and 9th, which also in Scripture may be found indicated as more solemn hours (*solemniores*);"

=== LEAF p90 ===
having referred unto the descent of the Holy Spirit at the 3rd hour, and unto the prayers of the Apostles Peter and John at the 6th and 9th hours, Tertullian continueth:

"although the apostles acted simply, without any intention of giving a rule for observance, nevertheless it will be good to establish a certain presumption (*praesumptionem*), which both confirmeth the admonition unto prayer and from time unto time, like a certain law, teareth us away from mundane affairs for the fulfillment of this duty,—namely, that we should pray daily not less than three times, as worshipers of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit,—which, as we read, was observed also by Daniel in accordance, of course, with the customs of Israel. We speak not of the prescribed (*legitimae*) prayers, which without any special reminder (*sine ulla admonitione*) ought to be performed at the onset of day and night" [^445].

In another passage Tertullian, having already become a Montanist, in defending the fasts of this sect against the reproaches of the Orthodox regarding novelty and severity, compareth the hours of prayer with the fasts:

"If in the same commentary (the Acts) of Luke there is indicated the third hour of prayer, at which those filled with the Holy Spirit were taken to be drunken, and the sixth, at which Peter went up into the upper chamber, and the 9th, at which they went into the temple, why do we not understand this not in such wise that one must pray indiscriminately always and everywhere and at every time, but in such wise that these were hours both more significant (*insigniores*) in human affairs, dividing the day, distributing labor, and having their effect in civic life, and more solemn in divine prayers, which is proved also by the example of Daniel, who prayed thrice a day, of course with the setting apart (for prayer) of certain hours, and none other than those which became thence significant also for the apostles, that is, the 3rd, 6th, and 9th?" [^446]

There is in Tertullian mention also of nocturnal prayers: in demonstrating unto a Christian woman the inconvenience for her of a pagan marriage, Tertullian speaketh of a pagan husband: "will he willingly let thee go unto nocturnal assemblies (*convocationibus*), if that should be required?" [^447]
