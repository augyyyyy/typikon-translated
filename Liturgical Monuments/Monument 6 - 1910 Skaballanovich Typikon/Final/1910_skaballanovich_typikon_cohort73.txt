=== LEAF p721 ===
certain of their manuscript copies (for example, in the Cryptaferrata MS *Falascae* of the 13th c.) also designate these words as a prayer of the priest following the words *(Orig. p. 602)* **"Especially"** [**"It is truly meet"** / **"All of creation rejoices in thee"**] (instead of **"All of creation rejoices in thee"** or **"It is truly meet"**, or alongside the second during its singing); at the Liturgy of the Apostle Peter (in the Rossano MS of the 11th c. only) these words are likewise set as a prayer of the priest before **"Among the first remember"**, herein with the addition following **"Full of grace"** of **"Mary"**, which is not found in the above-mentioned MSS [^2356]. — As regards the troparion at Vespers, in the Horologion of the Sinai Library No. 863 at the end of Vespers no troparion is appointed. According unto Western Studite typika at Vespers there was also, apparently, no troparion. So too at Coptic Vespers. According unto the Alexis-Studite Typikon, at Saturday-Sunday Vespers the appointed troparia were: **"Baptist of Christ, and the rest"**, replaced for a celebrated saint by his troparion. So too at Ethiopian Vespers, besides a troparion analogous unto **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"**, there are appointed hymns similar unto **"Baptist of Christ"**, **"Pray for us"**, and **"Beneath Thy Compassion"**. The Evergetis Typikon: "a dismissal troparion, and if the saint hath none, the Sunday troparion of the tone" [^2357].

Something analogous unto our Theotokion troparion is presented by the Biblical Canticle of the Theotokos [the *Magnificat*] at Roman Catholic Vespers. The troparion corresponding unto **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** there enters into the composition of the usual beginning, and furthermore, preceded by four verses with responsories and with a special prayer following it, is pronounced thrice at dawn, at noon, and in the evening; such a private devotion, called the *Angelus*, arose in the 12th century, at first for the evening [^2358], probably under the influence of the opinion that the Annunciation took place in the evening. The text of the prayer in the Ethiopian Horologion hath the following variant reading from ours: "Rejoice, O Virgin, who hast found grace, Holy Mary"; among the Roman Catholics: "Hail (Lat. *Ave*, Polish *Zdrowaś*), Mary, full of grace — [the fruit] of thy womb Jesus. Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray for us sinners, now and at the hour of our death" [^2359].

## Manner of Execution

The troparion **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** [*Bogoroditse Devo*] is set in Tone 4, the usual tone for the troparia of feasts. In view of the solemnity of the moment, the troparion is sung thrice, which is otherwise appointed only on the Twelve Great Feasts and feasts similar unto them. The censing of the loaves performed during the singing of the troparion also contributes unto this solemnity, having, however, as its immediate purpose the sanctification of the loaves.

### According unto Ancient Typika

=== LEAF p722 ===
Ancient MSS of the Jerusalem Typikon concerning the execution of the troparion: the Georgian MS: "and we [sing] the troparion Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos, 3 times"; Greek Sevastyanov and No. 381: "and we [sing] the dismissal [troparion], Tone 4 (No. 381: Tone 5), Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos, saying it thrice." The Order [*Diataxis*] of Patriarch Philotheus: "when the final dismissal [troparion] is sung aloud and slowly (*argōs* [Gr. ἀργῶς]), *(Orig. p. 603)* the deacon — censes." The remaining typika and *Sluzhebniki* are as the present one, or close to it. The present Greek *Euchologion* before the blessing of loaves appoints the singing of the dismissal troparion of the day and the Theotokion. According unto the Constantinopolitan *Hieratikon* [Gr. Ἱεраτικόν], the priest, censing the loaves, having before him the deacon with a lamp, sings **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"**, and the left choir sings the conclusion of the troparion: **"For Thou hast borne the Savior of our souls..."** [*Yako Spasa rodila yesi...*]; such a distinctive singing of this troparion according unto the Constantinopolitan Typikon is explained by the fact that besides it, at the end of Vespers the dismissal troparion is sung (the blessing of loaves and **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** take place before the aposticha stichera) [^2360].

## Curtailment and Omission

**"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** [*Bogoroditse Devo*] at Great Vespers is sung thrice only on those Sundays with which no other great commemoration is combined; in these latter cases, however (for example, on the Sundays of the Fathers, certain Triodion Sundays, on forefeasts, afterfeasts, and leavetakings—not all, however), **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** is sung twice and the troparion of the commemoration or saint once (on the forefeasts of the Transfiguration, the Entry into the Temple, the Annunciation, and the leavetakings of the latter two, however, **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** is sung thrice). At a great concurrence of commemorations with Sunday, such as: on the 7th Sunday after Pascha, on the Sunday of the Holy Fathers [Genealogy / Ancestors of God] (if, however, it falleth not on December 31), if December 24 falleth on a Sunday, or March 25 on the 3rd Sunday of Great Lent, — **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** is not sung at all.

# THE BLESSING OF LOAVES (*Blagoslovenie khlebov* — *Artoklasia*)

Having replaced the Liturgy and the agape meal, Vespers must provide the worshippers also with sustenance, even such as the sanctity of the temple permits. This is accomplished in the form of offering unto the worshippers blessed bread and wine, together wherewith there are blessed (as it were for the morrow's meal) oil and wheat. Unto this end, while the litiya is still being celebrated, there is prepared ("having been previously prepared" — *proeutrepisthentos* [Gr. προευτρεπισθέντος]) a "four-legged stand" (*tetrapodion* [Gr. τετραπόδιον]) [^2361], or, as it is subsequently termed, a "table", a small table, upon both sides of which the litiya candlesticks are placed; upon the small table is placed a dish (*diskos* [Gr. δίσκος]) with wheat (*sitos* [Gr. σῖτος]) and five (according unto the Gospel number) "loaves, whereof (Gr. ὧν; in the Slavonic *yezhe* refers not unto "dish", but signifies generally "which") we have the custom to make offerings unto the church", that is, with prosphora; upon both sides of the dish are two vessels (*angeia* [Gr. ἀγγεῖα]), unto the left (naturally, from the perspective of the worshippers [^2362], in accordance with which the icons also are arranged in the temple) with wine, and unto the right with oil; judging by the fact that in the prayer of blessing *(Orig. p. 604)* wine is named first, it is placed on the left only in order that, when pointing in this prayer unto the loaves, wheat, wine, and oil, a natural cruciform motion may be made with the right hand [^2363]. The deacon during the singing of the troparion, having taken a blessing from the priest, censes round about the table—wherewith the consecration of objects is frequently accompanied—and in view of such a specific purpose of this censing, besides the table he censes only the superior standing in his place (as one spiritually participating in the sacred ministration) and the priest (who performs the consecration), and thereafter once more the table ("the loaves") from the front only. The superior or, as is customarily done, the priest pronounces the prayer (understood to be following the preliminary "Let us pray to the Lord", "Lord, have mercy") "with a loud voice" [*veleglasno*], as hitherto only a single prayer has been pronounced—the final litiya prayer. The prayer beseeches the Savior, Who showed in the miracle of feeding the five thousand all the power of His blessing, for the same blessing and (wonder-working) multiplication of the proffered substances, as well as for the sanctification of those who partake of them (= replacement of the Eucharist). Before the words of the prayer: **"Do Thou Thyself bless"**, the priest makes the sign of the cross over the substances, yet not with the hand alone, but with one of the loaves, signifying the heavenly Bread, "whereby [also] he shows that Christ accomplished the same, having taken in like manner into His hands five loaves" [^2364]; at the enumeration of the substances the priest points unto them with his hand, whereby the form of a cross is likewise produced. The prayer concludes with the glorification of those divine attributes through the manifestation of

=== LEAF p724 ===
which the expected sanctification serves (among others, "All-holy, Good, and Life-creating...").

# "BLESSED BE THE NAME OF THE LORD" (*Budi imya Gospodne blagoslovenno*)

In correspondence with the Liturgy, out of which it was born, Great Vespers concludes with a threefold prayer-sequence, consisting of a psalm-verse, a psalm, and a priestly blessing, having one common theme—blessing. The verse **"Blessed be the name of the Lord..."** from Psalm 112:3 [LXX; Masoretic Ps. 113:2] (cf. Job 1:21) corresponds unto the blessing of the name of God at the beginning of each service and reminds that with the end of the day the glorification of God does not come to an end, but will continue for ever, most immediately throughout the upcoming night (for which reason at Vespers without a vigil this verse is not appointed). Thus, together with the following *(Orig. p. 606)* Psalm 33, **"I will bless the Lord at all times"** [LXX; Masoretic Ps. 34], this verse constitutes a very skillful transition unto Matins. Containing David's ecstatic thanksgiving unto God for deliverance from imminent peril (under one of the circumstances of Saul's persecution), Psalm 33 depicts all the blessings of complete trust in God, inviting one directly to taste of this goodness in order to become convinced of it, and especially by this expression is suited unto the present moment. Of the psalm, which at the Liturgy is appointed in its complete form, here only half is pronounced (of the 22 verses—11, through "shall not lack any good thing"), the more poetic portion of the psalm, which thereafter passes into moral instruction. Moreover, verse 11 [LXX 33:11 / Masoretic 34:10], wherewith the psalm here concludes: **"The rich have become poor and suffered hunger..."**, is suited unto the upcoming meal. Unto our blessing of God in the verse **"Blessed be the name"** and in Psalm 33, God answers with His blessing through the priest. This blessing, so natural and necessary at the end of the service, is imparted not by the usual formula "Peace be unto all", but with that fullness (abundance) and definitiveness as at the Liturgy: "The blessing of the Lord be upon you, through His grace (not a general blessing solely according to love for mankind, but grace-filled through the power of redemption and the mysteries) and love for mankind, always, (more specifically:) now and ever...".

## History

The "blessing" of the Lord, corresponding unto the verse **"Blessed be the name of the Lord"**, was at the end of almost all Liturgies. Thus, at the Coptic Liturgy of Basil the Great of the 2nd redaction, immediately before communion the priest says: "Blessed be the Lord unto the ages, amen"; according unto the 1st redaction: "Blessed be the Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, and the Holy Spirit, amen"; at the Liturgy of the Apostle Peter following communion: "Blessed is our God always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages"; according unto the most ancient

=== LEAF p728 ===
copy of the Liturgy of the Apostle James (*Messanensis* of the 10th c.), before the communion of the laity: "Blessed be the name of the Lord our God." According unto later copies of the same Liturgy (*Rossanensis* of the 11th c. and later), the verse **"Blessed be the name"** is already appointed. It is likewise appointed at the end of the Liturgy of the Apostle Mark, of the Roman [Mass] at a hierarchical service (entering here into the composition of the solemn final blessing of the people), at the beginning of the Ambrosian (Milanese) and Lyonese rites, in the blessing of the people by a newly consecrated Pope [^2367], and in Protestant worship (see below, Protestant Vespers). — Psalm 33 is appointed in the Liturgy of the *Apostolic Constitutions* [^2368] during communion, and its 9th verse (**"O taste and see..."**) is the Communion Hymn at the Liturgy of the Apostle James. — The blessing of the priest at the end of the service was always considered an indispensable attribute of the service. In the 4th–5th centuries in various places such a blessing was imparted through the laying on of hands by the bishop (Introductory Chapter, pp. 136, 141, 142), whereunto all bowed their heads, and the bish*(Orig. p. 607)*op read a special prayer corresponding unto the present prayers of the bowing of heads. With the increase of worshippers in temples, the individual blessing of each person had to be replaced by a general blessing of all. In the West it was considered no less, if not more, an indispensable attribute of every service, and especially of Vespers, than in the East. The Council of Agde (*Concilium Agathense*) of 506, Canon 30, requires "that the people, upon the completion of the prayer towards evening, be dismissed by the bishop with a blessing." In the *Life of Caesarius, Bishop of Arles* († 542), it is related that "it was established that at a known time four men should go forth to meet him; with them he proceeded unto Vespers in the basilica of St. Stephen, since upon the conclusion of Vespers he gave a blessing unto the people" [^2369]. The blessing at the end of services was preserved even by Protestants of all confessions and sects, not only Anglicans, but also Lutherans, Reformed, Presbyterians, Irvingites, Anabaptists, and Moravian Brethren [Herrnhuters].

## Manner of Execution

This tripartite concluding prayer-sequence of Vespers is performed with the following particulars. **"Blessed be the name of the Lord"** is sung ("we sing") in Tone 4, the usual tone of festal prokeimena, corresponding moreover unto the tone of the dismissal troparion for Sunday. As a prokeimenon, for which purpose it as it were serves with respect to Psalm 33 (corresponding unto the antiphon in Western worship), this verse is sung thrice, naturally alternately by the choirs. "Then Psalm 33, **'I will bless the Lord at all times'**, and we speak it unto 'shall not lack any good thing'" — an indefinite expression, which can be understood both in the sense of the singing of

=== LEAF p729 ===
the psalm, and in the sense of its reading: singing is required both by the solemnity of the moment and by the analogy of all the other psalms of Vespers, which are all required to be sung (ordinarily a psalm is read). "And the priest, having gone, stands before the Royal Doors, looking towards the west", unto the people—for the purpose of imparting the blessing unto them. "And upon the completion of the psalm he says: The blessing of the Lord be upon you... And the reader [says]—amen." "Amen" is appointed to be pronounced by the reader, since immediately thereafter the reading (from the Apostle) is appointed (where the latter is omitted, there the amen is sung).

### According unto Ancient Typika

The Georgian MS:
"**'I will bless the Lord...'** unto 'shall not lack'; the priest stands before the holy doors, turns, and says: 'The blessing of the Lord be upon you...' and immediately follows the reading."

## According unto the Present Constantinopolitan Order

Following the dismissal troparion — the deacon: "Let us pray to the Lord", the choir: "Lord, have mercy"; the hierarch or priest from the solea blesses the people, exclaiming: "The blessing of the Lord and His mercy come upon you, through His grace and love for mankind, always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages" [^2371].

In the present Greek *Euchologion* the same — yet without "Let us pray to the Lord", "Lord, have mercy" [^2372].
