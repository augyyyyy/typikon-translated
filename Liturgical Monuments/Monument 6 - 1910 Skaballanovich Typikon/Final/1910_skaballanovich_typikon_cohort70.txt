## Who Recites the Augmented Litany at Vespers

The Augmented Litany at Vespers, unlike the Great Litany, the Typikon directs to be recited by the deacon, both in view of the greater solemnity of this part of Vespers, and because the priest hath already entered the altar to stand before the Holy Table and offer our prayers there, whereas litanies are recited upon the ambo.

In this requirement of the Typikon all ancient manuscripts thereof agree, although the majority of them, as we have seen, give the same direction also regarding the Great Litany.

# "Vouchsafe, O Lord" (*Spodobi, Gospodi* — *Kataxioson, Kyrie*)

Following the prayer for the faithful, which the Augmented Litany represents, at Vespers there follows a prayer for needs, and first for the immediate ones prompted by the approaching evening. A prayer of this kind is divided into two prayers: a simple [lay] one and a sacerdotal one—**"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Spodobi, Gospodi*) and the Litany of Supplication.

**"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Kataxioson, Kyrie* [Gr. Καταξίωσον, Κύριε]) is rather a hymn than a prayer; by its prayerful-laudatory content **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** cannot strictly be called either a prayer or a hymn, for which reason it is always named solely by its opening words. By its content and its place at Vespers, this hymnographic prayer resembles the Great Doxology at Matins, with which it literally coincides also in the majority of its individual expressions. At daily Matins it is appended in the same form as at Vespers unto the correspondingly modified and abridged Great Doxology. Like the latter, it is composed of biblical expressions verbatim or with slight necessary modifications. In comparison with the Doxology, it is of a more suppliant and not so festive a character.

In **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**, the prayer for a sinless evening, which we entreat on account of the blessed Name so glorified by ancient righteous men (**"Blessed art Thou, O Lord, God of our fathers"**—from the Song of the Three Holy Children: Dan. 3:26), so as not to disrupt the eternal harmony of this praise—this prayer, naturally the first for a Christian at the arrival of evening, transitions into a more general prayer for the mercy of God in accordance with our hope (**"Let Thy mercy, O Lord, be upon us..."**—Ps. 32:22 [LXX]). Let this mercy be expressed first of all in the arduous instruction in that which can justify man before God. The sentence of Ps. 118:12 [LXX]: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord..."**, as containing this fundamental petition, is repeated thrice, yet not verbatim as at the Great Doxology, but with modifications in the text adapted unto the Persons of the Holy Trinity, thereby supplying that which is lacking in **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** in comparison with the morning Doxology: an address by Name unto the Persons of the Holy Trinity and Their glorification. The particular prayer for instruction in the will of God returns once more unto the general prayer for the mercy of God, a prayer already expressed in a stronger form, with even greater assurance: **"O Lord, Thy mercy endureth for ever..."** (Ps. 137:8 [LXX]), an expression which, like the subsequent concluding doxology, hath nothing in common with the Great Doxology. The conclusion of the prayer **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (**"Unto Thee belongeth

=== LEAF p694 ===
praise..."**) resembles the ecphonesis of the Great Litany and represents, like the prayerful beginning, not a borrowing from Holy Scripture, but a brief hymn of praise unto the Holy Trinity, composed independently by Christians and tripartite in Her honor.

## History of the Prayer

In the *Apostolic Constitutions*, alongside the morning doxology, almost literally identical with the first part of the present Great *(Orig. p. 580)* Doxology, there is cited with the superscription (in later redactions): "Evening Prayer", the following prayer-hymn:

"Praise the Lord, ye servants, praise the Name of the Lord. We praise Thee, we sing unto Thee, we bless Thee for Thy great glory, O Lord King, Father of Christ, the Lamb without blemish, Who taketh away the sin of the world. Unto Thee belongeth praise, unto Thee belongeth song, unto Thee belongeth glory: unto God and the Father through the Son in the All-Holy Spirit, unto ages of ages. Amen" [^2287].

This hymn, therefore, constitutes the foundation and primitive form of **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**. Remarkable is its strict analogy with the morning doxology: only the angelic New Testament praise at the beginning is replaced by psalmody (Ps. 112:1 [LXX]). Immediately following this prayer, the *Apostolic Constitutions* cite the Canticle of Simeon the God-receiver [*Nunc Dimittis*]. However, in the very order of Vespers provided by the *Apostolic Constitutions*, this prayer, as also **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"**, is not indicated—among other reasons (see Introductory Chapter, p. 137), perhaps also because this prayer had not yet received widespread dissemination. The Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople likewise did not know it, not only in its ancient redactions, but also in the Chanted Office [*asmatike akolouthia*] of the 15th–16th centuries (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 339, 342). Yet the eastern type of daily services, having borrowed it, evidently from the Syrian Church (the homeland of the *Apostolic Constitutions*), already knew it in the 6th century: in the account of the visit of Sophronius and John Moschus unto Nilus of Sinai, **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** at Vespers occupies its present place, as it doth also in the Holy Sepulchre Typikon of 1112 and in the most ancient manuscripts of the Studite Typikon (ibid., pp. 295, 355).

In the Coptic Horologion, where this prayer is placed not in the order of Vespers, but in an appendix unto the Horologion among the seven night and day prayers, and in the 14th-century Ethiopic Horologion for the Coptic Church, where it constitutes only the 2nd part of the morning doxology, it

=== LEAF p695 ===
possesses a greater volume than with us, and the following differences from our redaction:

after **"Let Thy mercy, O Lord..."**: **"For the eyes of all look with hope unto Thee... in due season. Hear us, O God... of the earth. And Thou, O Lord, shalt preserve us and keep us from this evil generation unto ages. Amen. Blessed art Thou, O Lord, teach me Thy statutes.** (Ethiopic: + **Blessed art Thou, O Lord, make me to understand Thy judgments). Blessed art Thou, O Lord, enlighten me with Thy righteousness. O Lord, Thy mercy... forsake not... Lord, Thou hast been our refuge from generation unto generation...** (and further as with us in the festal Great Doxology down to:) **unto them that know Thee and Thy righteousness unto the upright in heart** (Ethiopic: + **O Lord, Thy mercy**—a second time). **Unto Thee belongeth holiness** (Ethiopic: **praise**), **Unto Thee belongeth song, unto Thee belongeth glory** (Ethiopic: **holiness**)..." [^2288].

## Manner of Performance

Occupying at Vespers a position analogous unto the Great Doxology at Matins, **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** would seem to require also the same manner of performance, that is, chanting. Yet in accordance with the lesser solemnity of Vespers in comparison *(Orig. p. 581)* with Matins, this prayerful hymn is not sung here, but read; in view of its importance, however, the Typikon entrusts its reading unto the superior, assigning it unto the number of the ten most important prayers read by the superior (see Chapter 7 of the Typikon, "Note" [*zri*]), although it permitteth its reading also unto a reader (in this 2nd chapter, whereas in Chapter 7 no such permission is given).

The reading of **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**, notwithstanding its hymnic-laudatory character and analogy with the Great Doxology, is an echo of the fact that for this latter doxology also in the Studite Typikon there was appointed chanting close unto reading (see Introductory Chapter, p. 368; see below, "The Great Doxology").—Manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon speak thus regarding the manner of performing **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**: 13th-century Georgian—"the people: Vouchsafe, O Lord"; the most ancient Greek (Moscow Rumyantsev Museum, Sevastyanov collection No. 491/35 of the 13th c.)—"we: Vouchsafe, O Lord"; later Greek (Moscow Synodal Library No. 381 of the 14th c.): "the appointed monk"; Slavic (for example, Kyiv Museum No. Aa 194 of the 16th c.)—"we say".

# The Litany of Supplication (*Prositel'naya Ekteniya*)

A supplement unto the prayer **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** is the following litany, wherein the petitions of this prayer are both expanded and intensified by being offered through the sacred ministers. This litany is called in popular parlance the "Litany of Supplication" [*prositel'naya*], and in liturgical books by its first word the Litany of "Let us complete" (*Plērōsōmen* [Gr. Πληρώσωμεν]). Such an opening indicates that it is concluding in the service. As remarked above, it serves as an addition unto the Augmented Litany in the respect that the latter contains petitions for persons, whereas this one is for the objects needed by those persons. And just as the petitions of the Great Litany concerning persons receive further development in the Augmented Litany, although concentrating upon the faithful alone, so also the brief petitions of the Great Litany concerning human needs here become more detailed and at the same time are elevated in their content, namely, being restricted unto the realm of spiritual needs alone. And by its structure the Litany of Supplication begins where the Great Litany concludes ("Help us, save us"). Beginning its supplication from that petition with which the Great and Little Litanies conclude, the Litany of Supplication divides our needs into six classes, speaking first of immediate needs (a sinless evening, morning, or day), and thereafter of enduring and ever more important ones: it entreats a guardian of sinlessness—an Angel [^2289], the conditions for further sinlessness—pardon of past sins [^2290], other favorable conditions for righteousness—such as *(Orig. p. 582)* the present evening, and of the entire evening ("the remaining time") of our life, and its sunset—our end [^2291]; after the recourse unto the intercession of the saints, common with the Great Litany, the litany points out the pledge of the fulfillment of prayer in the goodness and love for mankind of God (cf. above, p. 569).

## History of the Litany

Deaconal proclamations almost literally coinciding with our Litany of Supplication are found in the order of Vespers and Matins provided by the *Apostolic Constitutions*, and at the present place in those services. Specifically, following the introductory psalm, the prayers for the catechumens, energumens, candidates for illumination, and penitents, and after their dismissal from the temple at Vespers and Matins, according unto this 4th-century monument (see Introductory Chapter, p. 136), there was appointed a prayer for the faithful with proclamations of the deacon close unto our

"Save and raise us up [^2292], O God, through Thy Christ (*dia Christou* [Gr. διὰ Χριστοῦ]). Having arisen, let us entreat the mercies of the Lord and His compassions; an Angel of peace [^2293], good and profitable things, a Christian end, a peaceful and sinless evening and night, and all the time of our life without condemnation, let us entreat. Let us commit ourselves and one another unto the living (*zōnti* [Gr. ζῶντι; printed ζώνη]) God through His Christ."

At Matins the same proclamation of the deacon was read somewhat differently:

"Save them, O God, and raise them up by Thy grace. ## The Bowing of Heads (*Glavopreklońenie*)

The Litany of Supplication, being in its conception the final prayer of the service, concludes with the impartation of peace, that is, the actual bestowing by God through the priest of that wherewith the supplications at Vespers (as at every service) began, and which is regarded as the sum of all blessings (in biblical usage "peace" signifieth also salvation; cf. the angelic hymn at the Nativity of the Savior; see above, p. 511). The prayer itself here, at its conclusion, reaches its summit in that action is joined unto words, that besides words it is expressed also in the action of the bowing of heads, which replaces the penitential bending of the knees. Over those who have bowed their heads at the invitation

=== LEAF p700 ===
of the deacon, there is read secretly by the priest (as are all his prayers at the services) a special "Prayer of the Bowing of Heads": **"O Lord our God, Who didst bow the heavens and come down"**, wherein the priest entreats God to look upon those who by this action express their submission unto God and their hope, to preserve them always and in the approaching night from every enemy, the devil, and evil thoughts; in the ecphonesis of the prayer: **"Blessed and glorified be the majesty of Thy Kingdom"**, God is naturally glorified as King.

### History of the Bowing of Heads

The Prayer of the Bowing of Heads replaced the ancient imposition of hands by the bishop at the end of the service upon each worshiper, as was the case, for example, in the Jerusalem Church of the 4th century (see Introductory Chapter, p. 142) and at Vespers and Matins in the *Apostolic Constitutions*, where the prayer at this imposition of hands is also cited, *(Orig. p. 585)* being of homogeneous, though not identical, content with the present one (ibid., p. 136). The present prayer is already read in manuscripts of the 10th century, yet certain manuscripts have in its stead other prayers of the bowing of heads. Thus, the Euchologion of the Sinai Library No. 957 of the 9th–10th centuries: **"O Lord our God, Who sittest upon the throne of glory and beholdest the abysses, looking down with a merciful eye upon our humility: bless all of us who have bowed our heads unto Thee, and exalt our horn in Thy goodwill; blessed be the all-holy name of Thy love for mankind."** The Euchologion of the Paris National Library: **"O Holy Master, who have bowed the neck of our body unto Thee, we pray: grant us ever with an upright and unswerving mind to look upon the heavenly glory and to be exalted unto Thy majesty; for Thine is the majesty and Thine is the Kingdom"** [^2302].
