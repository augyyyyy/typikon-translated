=== LEAF p261 ===
because on this day the Cross of the Lord was found. Therefore was it so arranged that the day of the first dedication of the aforesaid churches should be the very day when the Cross of the Lord was discovered, in order that all things together might be celebrated upon one and the same day with all joy. And in Holy Scripture we find that the day of dedication is that day when holy Solomon, upon completing the House *(Orig. p. 274)* of God which Solomon had built, stood before the altar of God and prayed, as it is written in the Books of Chronicles [Paralipomenon]. And so, when these days of dedications arrive, they are kept for eight days: many days prior, people begin to assemble from every quarter—not only of hermits or apotactites from various provinces, that is to say from Mesopotamia, and Syria, and Egypt, and the Thebaid, where there are many hermits, but likewise from all diverse places and provinces; for there is none who upon this day doth not hasten unto Jerusalem unto so great a joy and unto such venerable days; and laymen, both men and women of faithful spirit, in like manner assemble in these days from all provinces unto Jerusalem, for the sake of the holy day. In these days also there assemble in Jerusalem the bishops—when they are few—to the number of more than forty or fifty; and with them come many of the clergy. And what need is there to say more? Whoever takes no part in so great a solemnity during these days accounteth himself to have fallen into the greatest sin, unless there be some hindering necessity which keepeth a man from his good intention. In these days of dedications the adornment of all the churches is the same as that which obtaineth at Pascha and at Theophany; and each day the service is celebrated in diverse holy places even as at Pascha and at Theophany. For upon the first and second days the service is celebrated in the great church which is called the Martyrium. On the third day—upon the Mount of Olives, that is to say in the church which is upon the mount from whence the Lord ascended into heaven following upon His Passion, in which church is the cave wherein the Lord taught the Apostles upon the Mount of Olives. On the fourth day... [defect in manuscript]' [^1428]

Likewise, according unto the testimony of Sozomen, from the time of the consecration of the Martyrium under Constantine the Great, 'the Jerusalem Church celebrateth this festival annually and with exceeding solemnity, such that the mystery of baptism is then conferred and the church assemblies continue for eight days; by occasion of this solemnity there flock thither, for the visiting of the holy

=== LEAF p262 ===
places, many from almost the entire inhabited world.' [^1429]

### Theotokion Feasts: The Dormition and the Annunciation

With the waning of paganism—the struggle against whose mythical elements had previously precluded dwelling upon the details of the earthly origin of the Saviour—Christianity was able to render due veneration, after Christ, unto His Mother as well. Constantine the Great erected three churches in Her honor in the new capital. In Rome, prior unto the construction of the Liberian Basilica (which until recent times was regarded as the most ancient church), there existed, as recent investigations have revealed, a church dedicated unto the Most Holy Theotokos (*Santa Maria Antiqua*). The consort of Emperor Marcian (450–457 AD), Pulcheria, erected in the Constantinopolitan suburb of Blachernae the church of the Theotokos that was subsequently to become so renowned [^1430], as well as churches unto Her of the Hodegetria and in Chalcoprateia. *(Orig. p. 275)* The Council of Ephesus (431 AD) took place in the Church of the Most Holy Mary [^1431]. Unto Her likewise was dedicated the Church of the Spoudaioi in Jerusalem [^1432]. In the encomium of Theodore upon St. Theodosius (circa 500 AD) it is stated that in the Palestinian monasteries there is celebrated annually with great solemnity the commemoration of the Theotokos (*Θεοτόκου μνήμη*) [^1433], that is to say, in all probability Her Dormition. If the homilies upon the Annunciation of Patriarch Proclus of Constantinople († 446 AD) and of Peter Chrysologus of Ravenna († 450 AD) are genuine, then in this same epoch there arose also the Feast of the Annunciation; in favor of this testifieth likewise its intimate chronological correlation with the Nativity of Christ.

### Commemorations of Martyrs

The cessation of persecutions, by relegating the martyrs into the sacred distance of the past, could not but intensify their veneration. Festivals in their honor arose all the more naturally in that their relics and sepulchres were before the eyes of all [^1434], and the exact days of their demise were known. Every local church possessed an extensive roll of martyrs, and men now began to take care to gather into a unified whole the records concerning the time and place of their suffering and to celebrate their memory without omission. In every significant church there was formed its own Menologion of saints. Its compilation was rendered all the easier by the fact that regarding many martyrs there were preserved either official judicial transcripts [^1435] of their interrogation and trial, or else records penned by Christian eyewitnesses themselves during the passion and immediately following upon the death of the martyr [^1436]. It was necessary merely to classify these materials, and this began to be done already in the III century, with the work in certain churches receiving the most painstaking and systematic execution [^1437]. In view of this, *(Orig. p. 276)* it is not surprising that in the IV century local churches came to possess Menologia of their own martyrs.

### The Ancient Menologia

=== LEAF p263 ===
#### a) The Philocalian Calendar

Originally these Menologia were appended unto pagan calendars, wherein alongside the pagan festivals there were designated the Christian solemnities; or else lists of saints venerated in a given locality were simply subjoined unto the pagan calendar by way of an appendix unto the calendar wherein the pagan festivals were recorded. Such, for example, is the calendar in the aforementioned Chronograph belonging unto Furius Dionysius Philocalus [^1438]. This is a purely pagan calendar for the year 354 AD; but subjoined unto it are: a list of twelve Roman popes (from Lucius unto Julius I, from 252 AD unto 352 AD) with the designation of the day of their burial (the list beareth the title *Depositio episcoporum*), and an analogous list of Roman martyrs (*Depositio martyrum*, extending down unto 304 AD), comprising 22 commemorations. Into this latter list were entered likewise the holy popes who died as martyrs (Sixtus, Fabian, Callistus, Pontian); there are mentioned also several non-Roman martyrs (Cyprian, Perpetua, and Felicitas—from the African Church, a daughter church in relation unto Rome); apart from the martyrs there are only two commemorations of the Apostles (February 22, 'the Chair of Peter at Antioch,' and June 29, Peter and Paul) and one festival of the Nativity of Christ—December 25. Beside the names there is no title of 'martyr' or 'saint'—of the 22 commemorations of martyrs in this calendar, 16 entered into our church calendar (the most important, apart from those already mentioned: Agnes on January 21 and Laurence on August 10).

#### b) The Calendar of Polemius Silvius

Of a similarly purely local and semi-pagan character is the calendar of Polemius Silvius, bishop of Sion (*Sedunum* / *Sitten*) in northern Italy (435–455 AD). In it are indicated pagan festivals (53 in all, whereas in Philocalus there are more than 100): *Carmentalia*, *Lupercalia*, *Terminalia*, *Quinquatria*, *Lavatio Cereris* (the washing of Ceres), *feriae servorum* (the festival of slaves, December 18 = Saturnalia), *feriae *(Orig. p. 277)* ancillarum* (the festival of handmaids, July 7), *natales* (birthdays) of the Caesars, of Cicero, and of Virgil, days of the sessions of the Senate, and circus games; and side by side—Christian commemorations: Theophany, the Death of Christ on March 25, His Resurrection on March 28, the Nativity of Christ, and six festivals of saints: *depositio Petri et Pauli* on February 22 (not June 29), Vincent, the Maccabees, Hippolytus, Stephen, and Laurence. Consequently, this was a calendar intended both for secular and for ecclesiastical use [^1439].

#### c) The Syriac Martyrology

Among purely Christian Menologia the most ancient is the Syriac Martyrology preserved in a manuscript (of Nitrian origin) of the year 411 AD, bearing the heading: 'The names

=== LEAF p264 ===
of our lords the confessors and victors, and their days wherein they received their crowns' [^1440]. It was compiled in 380–400 AD. It consisteth of two distinct parts: the martyrology proper, concluding with the words 'here end the confessors of the West,' and an appendix unto it bearing the title: 'The names of our lords the confessors slain in the East,' wherein no longer according unto the days of the month, but according unto hierarchical orders there are indicated the bishops, presbyters, and deacons who suffered martyrdom in Persia (Persia was thus accounted at that time to be the Christian East). In the martyrology, which begins with December (though December 1–25 is defective), there are commemorations for more than half the days of the year. Before the name of a martyr there is designated for the most part the place of his suffering; for hierarchical persons their dignity is stated. Apart from the martyrs, among whom are found also the Maccabees, there occur commemorations of bishops: one of Rome, two each of Alexandria, Antioch, and Palestine, and two of unspecified cities; moreover, for certain of these non-martyrs there is added: 'commemoration.' Among the Apostles: 'Stephen the Apostle and Coryphaeus of Martyrs' on December 26 [^1441], John and James on December 27, Paul and Peter 'the Coryphaeus of the Apostles' on December 28; of dominical festivals only Theophany on January 6, while the Nativity of Christ is lacking through manuscript defect; 'on the Friday following upon Pascha the commemoration of all the martyrs.' Consequently, the Menologion is almost exclusively martyrological. Certain martyrs are distinguished by the martyrology into the category of 'ancient'; these are in all probability all those who suffered prior unto Diocletian. Regarding the geographical distribution of the commemorations, the Nicomedian saints predominate: the name of Nicomedia is mentioned 32 times; Antioch 24, Alexandria 19, Constantinople, Rome, and Jerusalem twice each; in all probability the martyrology was originally compiled in Nicomedia and gradually moved eastward, becoming enriched with local commemorations, and in Persia received the mechanical addition of the second part. It representeth, therefore, the type of a local Menologion in the earliest stages of its evolution into *(Orig. p. 278)* an ecumenical one [^1442]. It shareth many common commemorations with the Pseudo-Hieronymian Martyrology of the VII century and with our own (for example, Ignatius, Polycarp, Agnes, Perpetua, Babylas, Pamphilus, Chionia, Agatha, Plato, Romanus, Pelagia, and others), though for the most part assigned unto different days of the month.

#### d) The Carthaginian Calendar

Another local Menologion of the same epoch is the Carthaginian Calendar, bearing the title: 'Here are contained the passions of the holy martyrs and the depositions of the bishops whom the Church of Carthage celebrateth annually' [^1443]. The latest commemoration dateth apparently from the beginning of the V century. The calendar begins with Pascha on April 19 and breaketh off at February 16; it containeth commemorations for 80 days; unto the names are appended titles: *martyr*, *holy martyr*, *apostle*, *evangelist*. Of festivals: the Nativity of Christ and Theophany (*Epiphania*); there are commemorations

=== LEAF p265 ===
of the Maccabees, John the Baptist, the Innocents slain by Herod, the Apostles Andrew, James 'whom Herod slew,' Luke, Stephen, eight Carthaginian bishops, and of the martyrs fifteen are common with our own calendar.

#### e) The Gothic Calendar

From the same epoch (prior unto the middle of the V century) there hath survived a fragment of a Gothic (Arian) calendar for October 23 – November 29 [^1444], containing exclusively Gothic names (it is strictly national—for instance, there are no commemorations of Clement or Cecilia); on November 14—the Apostle Philip; on November 29—the Apostle Andrew.

### Orders of Saints in the Menologia

A survey of this series of calendars demonstrateth sufficiently that these were strictly martyrological Menologia, and only rarely and by degrees did other commemorations enter into them—first and foremost those of the Apostles, chiefly as the primary martyrs [^1445]. One may conclude that of the Apostles *(Orig. p. 279)* the majority already in this epoch attained a place in the calendars of those churches which regarded them as their founders or wherein their relics had been discovered (in Rome—Peter and Paul; in Jerusalem—James; in Alexandria—Mark [^1446]). Very soon, however, earlier than those of the martyrs, apostolic commemorations must have become universal throughout the Church. Emperor Constantine the Great erected in Constantinople the 'Church (*μνήμη*) of the Apostles' [^1447], wherein he himself was entombed [^1448]. Among individual Apostles, in this epoch the commemoration of the Apostles Peter and Paul attained universal observance throughout the Church. The same may be said concerning the commemoration of John the Baptist and St. Stephen [^1449].

The calendars examined demonstrate that alongside their own martyrs the local churches venerated the memory of their bishops; yet it is characteristic that the days dedicated unto the latter were designated by a different term: not *natalitia* ('birthday,' that is to say 'passion'), but 'commemorations' (the Syriac Martyrology) or 'depositions' (the Carthaginian Calendar). According unto Sozomen likewise, each autonomous local church (for instance, Maiuma, Gaza) 'celebrateth its own solemnities (*πανηγύρεις*) of the martyrs and commemorations (*μνείας*) of the priests who had been among them' [^1450]. The departed hierarchy of the local church was thus placed almost in the same category with the martyrs, and in all probability in its entirety (in the Carthaginian Calendar there are eight local bishops) [^1451]. *(Orig. p. 280)* Later and more slowly than all others, Old Testament saints and angels entered into the Menologia. The former entered in all probability in proportion as their relics were discovered. Sozomen mentioneth the finding of the relics of the Righteous Zechariah [^1452]; Blessed Jerome mentioneth the translation of the bones of the Prophet Samuel from Judea unto Constantinople [^1453]; subsequent records

=== LEAF p266 ===
(Synaxaria, Menologia) speak of the discovery of the relics of the Prophet Isaiah, of Nicodemus, the Prophet Daniel, the Maccabees and their mother, Zechariah the father of the Forerunner, the Bethlehem Innocents, and Simeon the God-receiver [^1454]. Yet of these saints during the present period veneration was accorded only unto the Maccabees, as we have observed from the Calendars of Polemius Silvius, the Syriac, and the Carthaginian, and the Innocents, named by this last calendar (St. John Chrysostom hath a homily upon the memory of the Maccabees, and Blessed Augustine two sermons). Thus the veneration of Old Testament saints penetrated very slowly into the Church and developed later; the Western Church hath them not in its calendar. As regards the Angels, their veneration in this epoch is sufficiently attested by the church erected by Constantine the Great in a suburb of Constantinople unto the Archangel Michael, at which, according unto the testimony of Sozomen who recounteth this, many healings were wrought [^1455]. In certain localities it was necessary even to contend against excesses in the veneration of Angels, as for example in Phrygia and Pisidia, where the worship of Angels, already rebuked by the Apostle Paul [^1456], elicited the decree of the Council of Laodicea 'that Christians ought not to forsake the Church of God and depart and invoke angels (*ἀγγέλους ὀνομάζειν*) and hold assemblies in their honor (*συνάξεις ποιεῖν*)' [^1457]. It is self-evident that of the veneration of monastic ascetics (*venerable fathers*) there could as yet be no question.

### The Services for Saints

The veneration of saints, that is to say for the most part of martyrs, was expressed by the celebration of a vigil on the eve of their commemoration day, and on the day of the feast itself by the Liturgy with an agape, or at least general communion. At the divine service an encomium upon the martyr was pronounced, and the account of his passion was read. The service was celebrated for the most part upon the tombs of the martyrs themselves, consequently outside the city walls. All this is evident from a series of homilies by the Holy Fathers upon the memory of the martyrs. 'Consider how ridiculous it is,' saith Chrysostom in a homily upon the day of the martyrs, 'after such an assembly (*σύνοδον*), after vigils (*παννυχίδας*), after hearing the Holy Scriptures, after partaking of the Divine Mysteries, and after spiritual bounty (*χορηγίας*—almsgiving unto the poor or an agape for them on the occasion of the feast), to behold a husband and wife spending whole days *(Orig. p. 281)* in a tavern' [^1458]. Sidonius Apollinaris, bishop of the Arverni (= Clermont in Gaul) in the V century, thus describeth the feast of St. Justus:

> 'We assembled at the tomb of St. Justus. The procession was before dawn, the solemnity annual, an enormous multitude of both sexes which the spacious basilica could not contain, though surrounded moreover by extensive cryptoporticos. Upon the conclusion of the office (*cultu*) of the vigil,

=== LEAF p267 ===
> which was celebrated by monks and clerical psalmists with alternating sweetness of song (*alternante mulcedine*), each departed unto his own abode, yet not far off, so as to return again by the third hour (9 a.m.), when the divine service (*res*) was to be celebrated by the priests.' [^1459]

Commemorations of martyrs occurred every week about twice, and in all probability each time were celebrated with the like solemnity. Chrysostom saith: 'Lately (*πρῴην*) the blessed Babylas with the three youths gathered us hither; today two holy soldiers (the Martyrs Juventinus and Maximinus) have arrayed in battle order the host of Christ' [^1460]. According unto Blessed Theodoret, 'unto the shrines of the martyrs we assemble not once, nor twice, nor five times in the year, but frequently celebrate their solemn festivals (*πανηγύρεις*)' [^1461]. With the like solemnity were celebrated the memories of those few saints whom the Menologia of that age contained apart from the martyrs, as for example the Maccabees, and the Innocents slain by Herod. It could not be otherwise, for the Church looked upon these saints in the very same manner as upon martyrs. St. Chrysostom remarketh the singular zeal wherewith upon the day of the Maccabees the people assembled [^1462] in Antioch, the site of their suffering, where there was a basilica in their honor [^1463]. Blessed Augustine calleth their festival *solemnem diem* [^1464]. As for the Innocents, their commemoration in the majority of churches must have been celebrated together with the Feast of Theophany or the Nativity of Christ, judging from the fact that mention of them is found only in sermons and homilies upon this festival [^1465]. With no less, if not still greater solemnity was celebrated the memory of the Apostles where festivals were held in their honor. According unto the description of Prudentius, who visited Rome in 405 AD, on the feast of SS. Peter and Paul the entire city was in prayerful motion (*Romam per omnem cursitant orantque*); a vast multitude formed two enormous waves pouring into the two churches of the two Apostles (situated over their sepulchres and considerably distant one from another), in each of which on that day the bishop officiated [^1466]. This festival *(Orig. p. 282)* in the V century had in Rome even an eight-day post-festive period, if one may credit the title of a Sermon of Pope Leo I: *in octavis ss. apostolorum* [^1467].

### The Day of All Saints

In view of the fact that during the mass executions of Christians in certain persecutions the names, as well as the days of demise, of many of those who suffered remained unknown, and moreover each local church honored chiefly its own martyrs, in order that not one of these latter might remain in the Church without commemoration, there arose in this same epoch a festival in honor of all the martyrs, which subsequently was transformed into the feast of All Saints. We have seen

=== LEAF p268 ===
that in the Syriac Martyrology such a festival is assigned unto the Friday following upon Pascha. According unto Chrysostom, however, who hath a homily upon this day, it hath its place, even as at present, on the first Sunday after Pentecost:

> 'Eight days have not yet passed since we celebrated the sacred solemnity of Pentecost, and once again the choir of the martyrs hath received us—or rather an army and host in no wise inferior unto the host of Angels seen by Jacob, but vying with and equal unto it.' [^1468]

### Commemoration of the Departed

In intimate connection with the commemorations of the saints stood the commemoration of ordinary departed Christians. In the III century, as we have seen [^1469], no marked distinction was as yet drawn between the former and the latter. Now it is otherwise. Blessed Augustine saith:

> 'In the heavenly fatherland there is no place for prayer, but only for praise. Why then is there no place for prayer? Because in nothing is there any lack. Whatsoever is thought upon there is present; whatsoever is hoped for is possessed; whatsoever is longed for is attained. Yet even in this life there is a certain perfection: it is that which the martyrs have attained. Therefore the Church requireth, as the faithful know, when the martyrs are named (*recitantur*) before the altar of God, not to pray for them, but to pray for the other departed who are commemorated. For it is an insult unto a martyr to pray for him (*injuria est enim pro martyre orare*), for one unto whose prayers we ought to commend ourselves. For he strived against sin even unto blood.' [^1470]

The *Apostolic Constitutions*, however, do not as yet draw so sharp a line between the saints and ordinary reposed Christians: 'Assemble in the cemeteries, performing the reading of the sacred books and singing psalms for the reposed martyrs and for all the saints from the beginning of the world, and for your brethren who have fallen asleep in the Lord' [^1471]. In a later section of the *Apostolic Constitutions* there are indicated likewise the present days of commemoration:

> 'Celebrate also the third day (*τρίτα*) of the departed in psalms, readings, and prayers, for the sake of Him Who rose on the third day; and the ninth day (*ἔναта*) in remembrance of those who are here reposed; and the fortieth day (*τεσσαράκοста*) according unto the ancient *(Orig. p. 283)* pattern, for even so the people of Israel mourned for Moses; and the annual anniversary in memory of the reposed. And let there be distributed from his estate unto the poor in memorial of him.' [^1472]

=== LEAF p269 ===
St. Ambrose, on the occasion of the fortieth-day memorial (*sorokoust*) of Emperor Theodosius the Great, remarketh: 'Some are accustomed to observe the third day and the thirtieth; others the seventh and the fortieth' [^1473]. There was a prolonged hesitation between adopting the 7th or 9th day, and the 30th or 40th, which depended, in all probability, upon the nationality of the local church; the ancient Greeks offered sacrifices for the dead on the 3rd, 9th, and 30th days; the Romans—on the 9th day [^1474]. Blessed Augustine censured the observance of the 9th day as a pagan custom [^1475]. In Palestine even in the VI century the 7th day was observed [^1476]. The Syrians, according unto the testimony of St. Ephrem the Syrian, observed the 30th day [^1477]. Gradually in the East the 3rd, 9th, and 40th days were adopted, while in the West the 7th and 30th days prevailed [^1478].

### Commemorations of Earthquakes, Victories, and Episcopal Consecrations

In order that the picture of the liturgical year in the IV–V centuries may be complete, it is necessary to name several additional festivals and fasts which were not incorporated directly into our present Typikon, but which influenced it indirectly or were characteristic of the epoch. Thus days were celebrated that had been marked by special benefactions of God, such as deliverance from flood, earthquake, or military victories. According unto the testimony of Sozomen, when Alexandria during the reign of Julian was threatened with inundation by a sea flood, which nevertheless soon subsided, 'the day on which this occurred the Alexandrians denominated the "memorial of the quake" (*γενέσια τοῦ σεισμοῦ*), and even unto this day honor it with an annual festival; this latter they celebrate with solemnity and devotion, kindling throughout the entire city a multitude of lamps and offering unto God prayers of thanksgiving (*χαριστηρίους λιτάς*)' [^1479]. Cf. in our calendar October 26. Concerning festivities in honor of victories Eusebius speaketh: upon the overthrow of Licinius, for Christians 'there dawned joyful and festive days of celebration; all things were filled with light... throughout the cities and villages were heard choirs of praiseful hymns, wherewith Christians glorified, as they had been taught, first of all God the King of all, and then the pious Emperor with all his God-beloved children' [^1480].

Cf. in our calendar June 27, December 25, and the chanting of Many Years at the conclusion of Vespers, Matins, and the Liturgy. As a distinct festival *(Orig. p. 284)* there was celebrated annually, at least in the West, the day of the bishop's consecration, denominated, like the day of a sovereign's coronation, his 'birthday' (*natalis*). Concerning such a festival mention is made in the letters of St. Ambrose, St. Hilary, and St. Paulinus of Nola [^1481]. There have survived also several sermons of Blessed Augustine and of Pope

=== LEAF p270 ===
Leo I upon their *natales episcopi* or *episcopatus*, testifying unto the deep compunction which this festival awakened in the bishop himself, and unto how this day was honored by all the people and how greatly it strengthened the bond of the bishop with his flock [^1482].

### The Ember Days (Fasts of the Four Seasons)

Of fasts, apart from the chief and ancient ones indicated above, there arose in this epoch in the West the 'fasts of the four seasons of the year' (the Ember Days). Pope Leo I (440–461 AD) mentioneth them: 'Throughout the entire year the fasts are distributed so that the law of abstinence is prescribed for all seasons of the year: namely, the spring fast is kept in the Forty Days (Lent), the summer fast at Pentecost, the autumn fast in the seventh month, and the winter fast in the tenth'; as the foundation for these fasts St. Leo indicateth gratitude unto God for the gathered fruits of the earth [^1483]. The present dates of these fasts (for the 1st—the 1st week of Great Lent; for the 2nd—the week of Pentecost; for the 3rd—the 3rd week of September; for the 4th—the 3rd week of Advent) were definitively determined under Pope Gregory VII [^1484]. The foundation for these fasts was laid by Pope Callistus (217–222 AD), who 'instituted a fast on three Saturdays of the year according unto the prophecy concerning corn, wine, and oil' [^1485]. Parallel unto these Western fasts, in all probability in the East as well the annual fasts were brought unto the number of four.

### The Movement Against the Veneration of Martyrs and Monasticism

Summing up the accomplishments of the IV–V centuries in the domain of divine worship, one must recognize as paramount in this endeavor the rapid enrichment of the liturgical year with commemorations, and the transformation of divine worship and of the very conception thereof under the influence of monasticism, which stood in intimate connection with the extensive development of this latter. Neither the one nor the other, it must be observed, passed without opposition on the part of the puritans of the Church in that age. Blessed Jerome left a polemical treatise (composed in 406 AD) against the Gallic presbyter Vigilantius, *(Orig. p. 285)* which acquainteth us with the contemporary movement within Christianity (which enjoyed the sympathy even of certain bishops) against the veneration of martyrs and the spread of monasticism.

> 'We see,' Blessed Jerome citeth the words of Vigilantius, 'that after the manner of pagan rites, under the pretext of piety, there hath been introduced into the churches—even while the sun is still shining—the kindling of a multitude of candles; everywhere likewise kissing they venerate a certain powder (*pulvisculum*—in all probability dust from the tombs of martyrs or ashes from cremated bodies), I know not what, in a small
