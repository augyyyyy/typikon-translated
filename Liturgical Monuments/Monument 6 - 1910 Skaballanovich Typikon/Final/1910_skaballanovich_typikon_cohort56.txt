According to the *Life of St. Sabbas*, written approximately 20 years after his repose, the canonarch summoned the brethren to the services "at the hour of striking" (*hora krousmatos* / ὥρα κρούσματος) each time "with the assent (*para gnōmēn* / παρὰ γνώμην) of the superior."[^1865] According to the Greek manuscript of the Rule from the library of the Monastery of St. Sabbas, MS 1458, the ecclesiarch tolls the bells for the service and prepares everything necessary for it. # THE USUAL BEGINNING

## Its Significance

The Typikon naturally attaches special significance to both the beginning and the end of church services and therefore surrounds them with special solemnity, seeking in both to present in compendium the entire content of divine worship, as it were encapsulating the whole faith and hope of Christians. Therefore the usual beginning, like the customary conclusion (the dismissal / *apolysis*), of church services is composed of the most *(Orig. p. 456)* substantive prayers. Most especially, the "usual beginning" directly constitutes, as it were, a complete and self-contained service.

## The Opening Blessing of the Priest

The usual beginning of church services consists of two parts quite unequal in scope: the opening exclamation (blessing) of the priest and the opening prayers of the reader. The priest's exclamation is thus, in the proper sense, the "beginning" of the service, its primordial starting point. The Typikon assigns it extraordinary significance. Without this blessing, the service cannot begin. Consequently, despite the brevity of this exclamation, it may be regarded as a component fully coequal with all the remaining extensive content of the usual beginning. The opening exclamation, generally speaking, contains the glorification of God. Through this content, the opening blessing well expresses the entire character of Orthodox worship, wherein the glorification of God predominates not only over petition, but also over thanksgiving and instruction; by virtue of this, Orthodox worship is devoid of that utilitarian-practical character typical of Roman Catholic worship—with its predominance of the petitionary element—and of Protestant worship—with its predominance of homiletical content. Thereby Orthodox worship most closely approximates heavenly worship, the angelic doxology of God. (And the character of worship stands, of course, in intimate connection with the general comprehension of Christianity across its three confessions). An exclamation of such content immediately elevates our thought to that glory and praise which all His creatures offer to God, and of which the service we are undertaking is intended to form a certain part. This initial glorification of God, or more precisely—merely

=== LEAF p553 ===
the confession of that Divine glory which exists even without us—is not identical before different services; rather, in its solemnity it corresponds to the degree of solemnity of the service itself. Most sublime and expressive is the opening exclamation of the Divine Liturgy, which glorifies distinctly all the Persons of the Holy Trinity and in Their most grace-filled and mysterious operation (in the establishment of the Kingdom of God); in the opening exclamation of Matins the Holy Trinity is glorified only in Her indivisibility and general operation upon the world (as life-creating); whereas the opening exclamation of the remaining services is limited to the glorification of God in general. Yet even in this last form, the opening exclamation of church services constitutes, in the view of the Typikon, such a sacred mystery that the unconsecrated lips of a layman cannot touch it. This is, of course, because within it the Name of God is pronounced for the first time in the service with extraordinary solemnity—as the constitutive fundamental concept and content of the entire service—the Name which the ancient Hebrew considered unlawful to utter, replacing it with descriptive divine designations or (like the Samaritans) with the word *shema* ("the Name"), or else accompanying it with the phrase: "He is blessed forever."[^1867]
*(Orig. p. 457)*

## History of the Opening Blessing

**"Blessed is God"** (*Blagosloven Bog* / *Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεός*) is a frequent Old Testament expression and the most common glorification of God in both the worship and everyday life of the ancient Hebrew.[^1868] But concerning how ancient the use of this expression is as the opening blessing of the Hours, Vespers, and other services, nothing can be asserted with certainty, since to this day no complete manuscript Horologia of public divine worship earlier than the 13th century have been discovered (cell Horologia have been preserved in 8th–9th century manuscripts), and even these Horologia do not deem it necessary (given the very purpose of the Horologion) to indicate the priest's exclamation. The Hieratikons of the 9th–12th centuries (Euchologia) restrict themselves to the text of the priest's prayers at Vespers and Matins. It is seemingly in 13th-century manuscripts that the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** (*Blagosloven Bog nash* / *Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*) is first clearly indicated; thus, for example, it is indicated in the 1292 Typikon of the Sicilian Monastery of Mili according to Vatican Library MS 1877 as the exclamation for the Hours and Matins (in the order for the first week of the Fast), whereas for Vespers the exclamation is **"Blessed is the Kingdom"** (*Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo* / *Εὐλογημένη ἡ βασιλεία*).[^1869] In the 13th-century Typikon of the Georgian Monastery of Shio-Mgvime, the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** is appointed for weekday Matins,[^1870] and in the Typikon of the Sevastianov Collection of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum MS 491/35 for Vespers (fol. 153v).

=== LEAF p554 ===
Naturally, in the earliest period the opening exclamation for divine services varied, like the liturgical orders themselves, across different local Churches. Yet it is remarkable that already the *Testament of Our Lord Jesus Christ* (*Testamentum Domini Nostri Jesu Christi*), a Syriac document of the 3rd century, indicates an exclamation by the bishop before Matins identical in substance to our current one: "Glory to the Lord."[^1871] The 6th-century Sinai ascetics began the service with "Glory to the Father..." (that is, with the Minor Doxology).[^1872] The Greek Horologion according to the order of the Lavra of St. Sabbas in Sinai Library MS 863 of the 8th–9th centuries likewise begins services with "Glory to the Father..." (in these latter two cases, the beginning may be indicated solely for cell services). The Coptic Horologion begins the offices with "In the Name of the Father..." The exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** (*Blagosloven Bog nash*) was presumably formulated in imitation of the exclamation at the Liturgies of St. Basil the Great and St. John Chrysostom: **"Blessed is the Kingdom"** (*Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo*). But regarding this latter exclamation, one likewise cannot state how ancient it is. The earliest manuscripts of these Liturgies already contain it in its current form—the Barberini manuscript of the 8th century and Imperial Public Library MS 226 (from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius Uspensky) of the 8th–9th centuries. Yet in the time of Chrysostom, "the presiding celebrant, entering the church (that is, before the service), said 'Peace to all.'"[^1873] And the earliest so-called "apostolic" liturgies—of the Apostle James, the Evangelist Mark, and others—possess nothing *(Orig. p. 458)* corresponding to the current solemn exclamation of the presiding celebrant before the service.[^1874]
Among our contemporary services, the rites for the consecration of a church and of an antimension—which represent very lengthy and complex services—begin without an exclamation (starting with "Let us pray to the Lord" and a prayer), but in their midst contain a series of exclamations that now serve, or previously served, as opening blessings: **"Blessed is our God"**, repeatedly "Glory to our God unto ages of ages," "Glory to Thee, Holy Trinity, our God, always, now and ever, and unto ages of ages," and "Blessed be the glory of the Lord from His place."[^1875] An allusion to the liturgical use of this last exclamation is made in the words of Abba Silouan (4th c.) cited by Nikon of the Black Mountain (11th c.): disapproving of the chanting of newly composed troparia, the elder remarks that in the heavens "one choir chants Alleluia unceasingly; another choir: Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord of Sabaoth; another choir: Blessed be the glory of the Lord from His place and habitation."[^1876] Inasmuch as the services of the daily cycle generally imitated the Divine Liturgy, in those Churches where the Liturgy lacked anything corresponding to the present opening exclamation, the daily offices lacked it as well. It was presumably the Cathedral / Sung Office (*asmatike akolouthia*) that first introduced solemn exclamations at the beginning of Matins, Vespers, and the Hours following the model of the Constantinopolitan Liturgy, wherein originally such an exclamation was presumably

=== LEAF p555 ===
literally borrowed from the Divine Liturgy; in the earliest monument of the Sung Office (12th c.), not only Matins and Vespers, but even the Hours possess the exclamation: **"Blessed is the Kingdom"** (*Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo*).[^1877] Monastic offices, adopt*(Orig. p. 459)*ing from the Sung Office the custom of a solemn exclamation at the beginning, presumably replaced the eucharistic liturgical exclamation with less solemn ones, introducing among them various gradations of solemnity.

## Structure of the Usual Beginning

The opening exclamation constitutes, as noted, although the chief, nonetheless the smaller and exclusively sacerdotal portion of the "usual beginning," which in the remainder of its content represents something independent. This remainder is subdivided, in turn, into several sections. First of all, one must distinguish from the usual beginning the first two prayers: **"Glory to Thee, our God"** (*Slava Tebe, Bozhe nash*) and **"O Heavenly King"** (*Tsaryu Nebesny*), as introductory prayers not constituting an indispensable portion of the usual beginning (it lacks them during fasts and at services for the departed). The first of these prayers constitutes, as it were, the opening exclamation of the reader following that of the priest (a variation of the priestly exclamation); the second (**"O Heavenly King"**) implores the abiding presence with us of the Holy Spirit, the true Author and Perfecter of every prayer pleasing unto God,[^1878] wherein, corresponding to the opening exclamation and the entire character of Orthodox worship, this petition is clothed in doxological form. With the exception of these supplementary and later (as we shall see) parts of the usual beginning, it represents a harmonious and intricate whole. Specifically, it resolves into three supplications: (1) the Trisagion (**"Holy God"**), (2) the Prayer to the All-Holy Trinity (**"All-Holy Trinity"**), and (3) the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"**), each concluding with a doxology: the first two with "Glory to the Father...", and the last with "For Thine is the kingdom..." The supplications increase in volume with each successive section: the threefold "have mercy on us" in the Trisagion gives way to a sevenfold petition in the second supplication (4 petitions in the prayer "All-Holy Trinity" and a threefold "Lord, have mercy" after this prayer) and a twelvefold "Lord, have mercy" in the third supplication. Correspondingly, the simple concluding doxology of the first two sections (the minor doxology) is augmented in the third into a sacerdotal doxology ("For Thine is the kingdom..."). Once again (like the opening exclamation and all Orthodox worship), this entire complex prayer—gradually ascending in its final section to the Lord's Prayer—despite all its spiritual tension, bears a joyful and laudatory character, beginning with the seraphic doxology, invoking the Persons of the Holy

=== LEAF p556 ===
Trinity by diverse laudatory names across all supplications addressed to Them, and concluding with doxology (the final "Glory to the Father...").
The transition from the supplications of the usual beginning to the introductory psalms of each service is provided by the verse from Psalm 94:6 [LXX 94:6 / MT 95:6]: "Come, let us worship and fall down before Him," repeated like a prokeimenon three times with subtle textual modifications that masterfully intensify its theological emphasis: "... to the King, our God," "fall down before Christ (the King...)," and "(fall down before) Christ Himself (... and our God)."
*(Orig. p. 460)*

## Abridgment of the Usual Beginning

However, the usual beginning does not always assume this form. This represents its most complete form, which it receives three times during the course of the day before the three cycles of daily offices: before the cycle of morning services, daytime services, and evening services; since these cycles are opened respectively: the first by the Midnight Office, the second by the Third Hour, and the last by the Ninth Hour, it is only before these offices that the usual beginning is appointed in its full scope. Before the other services it is abbreviated to **"Come, let us worship"** alone. The most solemn services—the Divine Liturgy, the Typika (*Izobrazitelnye*) which replaces it, and the Mystery of Crowning (Marriage)—possess no usual beginning whatever. Conversely, during fasts, for the most important services that follow others—namely, Matins, and Vespers with the Ninth Hour (if these latter two do not follow immediately after the Sixth Hour)—as well as for services for the departed, the usual beginning omits only its introductory portion (**"Glory to Thee, our God"** and **"O Heavenly King"**), in view of its joyous tone (moreover, these prayers were introduced into the "usual beginning" later than the others, and without them it assumes its ancient form).

## History of the Usual Beginning

Like all ecclesiastical orders, the usual beginning evolved gradually. Its earliest composition was presumably restricted to the Lord's Prayer alone. In the 2nd century, secondary daily offices such as the Hours existed simply in the form of this single prayer.[^1879] When the structure of the services expanded, it was natural to place this prayer at the head of the service or make it its center. How early it became customary to begin church services with the Lord's Prayer is demonstrated by the fact that in this regard, the practice of the Western Church—generally so divergent from the Eastern—coincides with it:

=== LEAF p557 ===
every Roman Catholic service, except the Mass and Compline, begins with the Lord's Prayer recited secretly (*sub silentio*). The present "usual beginning" of Roman Catholic offices places it in the first position. Yet according to ancient breviaries—for example, a Mozarabic one—it was preceded by: "Lord, have mercy; Christ, have mercy; Lord, have mercy" (*Kyrie eleison, Christe eleison, Kyrie eleison*),[^1880] an exclamation preceding the Lord's Prayer in a somewhat modified and expanded form in our own "usual beginning." According to the Greek Typikon of the Sicilian Monastery of Mili (Vatican Library MS 1877) of 1292, the usual beginning of Matins and Vespers, at least in Great Lent, consists solely of the **"Our Father."**[^1881] In addition to the Lord's Prayer, the usual beginning in the Roman Catholic Church includes the "Hail Mary" (*Ave Maria*) and the Creed (the latter not always: only before Matins and Prime). With this one may compare the counsel of Abba Silouan (4th c.) recorded by Nikon of the Black Mountain: "When Thou risest from sleep, first of all glorify God with thy lips, and *(Orig. p. 461)* do not begin thy rule immediately; rather, stepping outside thy cell, recite the confession of faith and the Our Father, and then entering, begin thy rule."[^1882] Is not our present prayer **"All-Holy Trinity"** (*Presvyataya Troitse*) a vestige of the former presence of the Creed in the usual beginning?
No less ancient a component of the usual beginning than the Lord's Prayer and the "Lord, have mercy" is the Minor Doxology. Among the 6th-century Sinai ascetics, the usual beginning of the office was **"Glory to the Father"** (*Slava Ottsu*).[^1883] The gloss "that is, the Trisagion," which certain manuscripts of Nikon's *Pandects* append to these words where the Sinai liturgical practice is described—for example, a 16th-century manuscript of the St. Nicholas Edinoverie Monastery—merely testifies to how strange the absence of the Trisagion from the usual beginning must have appeared to later copyists of Nikon. Meanwhile, the usual beginning lacked the Trisagion not only in the 6th century, but even in the 8th–9th centuries, as demonstrated by the Greek Horologion according to the order of the Lavra of St. Sabbas in Sinai Library MS 863, wherein services begin with the Minor Doxology and the **"Our Father."**[^1884] Still later than the Trisagion was the prayer **"All-Holy Trinity"** introduced into the usual beginning; it is, however, already mentioned by the 11th-century Studite Typikon, Moscow Synodal Library MS 330/380 (fol. 12). Latest of all was the prayer **"O Heavenly King"** (*Tsaryu Nebesny*) introduced into the usual beginning; it appears in this position for the first time in 14th-century typika and horologia. Later still was introduced the prayer **"Glory to Thee, our God"** (*Slava Tebe, Bozhe nash*); although it is present in certain 14th-century Horologia (for example, Moscow Synodal Library MS 48/151/1238), it is absent from 16th-century manuscripts and from the printed Horologia of the Krakow

=== LEAF p558 ===
edition of 1491. In the modern Greek *Hōrologion*, this prayer is indicated only in the usual beginning of the Midnight Office; for the Third and Ninth Hours, the prayer **"O Heavenly King"** is designated as the first prayer.

## History of the Individual Prayers of the Usual Beginning

Although in its present form it is of comparatively late origin, the usual beginning consists, nonetheless, of prayers of profound antiquity. The most ancient prayer within it must be acknowledged as **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Gospodi pomiluy* / *Κύριε ἐλέησον*).

### "Lord, Have Mercy"

"Have mercy" is the most ubiquitous prayerful exclamation in the Psalms; the formula **"Lord, have mercy"** occurs in the Prophets Isaiah and Baruch.[^1885] Concerning its liturgical use in the Christian Church, the earliest witness is provided by the *Apostolic Constitutions* (4th–5th c.), where it serves, as with *(Orig. p. 462)* us, as the response to the petitions of the liturgical litany (*ektenia*).[^1886] Into the daily offices this formula was borrowed from the litany and, consequently, serves as a substitute for it.

### "Our Father"

The next most ancient prayer in the usual beginning is the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"** / *Otche nash* / *Πάτερ ἡμῶν*). However, its concluding doxology did not issue from the lips of the Savior in the form in which it is used among us today, but rather, according to the most widely received text in the Evangelist Matthew, in the following form: "For Thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory forever, Amen";[^1887] according to the Evangelist Luke, the Lord's Prayer possesses no doxology whatever.[^1888] In view of this, and on account of the variance in the transmission of this doxology across different documents (for instance, in the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* [Didache][^1889] it is transmitted in an even briefer form than in Matthew: "For Thine is the power and the glory forever"), scholars today consider that this doxology found its way into the text of the Gospel of Matthew from liturgical practice.[^1890] (In the Roman Catholic Church it is used without a doxology).

### The Minor Doxology

The next most ancient component of the usual beginning is the doxology, the so-called Minor Doxology: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, now and ever, and unto ages of ages. Amen"** (*Slava Ottsu i Synu i Svyatomu Dukhu, nyne i prisno i vo veki vekov. Amin'* / *Δόξα τῷ Πατρὶ καὶ τῷ Υἱῷ καὶ τῷ ἁγίῳ Πνεύματι, νῦν καὶ ἀεὶ καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων, ἀμήν*), which enjoys the most pervasive use throughout Orthodox divine services. It arose,

=== LEAF p559 ===
presumably, from the concluding doxologies of liturgical prayers: traces of these doxologies can be found repeatedly in the New Testament Epistles and in the Apocalypse. The simplest formula of such doxologies is: "To Thee" or "To Whom be glory (*doxa* / δόξα) forever."[^1891] This formula began to be expanded in all three of its clauses, wherein the expansion of the first term ("To Him") and of the last ("forever") gave rise to the present Minor Doxology, while the elaboration of the second term ("glory")—for example, into expressions such as "glory and honor," "glory and dominion"—gave birth to our contemporary litanic exclamations (concerning which more will be said hereafter). The Person ("To Him") to Whom the doxology is offered in New Testament literature is in most instances God the Father alone,[^1892] or God the Father through Jesus Christ, for example: "Unto Him be glory in the Church by Christ Jesus..."[^1893] or "To the only wise God our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory and majesty, dominion and power..."[^1894]
*(Orig. p. 463)*
Occasionally glory is ascribed to Christ alone.[^1895] The third term of the primitive brief doxology—"forever"—likewise receives amplification in apostolic literature: "unto ages of ages,"[^1896] or "throughout all generations of the age of ages,"[^1897] or: "before all ages, and now, and unto all ages."[^1898] In this sense one may say with Blessed Theodoret that the Minor Doxology was "handed down to the Church by eyewitnesses and ministers of the Word."[^1899] But the first example of a complete doxology explicitly naming all the Persons of the Holy Trinity is found in the final prayer of the Martyr Polycarp: "through Whom [Jesus Christ] to Thee, with Him and with the Holy Spirit, be glory..."[^1900] In like manner, the martyrial acts of St. Ignatius the God-bearer conclude with the words: "he finished his course in Christ Jesus our Lord, through Whom and with Whom be glory and might to the Father with the Holy Spirit forever, Amen." In similar fashion conclude the acts of other 2nd-century martyrs.
Moreover, in the *Life of St. Eugenia*, who suffered under Emperor Commodus (180–192 AD), there occurs a doxology almost in its current form; it is recounted therein that Angels escorting the martyr to heaven sang a hymn in which one could discern the words: "glory and honor to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, both now and ever, and unto ages of ages, Amen."[^1901] According to St. Irenaeus of Lyons (2nd c.), the Gnostics perceived in the words "unto ages of ages" (*eis tous aiōnas* / εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας...), uttered "by us in our thanksgivings," a designation of the aeons.[^1902] Tertullian (3rd c.) remarks that a Christian attending the public spectacles is forced "with those very lips wherewith he utters Amen in church (*in sanctum*), to give

=== LEAF p560 ===
acclamation to a gladiator, shouting 'forever' to someone other than to Christ God."[^1903] In these passages of Irenaeus and Tertullian scholars discern a reference to the doxology.[^1904] Origen states that "prayer must always conclude with the doxology of God through Christ in the Holy Spirit."[^1905] In the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* (3rd c.), the formula of the doxology is identical to that in Polycarp, but alongside it our contemporary formula also occurs, occasionally both combined together, for instance: "through Whom [Jesus Christ] to Thee [the Father] be glory, and power, and honor to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit in the Holy Church, now and ever, and unto ages of ages";[^1906] or: "Glory to Thee, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, unto ages of ages."[^1907] Nevertheless, even in the 4th century the names of the Persons of the *(Orig. p. 464)* Holy Trinity in the doxology were predominantly not placed in the identical grammatical case. Thus, the prayers in the Euchologion attributed to Serapion, Bishop of Thmuis (4th c.), for the most part have the conclusion: "through Whom [Jesus Christ] to Thee [the Father] be glory and dominion in the Holy Spirit, now and unto all ages of ages."[^1908] Basil the Great cites the doxology in the form: "Glory to the Father and to the Son with the Holy Spirit."[^1909] The Arian heresy, exploiting for its own designs one of the ancient doxological formulas—"Glory to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit"—compelled the Church definitively to adopt the formula: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit"**, as that which most clearly expresses the consubstantiality (*homoousion*) of the Holy Trinity. According to certain reports, this formula was ratified at the First Ecumenical Council.[^1910] According to the testimony of Blessed Theodoret, Leontius, Patriarch of Antioch, in order to conceal his Arianism, pronounced the words "to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit" in the doxology in an undertone, but "and now and ever..." loudly and distinctly.[^1911] St. Flavian, Patriarch of Antioch, together with a multitude of monks, was the first of all to chant with distinct clarity: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit."**[^1912] In the treatise *On Virginity*, attributed to St. Athanasius the Great, the thanksgiving prayer after a meal concludes: "Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit." One of the Gallican Councils (of Vaison or Bazas), held several years after the Council of Nicaea, decreed: "Inasmuch as not only in the Apostolic See, but also throughout the entire East and in all Africa and Italy, on account of the machinations of heretics blaspheming against the Son—alleging that He was not always with the Father, but began from a certain time—in all conclusions of prayers (*clausulis*), after 'Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit,' there is said: 'As it was in the beginning, is now, and ever, and unto ages of ages,' therefore we decree that in all our churches it shall likewise be recited in this manner."[^1913] The Minor Doxology possesses this very form today in the Catholic Church: *"Gloria Patri et Filio et Spiritui Sancto. Sicut erat in principio, et nunc, et semper et in saecula saeculorum"*,[^1914] consequently differing from our own
