### From the Author

*(Orig. p. 6)*  
History is the best teacher, and the most profound interpretation of the Typikon will be its historical interpretation. Having in view from the very outset to provide the Typikon chiefly with an historical explanation, the author initially intended to present the latter in the form of exclusively detailed notes upon individual points and particulars of the Typikon. But were the work to be reduced merely to this, such an exposition could scarcely be termed historical, insofar as history is something integral and unified. Therefore, the author deemed it best to unite those historical explanations of the Typikon that could be joined together into a single large chapter, so that in the examination and analysis of the Typikon itself—without repeating what has already been stated in this latter chapter, and confining oneself merely to cross-references—one might dwell solely upon minor details. Thus, the interpretation of the Typikon in its very foundations and essential features is already presented to the reader in this "Introductory Chapter." This is neither a preface to the *Tolkovy Typikon* nor a mere introduction; it is its most fundamental and essential part. The author trusts that it is worthy of its honorable title as a "chapter" of the Typikon, albeit only an introductory one. The author is confident that whoever possesses the inclination or patience (either of which will depend not only upon one's interest in liturgical worship, but also upon the extent of the reader's general knowledge of church history)—whoever possesses the patience or inclination to read through this "chapter" will have the veil removed from before many mysteries and enigmas of the Typikon. Such a reader will perceive that the Typikon is already half and in its main substance explained, and will look to the subsequent contents of the book for the explanation of such particulars as, for example, why at Vespers the Augmented and Supplicatory Ectenias are appointed after the Entrance and reiterate the petitions of the Great Ectenia, or why at the Ninth Hour during Great Lent only three prostrations are appointed. Yet answers to such questions must be sought in the future installment of our work by one who reads this present installment attentively, unless this reading itself prepares him for the independent resolution of such perplexities.

The author does not fear that the scholarly character of the book will hinder its comprehension on the part of anyone interested in the ecclesiastical Typikon and *(Orig. p. 7)* studying it. A journalistic article may present, by its abundance of foreign loanwords and specialized terminology, far greater obstacles to understanding than the straightforward exposition of the present volume, which endeavors nevertheless not to sacrifice scientific scholarly objectives in the least, as befits a science that does not seek access merely to the broad masses.

### A. The Liturgical Typikon Prior to the Appearance of Its Complete Manuscripts
#### (1st–8th Centuries)

Inasmuch as our liturgical Typikon takes as its task the adaptation of church services to the sacred commemorations of the day, its compilation—which was naturally mental and conceptual at first, rather than written—must have begun with the very emergence of sacred commemorations within Christianity. The appearance of the first sacred commemorations dates back to apostolic times, when there unquestionably existed already a liturgical celebration at least of the day of the Resurrection (the Lord's Day / Sunday). Thus, it is no exaggeration to state that the first lines of our ecclesiastical Typikon, which set forth precisely the order of Sunday worship, were conceived by the holy Apostles themselves. It is not surprising, therefore, that ancient Russian scribes called the Typikon an "inspired book" [^2]. Its compilation began in those ages when there existed in Christianity a spiritual elevation of life that made possible the emergence of divinely inspired scriptures.

The formation of our divine services, which began in the apostolic age, reached its conclusion, one might say, only by the sixteenth century, when its Typikon acquired fully its contemporary form. And certainly, the value of this Typikon cannot be diminished by the circumstance that a millennium and a half was required for its creation. Rather the reverse: thanks to such an extended period of development of our liturgical Typikon, the Church of diverse epochs was able to take part in this development, and each of these epochs possessed its own strength and beauty and reflected them upon our centuries-old Typikon. And not only did the Church of different eras create the present Typikon, but also the Church of diverse localities and lands; consequently, it was wrought by the Church not merely Apostolic, but Catholic (*Soborna*) *(Orig. p. 9)* both in space and in time. From the pages of the Typikon there breathes the tender spirit of ancient ecclesiastical life, now of pious Constantinople, now of ascetic Egypt and the Thebaid. If, moreover, one takes into account the multitude of Old Testament material that has entered into our divine worship, in the moments of the latter we spiritually relive even sacred biblical antiquity, and "walk in unity of mind" in the Church of the Patriarchs and Prophets.

Being the conciliar creation of the Church, the liturgical Typikon was nonetheless elaborated preeminently by her most worthy sons alone. Its immediate authors were men who had no other life than prayer and fasting, for whom the sweetness of divine worship permitted them to partake of food but once every five days and to sleep only standing or sitting, who brought their all-night vigils to completion in churches locked and set ablaze

from without by pagans—men whose martyr blood was at times mingled with the Eucharistic Blood. The pages of the Typikon compiled by them could not fail to be drenched with tears of compunction and the God-offered blood of confession, which cannot fail to be felt therein and to impart to the book a spirit of singular sanctity and purity. Herein lies the secret of the profound influence of Orthodox divine worship upon the soul: he who participates in it absorbs the influence of the Church of all ages in the persons of her finest powers and lives the life of the entire Church. This influence emanates not only from the poetry and music of the service, but also from its very architectonics—from these fortyfold repetitions of **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Господи, помилуй*), silent bows and prostrations intruding as lengthy pauses into chanting and reading, from the antiphonal manner of singing, and the like. The Typikon is concerned precisely with the architectonics and aesthetics of divine worship. In particular, among the array of ascetics, the currently authoritative Typikon attributes its compilation to Saint Sabas—more precisely, "that of the holy Lavra of our venerable and God-bearing Father Sabas in Jerusalem." This is the monastery not far from Jerusalem in the rugged Judean wilderness, founded in the fifth century by Saint Sabas the Sanctified. What share of participation belongs to this venerable father in the elaboration of the present Typikon cannot yet be determined in the present state of scholarly sources. In any case, the homeland of the present Typikon is Palestine and its wildernesses cultivated by ascetic labors. The creators of our ecclesiastical Typikon were, consequently, nurtured by that very wilderness which nurtured Elijah and John the Baptist, and which prepared the Savior Himself for His public ministry. Here the very air was saturated with sacred memories. Nearby were the greatest holy places of the Christian world—Jerusalem with the Sepulchre of the Lord and the Church of the Resurrection, where prayer of its own accord grew more fervent, and where, according to the testimony of a Western female pilgrim of the fourth century (Silvia of Aquitaine [Egeria]), when the Gospels of the Passion were read, "all the people raised such a cry and lamentation that there was no one who was not moved to tears at that hour" [^3].

*(Orig. p. 10)*  

### Old Testament Jewish Divine Worship

In order to form a proper conception of the origin of Christian divine worship and its ritual order, one must not forget that the Apostles and the most eminent members of the primitive Church were Jews; and though their faith and hope were no longer Old Testament, but new, their prayer and divine worship could not depart far from the Jewish forms of that day. Divine worship and ritual are conservative by their very nature; here sanctity is measured by antiquity... The religious enthusiasm that seized the Apostles and the other disciples of Christ after the Resurrection

---

=== LEAF p15 ===
of Christ is manifested in the fact that they spent whole days in the Temple ("they were continually in the temple") [^4], which Christ had come to abolish, where, of course, praising and blessing ("praising and blessing") God was possible in Jewish ritual forms. If we direct our attention to these forms, it will become evident to us what influence they exerted upon the subsequent development of Christian divine worship.

### Old Testament Temple Worship

Although the essence of Hebrew Temple worship consisted of sacrifices, a musical and vocal element always entered into it to such an extent that Temple worship seemed almost entirely transformed into music and singing [^5], while the latter was regarded as a special kind of votive offering alongside material sacrifices [^6].

#### The Order of Sacrificial Psalmody

Instituted by Moses only for feast days and especially solemn sacrifices [^7], their accompaniment by the sound of trumpets [^8] became an adjunct of the daily morning and evening sacrifice and developed into an elaborate order of liturgical *(Orig. p. 11)* chanting and music [^9]. By the altar stood a choir of Levites with musical instruments, and two priests with trumpets. As soon as the sacrificial animal was laid upon the wood, the Levitical choir commenced the chanting of the psalm to musical accompaniment, so that the sacrifice was consumed amid the sounds of this chanting and music.

The chanting of the psalm at the daily sacrifice began properly with the libation of wine upon the sacrifice, because chanting is possible only in gladness of heart, and wine, said the rabbis, "cheereth God and man" [^10]. During chanting, the psalm was divided into three sections by fairly prolonged pauses; at these pauses the chanting and music fell silent, and only the sound of the trumpet rang forth. Concluding the third section of the psalm, the Levites raised the pitch [^11], and this served as a signal for the priests to sound their trumpets. At these solemn pauses in the psalm the people fell prostrate, worshipping the Lord. Thus the sound of the sacred trumpets was heard three times on ordinary days during sacrifice. (On the eve of the Sabbath they sounded the trumpets twice more before this: the first time to cease labor, and the second time to separate the weekday from the Sabbath). Upon the completion of the psalm the people exclaimed: **"Amen. Alleluia"** [^12].

Upon the burning of the sacrifice and the conclusion of the psalm, the officiating priest, standing upon the steps of the Temple vestibule (*Ulam*), imparted the blessing to the people, who thereupon fell prostrate, especially if the high priest himself had offered the sacrifice and imparted the blessing [^13].

#### Daily and Festive Psalms: Canticles of the Sabbath

For the daily sacrifice, a proper psalm was appointed for each day

of the week: on the Sabbath, Psalm 91 [92]; on Sunday, Psalm 23 [24]; Monday, Psalm 47 [48]; Tuesday, Psalm 81 [82]; Wednesday, Psalm 93 [94]; Thursday, Psalm 80 [81]; Friday, Psalm 92 [93] [^14].

*(Orig. p. 12)*  
On especially important occasions proper psalms were chanted, and the psalm was sung not by the Levites alone, but the entire congregation participated in its singing, taking up the refrains at the ends of the verses. For this purpose certain psalms are structured in such a manner that each verse or section concludes with one and the same refrain (for example, Psalm 135 [136], where every verse has the refrain **"For His mercy endureth forever"** [*Яко в век милость Его*]; while for a series of psalms, 105–106 [106–107], 112–118 [113–119], 134–135 [135–136], **"Alleluia"** served as such a refrain). In the Bible several instances are indicated where such psalms were sung with the participation of the people, and in all these cases reference is made, apparently, to one and the same psalms—134 and 135 [135 and 136] [^15]. These psalms were suited for divine worship among the Jews, as among the early Christians, also because they are directly directed against idolatry [^16].

On the Sabbath, at the time of offering the special Sabbath sacrifice, the Canticle of Moses was sung: **"Give ear, O heaven"** (*Вонми, небо*; Deut. 32:1–43)—a one-sixth part of it on each Sabbath in succession—at the morning sacrifice; while at the evening sacrifice was sung **"I will sing unto the Lord"** (*Поим Господеви*; Exod. 15:1–19). (Hence is understood the passage in the Apocalypse [^17] where it is said that the saints, entering into eternal Sabbath-rest, sing the Song of Moses) [^18].

On the feasts of Pascha and Pentecost they chanted the Lesser or Egyptian Hallel, that is, Psalms 113–118 [114–119], which were considered especially rich in content, since they glorify the Exodus from Egypt. At the Feast of Tabernacles (*Sukkot*) was sung the Great Hallel, consisting, according to differing opinions of the rabbis, of Psalms 117–135 [118–136], or 119–133 [120–134] (the Gradual Psalms / Psalms of Ascents), or 134–136 [135–137] [^19]. Verses of one of these psalms—the 117th [118th]—entered into our typikal **"God is the Lord"** (*Бог Господь*). (According to ancient Typika, **"God is the Lord"** was appointed only on feast days) [^20]. After the model of the Gradual Psalms are composed our Gradual Antiphons (*anabathmoi*), which formerly replaced our present Polyeleos (according to ancient Typika, these latter were indeed sung with the verses of these psalms) [^21].

#### Significance and Character of Old Testament Temple Singing

For the singing of the psalms at divine worship, David set apart 4,000 singers and musicians out of all the 38,000 Levites [^22]; from that time there was formed among the Hebrews a kind of hereditary caste of singers, preserved even after the Babylonian Captivity [^23]. A strict specialization was introduced into it: the descendants of Jeduthun and Asaph played upon stringed instruments, the choir of Heman upon percussion instruments, while the sons of Korah were, apparently, the vocalists [^24]. Although the psalms were sung to musical accompaniment, judging by their titles, certain psalms were intended exclusively or *(Orig. p. 13)* preeminently for vocal singing—namely, those superscribed *shir*, *ᾠδή*, "song" / "canticle"; others for instrumental music—namely, those superscribed *mizmor*, *μέλος*, *ψαλμός*, "psalm." Sometimes these superscriptions are combined, now the first being placed first, now the second; such psalms must

---

=== LEAF p17 ===
have been designated for singing at first with the voice, and then with music, or vice versa. In general, however, vocal singing in the Old Testament Temple was deemed more important than instrumental music. In the thought of the Talmud, the glorification of God in the Temple was to be expressed chiefly by a loud voice, whereas instrumental music was admitted only out of necessity [^25], "inasmuch as instruments supported the voices and singing of the Levites" [^26]. Membership in the Levitical order was requisite only for the singers; any Israelite, if known as an adept in music, could present himself at the Temple with his instrument and join the band of musicians, but no kinship with the priests conferred the right to sing vocally with the singers: this was the sacred prerogative of the Levites alone. The instrumental musicians were termed "vexers of the Levites," because (according to Maimonides' exposition) by their playing they drowned out the beauty of the Levitical song [^27]. In such relation as instrumental music stood to vocal singing, in like relation stood the playing of trumpets to instrumental music: it constituted the exclusive prerogative of the priests and was forbidden to the Levites. Levitical women also participated in Temple music and singing [^28].

The singing of psalms in Old Testament divine worship was distinguished by that simplicity characteristic of all antiquity; it was, of course, unisonous (like our ancient Russian and contemporary Old Believer chant), to which an indication is also found in the Bible ("and they were as one, the trumpeters and singers, making one sound to be heard") [^29]. Thus Hebrew music, calculated not for variety but for acoustic power, had a thundering and piercing character. The Talmud states that when they played in the Temple, it was heard as far as Samaria [^30]. Clement of Alexandria compares Hebrew music with Greek, specifically with the Dorian mode [^31], which formed the basis of our Tone 1. In any case, the singing was diatonic, not enharmonic or chromatic [^32]. High voices were perhaps termed *alamoth*, "after the manner of maidens" (soprano), as certain psalms are superscribed (Slavonic: *o taynykh*, "concerning secrets"); low voices—*sheminith*, "upon the eighth" (octave, Slavonic: *o osmey*); the former tones expressed joy, and the latter sorrow: for the most part, psalms with the first superscription are of a joyful character (for example, Ps. 45 [46]), while the latter are mournful (for example, Ps. 6, **"O Lord, rebuke me not in Thine anger"** [*Господи, да не яростию Твоею обличиши мене*], and Ps. 12 [13], **"How long, O Lord"** [*(Orig. p. 14)* *Доколе, Господи*]). The Psalms of Ascents (Gradual Psalms) must have united all tones within themselves, beginning from the lowest and concluding with a loud *forte* [^33].

#### Lamps and Incense

Concurrently with the daily morning and evening sacrifice in the first court of the Temple, its outer court, the other two divisions of the Temple—the Sanctuary (Holy Place) and the Holy of Holies—were not left without divine worship, though the service here was reverently silent. In the Holy Place in the morning, the high priest or priest performed the preparation (Heb. *hetiv*, properly "adorning / trimming," Slav. *ustroyayet*) of the lamps [^34] for their evening

---

=== LEAF p18 ===
kindling, and in the evening their actual kindling for the entire night [^35]. It is possible that from this Christian Vespers received the title of "lucernarium" service, *λυχνικόν* (*lychnikon*, a name applied to it already by fourth-century monuments) [^36]. At the same time, the burning of sweet incense took place upon the Altar of Incense, which stood directly before the veil of the Holy of Holies and therefore belonged rather to the latter than to the Holy Place [^37]. Occurring thus in the holiest precinct of the Temple, this offering of incense was accounted as much a sacrifice as the bloody sacrifice [^38]. From this, perhaps, our Matins and Vespers received the offering of incense at their beginning.

#### Rites of the Feast of Tabernacles (*Sukkot*)

We do not dwell upon the Hebrew festivals, which are sufficiently known to everyone. We note only certain rites of the Feast of Tabernacles. On this feast the entire altar was adorned with greenery, and the rite of the drawing of water took place. The Temple was not locked in the evening as usual, but the people remained therein through the entire night; and to the music of the Levites, with singing and with burning torches in hand, four men performed sacred dances. Moreover, the Temple court was illuminated by lamps set aloft in such numbers that they were said to illuminate all the courtyards of Jerusalem [^39].

We have set forth the order of Old Testament Temple worship with attention directed to those parts of it which, in our opinion, could have exerted an influence upon our divine services. From the exposition presented, the degree of this affinity is sufficiently manifest.

Upon the dispersion of Christians from Jerusalem and upon the destruction of the Temple, the majestic sacred music of the Temple and the Levitical choirs must have resounded in the ears of the faithful for far too long a time for this not to have left its imprint upon the elaboration of Christian divine worship.

*(Orig. p. 15)*  

### Synagogal Worship

Synagogal worship likewise could not fail to exert a certain influence upon the development of Christian divine services. Synagogal worship was closely related to that of the Temple. In the Temple itself, upon its outer court, there was a synagogue, in which the divine worship celebrated in the Temple was merely supplemented. Devoid of bloody sacrifices and of all the intricate ceremonial of the Mosaic Law, synagogal worship, far more than that of the Temple, was distinguished by a spiritual, verbal (rational) character, and therefore stood closer to Christian worship [^40].

Divine worship in the synagogue, as now, was celebrated not daily, but only on Saturdays (the Sabbath), on great feasts (such as Pascha, Pentecost, Tabernacles, and Purim), on the Day of Atonement (*Yom Kippur*), and other fasts. Very early there were joined to these days Monday and Thursday [^41], simply, perhaps,

---

=== LEAF p19 ===
as market days in ancient Jerusalem. In addition to morning and evening worship, corresponding to the morning and evening sacrifice, the synagogue early introduced also a day (midday / noon) service. In this they may have had in view the ancient Hebrew custom of praying three times a day, mentioned by David and observed by Daniel and his companions in captivity [^42].

The synagogue was originally and in its concept rather a religious-educational institution than a liturgical one. But since in cities distant from Jerusalem (and in the Babylonian Captivity, where its inception may be sought) it was the sole place where religious consolation could be obtained, almost from its very emergence prayer began to be joined to the reading and exposition of Scripture; and already by the time of the Maccabees the synagogue possessed a very complex liturgical ritual [^43]. During the earthly life of the Savior and the Apostles, this ritual must have possessed an appearance quite close to contemporary synagogal worship. On the basis of investigations into this question (beginning with the twelfth-century rabbi Maimonides) [^44], one may delineate in ancient and contemporary synagogal worship approximately those components that must trace back to the first century after Christ.

Synagogal worship consists of: 1) introductory verses, psalms, and two prayers; 2) the recitation of the *Shema* (confession of faith) with its surrounding benedictions; 3) a series (from 7 to 18) of "Benedictions" or prayers of the *Shemoneh Esreh*; 4) the reading of the Law (except at the midday service); 5) the reading of the Prophets (at the morning Sabbath and festival services); 6) the concluding priestly blessing.

*(Orig. p. 16)*  

#### Introductory Psalms and Prayers

Divine worship in the synagogue now begins with introductory verses from the Psalms, whole psalms (akin to our introductory / preparatory psalms), and prayers (in one of which occur the words: "I will bow down and fall prostrate and bend the knee"). Psalms predominantly of an alleluiatic character are employed (perhaps because the very word *alleluia* may be regarded as an address of the reader to the congregation with an invitation to praise the Lord). The verses of the psalm are appointed to be read or chanted in recitative by one individual, while the congregation repeats either the same verse, or the first verse of the psalm, or responds to it with the refrain "alleluia" and others [^45]. All of this is regarded merely as preparation for divine worship. In different epochs the introductory part of synagogal worship assumed diverse forms, and concerning its degree of antiquity nothing definitive can be asserted.

Proper divine worship commences with the officiant (the *sheliach tzibbur*, cantor) approaching the ark of the scrolls and reciting the *Kaddish* and *Barechu* prayers, which contain the glorification of the divine Name and begin with the words: "May Thy name be magnified

#### The Shema

Then follows the reading of the so-called *Shema*, akin to our Creed [^48], consisting of the following three sections of Scripture: Deuteronomy 6:4–9; 11:13–21; and Numbers 15:37–41 (the first section begins "Hear," Heb. *Shema*, whence also the title).

*(Orig. p. 17)*  
The *Shema* is preceded and concluded by proper prayers [^49]. In the prayers of the *Shema*, God is glorified for the creation of light and the Angels and for the election of Israel, and a petition is contained that God would extend His mercy to the descendants of the holy Forefathers and turn them toward the fulfillment of the commandments [^50]. The *Shema* with its prayers is now read by a single reader, and the congregation responds to the prayers with "Amen." Every Jew, with the exception of women, children, and slaves, is obligated to recite the *Shema* with its prayers even outside the synagogue, morning and evening [^51], yet without that prayer which contains the doxology of the Seraphim: "Holy, holy..." The *Shema* is one of the most ancient parts of synagogal worship. The contents of its prayers are reported by Flavius Josephus, who attributes them to the time of Moses [^52]. An allusion to the *Shema* is found with great probability in the question of the Savior to the scribe, a man learned in the Law: **"How readest thou?"** (*Како чтеши?*) [^53]; the scribe responds to this question with the words of Deuteronomy contained in the *Shema*; perhaps the question of Christ was understood by the scribe not in reference to the entire contents of the Law, but in reference to that reading which was termed reading *par excellence*, that is, to the reading of the *Shema*.

#### The Benedictions: Shemoneh Esreh

The reading of the *Shema* is followed by the prayers termed "Benedictions" (*Tefillah*) or *Shemoneh Esreh*, which signifies "Eighteen," because these prayers number eighteen (now properly nineteen), although they are never read in such full number. The first three of these prayers glorify the omnipotence and goodness of God; the last two contain thanksgiving for the divine mercy and a general supplication for the blessing of God; while the remaining prayers contain diverse particular petitions: for the conversion of Israel to repentance, forgiveness, deliverance from evil, health, the fertility of the earth, the ingathering of dispersed Israel, the restoration of the kingdom, the destruction of heretics (*minim*), the reward of the righteous, the rebuilding of Jerusalem, the sending of the Messiah, the hearing of prayer, and the restoration of sacrifice [^54]. The "Benedictions" are stamped with the character of profound antiquity [^55], and are distinguished *(Orig. p. 18)* by deep religious sentiment [^56].
