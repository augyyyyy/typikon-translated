## History of the Entrance

Already at the Jerusalem services of the 4th century in the Church of the Resurrection there was something corresponding unto the Entrance, namely, the entrance of the bishop

=== LEAF p664 ===
behind the lattice (iconostasis) into the cave of the Tomb of the Lord, which replaced our altar [sanctuary], for prayer and the bringing forth thence of the blessing. Yet it is remarkable that such an entrance took place at all services there, even at the Hours, with the exception of Vespers alone, at which the bishop prays not behind the lattice, but before it (Introductory Chapter, pp. 140–142). This latter circumstance was, perhaps, because an entrance in the evening took place at the Liturgy, which in some places was still celebrated in the evening. When the Liturgy was everywhere transferred unto the morning, it was bound to transmit all the rites of its Entrance unto Vespers. All the particulars of the Vesperal Entrance are explained historically from the Liturgical entrances and their setting, especially in the Constantinopolitan Church *(Orig. p. 555)* of Hagia Sophia, the Typikon whereof influenced all other Typika. According unto the description of the treatise *On the Ceremonies of the Byzantine Court* (*De cerimoniis aulae Byzantinae*) of the 10th–11th centuries, the Little and Great Entrances at the Liturgy of great feasts in Hagia Sophia took place in the following manner. The patriarch, as well as the emperor, arrived at the Liturgy for the Little Entrance. The patriarch arrived earlier and awaited the emperor in the inner narthex (vestibule) with all the clergy; the patriarch held a cross, the archdeacon the Gospel, and the deacons censers and lamps; prior unto entering into the narthex the emperor in a special chamber of the outer narthex removed his crown (in token of reverence), in order to enter into the temple without it; having entered into the inner narthex, the emperor venerated the Gospel and the cross, the patriarch bowed unto the emperor and censed him, they kissed each other, and the emperor, taking the patriarch by the right hand, proceeded with him through the narthex unto its main doors, called the "Imperial Doors"; before them the patriarch read quietly the prayer of the Entrance; the emperor with a candle in his hand bowed thrice before the doors, and upon the blessing of the Entrance by the patriarch with the present words, during the singing of **"Come, let us worship"**, the emperor, having relinquished the candle, having venerated again the cross and the Gospel and taking the patriarch by the hand, entered with him into the temple and passed unto the solea, preceded by the clergy, the archdeacon with the Gospel, deacons with censers and lamps, and banners; the suite of the emperor entered into the temple through the side doors before the emperor and, as he passed through the temple, chanted *polychronion* (many years) unto him; the banners were set upon the solea. Having venerated the Holy Doors [Royal Doors], the patriarch with the words **"I will enter into Thy house..."** entered into the sanctuary; the emperor, however, taking the candle again, made a threefold bow before the Holy Doors and, having relinquished the candle, entered through the Holy Doors into the sanctuary, wherein the patriarch or hierarch drew the Holy Doors near unto him for kissing; having approached the Holy Table, the emperor kissed its covering raised for this purpose by the patriarch, took from one of the officials who had entered the sanctuary through the side doors the coverings or a chalice, which he brought as an obligatory gift at each such entrance into the temple, and set them upon the Holy Table, kissing the sacred

=== LEAF p665 ===
objects situated upon the latter; then the emperor censed the Holy Table, going round about it with the patriarch; he censed also the other sacred treasures of the sanctuary, before which he previously bowed with a candle in his hand. After the censing, having bowed mutually with the patriarch and kissed him, the emperor withdrew into a special compartment of the temple—the *metatorion* (*mutatorium*), where he listened unto the Liturgy until the Great Entrance. Before the Great Entrance, in which, besides the emperor in the proper sense, or "the great [emperor]", there participated also he who bore the title of Caesar, or "the lesser emperor" (that is, the co-emperor crowned during the emperor's lifetime, or another imperial kinsman), it was reported unto the emperor that the time of the Entrance had arrived; he vested in a chlamys (which he removed after the Little Entrance) and, preceded by banners, proceeded along the right side of the temple toward its center, where the sacred ministers already stood with the Holy Gifts; unto the emperors, great and lesser, officials handed the candlesticks with lighted candles standing right there, customarily borne by readers at the entrances, and the emperors, performing now the duties of *depotatoi* (the lowest clerics, taper-bearers [^2205]), *(Orig. p. 556)* carried the candlesticks before the Holy Gifts from the ambo (in the center of the temple) unto the Holy Doors [Royal Doors], preceded by banners; at the Holy Doors the emperors handed the candlesticks unto court dignitaries, who set them upon the solea; the emperors took their stand on both sides of the Holy Doors, in which the patriarch stood and received the diskos [paten] and chalice from the archdeacon and the first priest who bore them; the latter, carrying the Holy Gifts past the emperors, said: **"May the Lord God remember your imperial realm in His Kingdom always..."**; and handing the sacred vessel unto the patriarch, they said: **"Thy high priesthood..."**; the patriarch, receiving the sacred vessel, prayed for the emperors and dignitaries; the emperors took their leave of the patriarch and withdrew into the *metatorion* until the kiss of peace at the Symbol of Faith [Creed] [^2206].

## The Distinction of the Vesperal Entrance from the Liturgical Entrances

Although the Vesperal Entrance was elaborated upon the foundation of the Liturgical entrances, yet it differs essentially from them in that it is performed without the Gospel (with the Gospel only when it is read at Vespers) and without the Gifts; by virtue of this the center of the Entrance is the priest himself. Unto him, depicting in himself Christ, whereas at the Liturgy Christ is represented by the Gospel or the Gifts, honor is rendered by the bearing before him of the censer and candle.

## Peculiarities of the Vesperal Entrance in Historical Development

The burning of incense was one of the accessories

=== LEAF p666 ===
of royal processions in the East and in Byzantium [^2207]. In exactly the same manner was also the bearing before him of lamps. The latter in Byzantium took its origin from the carrying of torches before the Roman Augusti in processions. Before the Byzantine emperor during certain ceremonies a special official, the *lampadarios*, carried the *divamvoulon* (*dibamboulon*), a precious candlestick with a gilded lamp, the edges whereof were dyed with cinnabar. A similar candlestick, called the *monamvoulon* (*monobamboulon*) (in these words are contained the numbers 1, 2 with some other unknown word), was carried in Byzantium before the patriarch [^2208]. Before the Patriarch of Jerusalem in the 8th–9th centuries on solemn occasions walked 12 monks with candles [^2209]. A newly elected bishop in ancient Russia was escorted unto the tsar, the patriarch, and unto the cathedral with 12, 8, 6, or 4 candles, which were carried before him by special clerics—"flame-bearers" (*plamenniki*) or "fire-bearers" (*ognenniki*) [^2210]. According unto the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, the Vesperal Entrance was preceded by three lamps [^2211].

Unto Psalm 140 the Entrance was assigned because with this psalm in antiquity Vespers began. At Chanted Vespers the Entrance was performed at verse 8 of Psalm 140 [LXX 140:8]: "For unto Thee, O Lord, O Lord, are mine eyes" (Introductory Chapter, p. 342). Yet already the Studite Typika indicate the Entrance after the Theotokion at **"Lord, I have cried"** [^2212]. "Blessed is the coming of Thy holy glory, O Christ our God, always..."

# "O GLADSOME LIGHT" (*PHOS HILARON*)

## Content of the Hymn

After the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, as it were in the capacity of a concluding sticheron, follows the hymn **"O Gladsome Light"** (*Svete tikhiy* / *Phōs hilaron*), termed in the Greek Horologion "the Lucernarium (*epilychnios* [printed *epilychnōs* / Gr. ἐπιλύχνως]) thanksgiving", uniting with simplicity an early Christian depth and power of feeling. The hymn contains in itself the glorification through Christ of the Holy Trinity and of Himself, evoked by the appearance of the evening light and by gratitude for having lived unto it. It falls into three parts so independent that they can be regarded as three distinct hymns.

1. **"O Gladsome Light"** (*Phōs hilaron* [Gr. Φῶς ἱλαρόν], in Greek it can also be the nominative of the predicate: "Thou, O Christ, art Light"; "gladsome" [gentle] in view of the evening) **"of the holy glory"** (intolerable for us sinners) **"of the Immortal"** (in contrast unto the setting of the sun and the self-emptying of Christ in His death upon the Cross) **"Heavenly Father, Holy, Blessed"** (whence cometh also visible light; two principal mutually linked attributes of divine life), **"O Jesus Christ"** (Christ as the Son of Man).
2. **"Having come unto the setting of the sun"** (having lived unto sunset), **"having beheld the evening light, we hymn"** (*hymnoumen* [Gr. ὑμνοῦμεν], in thanksgiving for this) **"the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, God"** [^2216]. (A Trinitarian hymn, after which we sing already "a hymn unto Christ as God" [^2217]:)
3. **"Worthy art Thou at all times"** (not in the evening alone) **"to be hymned with reverent voices"** (more fitting than ours; Greek *aisiois* / *aisiais* [Gr. αἰσίαις / printed αἰσίοας], blessed), **"O Son of God, Who givest life; for which cause the world"** (the thankful world) **"glorifieth Thee"** [^2218] (in certain MSS *heortazei* [Gr. ἑορτάζει]—keepeth festival unto Thee).

In this manner, the hymn continues the thoughts of the Theotokion (Dogmatikon) and depicts with words the silent actions of the Entrance.

## Origin of the Hymn

**"O Gladsome Light"**, alongside the Great Doxology and **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**, is an ancient Christian hymn. It is cited in the Alexandrian *(Orig. p. 559)* Codex of the Bible of the 5th century. This is typical Christology of the 2nd–3rd centuries. Unto its antiquity testifies also the fact that it has lost the name of its author. It is significant that in this respect it shares the fate of several other very sublime hymns, such as, for example, besides the two indicated, certain troparia as well. Were not these hymns composed in a manner similar unto that of

=== LEAF p669 ===
works of folk poetry, that is, were they not collective creations of the Church? [^2219] This supposition receives yet greater probability from the composite nature of certain of these hymns, as we have seen in **"O Gladsome Light"** (the same is true of the Great Doxology and **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**). For **"O Gladsome Light"** two authors are named: the present Greek Horologion inscribes this hymn: "an ancient creation (an allusion unto its ancient anonymity) or, as some say, of Athenogenes the Hieromartyr" (Bishop of Sebaste in Armenia, †311, or another), wherein reference is made (in a footnote): "St. Basil the Great, chapter 29, *On the Holy Spirit*." In the Slavic Horologion the hymn is attributed unto Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem (634–644), concerning which certain Greek Horologia make the remark: "of St. Athenogenes the Martyr, and not of Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem, as some unjustly think; witness Basil the Great." In the time of Sophronius, in truth, the hymn was already in ecclesiastical use, as the Alexandrian Codex shows and the account of Sophronius himself concerning his visit unto Nilus of Sinai (see Introductory Chapter, p. 295). Yet the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople and the Chanted Office do not know this hymn (Introductory Chapter, p. 342). Unto St. Athenogenes the hymn is attributed, presumably, upon the foundation of the following words of Basil the Great: "Our fathers did not wish to receive in silence the grace of the evening light, but immediately upon its arrival offered thanksgiving. Although we cannot positively say who was the creator of those praises which we recite during the Lucernarium prayers, yet the people repeat the ancient voice, and unto none has it yet appeared that they utter blasphemy when they say: We praise (*ainoumen* [Gr. αἰνοῦμεν]) the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, God. If anyone knew also the hymn of Athenogenes, which he, in place of a safeguarding remedy, left unto his disciples when he himself hastened *(Orig. p. 560)* already unto the holocaust, he would know also the mind of the martyrs concerning the Spirit" [^2220].

In this testimony, apparently, a distinction is made between the hymn with the words "We praise the Father, the Son...", that is, perhaps, **"O Gladsome Light"**, and the hymn of the Martyr Athenogenes mentioning the Holy Spirit [^2221].

## Manner of Execution

Concerning the manner of executing **"O Gladsome Light"**, the Typikon says: "and we chant", which fully corresponds unto the poetic and musical character of this "evening thanksgiving". Yet at weekday Vespers this hymn is read ("he says" [*glagolet*]), however, not by an ordinary reader, but by the superior himself (Chapter 9 of the Typikon), like other most important prayers (the prayers

read by the superior are enumerated in Chapter 7 of the Typikon, note "Mark" [*zri*]).

## Ancient Typika Concerning "O Gladsome Light"

The Studite Typikon required **"O Gladsome Light"** to be sung *"vely"* (slowly and quietly?), as also the Six Psalms (concerning which in another place therein it is noted that it is sung or "spoken with a subtle and penitent voice"), as also the end of Matins, the Introductory Psalm at Vespers, **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**, the **"Now lettest Thou"** [Canticle of Simeon], the Trisagion, and the troparion—in contrast unto **"The Lord is God"** and the Alleluia at Matins, which according unto the Studite Typikon were executed "with a shout and with chanting", "with a clear voice", "plainly" [^2222]. This was, presumably, among other reasons, in consequence of the unvarying repetition of the first class of hymns at every service. This class of singing, although it was performed by the entire brotherhood, was similar unto reading. Hence arose the vacillation of subsequent Typika and disputes in the Russian Church concerning whether one ought to chant or read these prayers, and in particular **"O Gladsome Light"**. Concerning the manner of executing the latter at the Vigil, ancient manuscript copies of the Jerusalem Typikon speak more indeterminately than the present Typikon. The 13th-century Georgian MS: "the people: **'O Gladsome Light'**"; 13th–16th-century Greek MSS and printed editions of the 16th–17th centuries: "and we: **'O Gladsome Light'**" [^2223]; Slavic manuscripts: according unto 13th-century *Sluzhebniki* of the St. Sophia Library at the St. Petersburg Theological Academy Nos. 518 and 524—the deacon: **"O Gladsome Light of the holy glory"**; the clergy: **"Of the Immortal Heavenly Father"** [^2224].

According unto the most ancient Slavic manuscript Typika: "the deacon, having drawn a cross with the censer, proclaims: **'O Gladsome Light, Wisdom'**; the appointed monk, while the priests enter, says: '... **Of the holy glory**'" [^2225]; according unto other MSS: "the deacon proclaims: *(Orig. p. 561)* **'Wisdom! Stand upright!'**; the appointed monk says: **'O Gladsome Light'**" [^2226]; according unto later ones the same, only "and we say: **'O Gladsome Light'**" [^2227]. The Old Believer Typikon: "the deacon: **'Wisdom! Stand upright! O Gladsome Light'**; and we chant: **'Of the holy glory of the Immortal'**" [^2228]. The custom of the deacon pronouncing, besides **"Wisdom! Stand upright!"** (or **"Wisdom!"** alone), before or after this also the beginning of the hymn **"O Gladsome Light"**, is thus, apparently, of Russian origin; yet the circumstance that in the 6th–8th centuries it was required that the most important hymns be intoned by the subdeacon, deacon, or even the priest (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 295–296, 307), a remnant whereof even now is the intoning of the Paschal heirmoi by the clergy, compels one to see in this custom a survival of profound antiquity preserved in the practice of the Russian Church, perhaps from the dominance of the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople. A similar survival, yet only from the Studite Typikon, was the custom of reading **"O Gladsome Light"**.
