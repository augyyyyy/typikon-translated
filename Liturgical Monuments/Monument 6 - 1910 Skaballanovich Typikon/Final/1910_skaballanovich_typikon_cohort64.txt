=== LEAF p631 ===
religion seeks above all mercy—help in needs and redemption from sins. Being so all-encompassing, this prayer formula is at the same time the simplest and most readily understood form of prayer for all, most suited unto believers of all stations, needs, and degrees of development. Without doubt, unto such merits of its content this prayerful exclamation oweth its widespread application and dissemination in Christian divine worship.

How much this prayer formula corresponds unto the fundamental religious needs of man is demonstrated by its use even in pagan religions. "Invoking God," says Epictetus, "we entreat him: Lord, have mercy (*Kyrie eleēson*)" [^2110]. In Virgil there is an address unto the gods: "have mercy upon me (*miserere mei*)", "have mercy." In the Old Testament this exclamation was heard in prayers almost as frequently as among us [^2111]. It is no wonder that in the Christian Church also we immediately encounter such a widespread application of it in divine worship [^2112] as it hath in the Jerusalem and Syrian Churches of the 4th–5th centuries, where the choir and people respond with it unto each petition of the litany, according unto the testimony of the 4th-century Pilgrim and the *Apostolic Constitutions* (see: Introductory Chapter, p. 142 and note 2 on that same page). It is remarkable, however, that the *Testamentum* *(Orig. p. 528)* *of Our Lord Jesus Christ*, likewise a monument of the Syrian Church, yet earlier than the *Apostolic Constitutions*, doth not speak of the response **"Lord, have mercy"** unto the petitions of its litany. In exactly the same manner, in the Liturgy of the Apostle James **"Lord, have mercy"** is appointed only at the conclusion of all the petitions with the rubric: "thrice." Nevertheless, this prayerful exclamation received ever broader dissemination not only throughout the whole East, where it is customary among the Syrians, Armenians, and Abyssinians (see Introductory Chapter, p. 299; above, p. 475, note), but also in the West, as is evident from the Ambrosian Liturgy and a series of other witnesses. According unto Blessed Augustine, it was used also among the Goths [^2113]. According unto later accounts, into the Roman Liturgy it was introduced by Pope St. Sylvester I (314–335) [^2114]. The Council of Vaison of 529 decrees: "Inasmuch as at the Apostolic See, as well as throughout all the Eastern and Italian provinces, there hath been introduced the sweet (*dulcis*) and exceedingly salutary custom of saying very frequently *Kyrie eleyson* with great feeling and compunction, it is pleasing unto us also that in all our churches there should be introduced this so salutary custom both for matins, and for masses, and for vespers" [^2115]. Pope Gregory the Great (590–604) in a letter unto John, Bishop of Syracuse, defending himself against reproaches that he had permitted certain innovations in divine worship in imitation of the Greeks, says: "*Kyrie eleyson* we neither have spoken nor do we speak

=== LEAF p632 ===
as do the Greeks: among the Greeks all pronounce it together; whereas among us it is spoken by the clerics and responded unto by the people, and an equal number of times there is alternately spoken *Christe eleyson*, which among the Greeks is not pronounced at all" [^2116]. The capitularies of Charlemagne and Louis the Pious require that "Christians on Sundays, instead of standing at crossroads and in the streets and spending time in conversations, dances, and secular songs, should go unto the vigil and vespers, and walking both thither and back should sing their *Kyrie eleyson*"; likewise at funerals instead of diverse pagan rites, "that, if they know not the psalms, they should sing aloud *Kyrie eleyson, Christe eleyson*, men and women alternately" [^2117]. In Rome at the procession on the Feast of the Dormition the people sang antiphonally 300 times *Kyrie eleyson* and *Christe eleyson* [^2118].

### The Exclamation

The exclamations at the litanies, which were once the conclusions of the priestly prayers uttered before or after the litany, now—when such prayers are not appointed at the litanies or are uttered secretly—stand in connection with the petitions of the litany, indicating the foundation for their fulfillment now in the glory of God, now in His power, now in His goodness. The exclamation of the Great Litany points out such a foundation precisely in the glory of God, consequently, in the general perfection *(Orig. p. 529)* of the Divine essence, which evokes an involuntary veneration before Itself (thus, in comparison with the other exclamations, being the first at the services, it is distinguished by the universality of its content). At the same time it returns our thought from our needs and cares, with which the prayer of the litany was occupied, unto that glory of God which alone is the ultimate end of the world and of ourselves [^2119], and the sublime confession of which the Orthodox Church places at the head of all Her services in their opening exclamations.

### Development of the Exclamation

The exclamations at the litanies, as remarked above (see p. 462), possess a common origin with the Lesser Doxology, being, in the original form of the doxology "Unto Thee be glory unto the ages", an expansion of its second member "glory", whereas the present Lesser Doxology is an expansion of the first member "Unto Thee". Such an expansion is already provided upon the pages of the Apostolic Epistles. A one-member formula, besides that one: "Unto Him be dominion (*kratos*) unto the ages" [^2120]. Two-member formulae: "Honor and glory" (*timē kai doxa*), "Glory and dominion", "Glory and eternal dominion"

=== LEAF p633 ===
[^2121]; later two-member formulae: "Glory and greatness" (*megalōsynē*), "Glory and power" (*dynamis*), "Glory and reverence" (*sebas*), "Glory and worship" (*proskynēsis*) [^2122]. Three-member formulae: "For Thine is the kingdom (*basileia*), the power, and the glory"; "Glory, honor, and reverence"; "Glory, reverence, and thanksgiving (*eucharistia*)" [^2123]. Four-member formulae: "Glory, greatness, dominion, and authority (*exousia*)"; "Blessing (*eulogia*) and honor and glory and dominion"; "Glory and greatness, dominion, honor"; "Glory, honor, dominion, greatness"; "Glory, honor, greatness, an everlasting throne (*thronos*)" [^2124]. Five-member formulae: "Glory, honor, dominion, and greatness, an everlasting throne"; "Glory, honor, praise (*ainos*), doxology (*doxologia*), thanksgiving"; "Glory, praise, magnificence (*megaloprepeia*), reverence, worship" [^2125]. A seven-member formula: "Blessing, and glory, and wisdom (*sophia*), and thanksgiving, and honor, and power, and might (*ischys*)" [^2126]. A further stage in the development of the exclamations, it would seem, is constituted by the glorification of the grace, mercy, and love toward mankind of God, which is not encountered in the Liturgy of the *Apostolic Constitutions* and is very common in the most ancient of the so-called "Apostolic Liturgies," *(Orig. p. 530)* the Liturgy of the Apostle James [^2127]. The formula "Glory and dominion" was especially customary among the Egyptians: the Liturgy of Mark hath it approximately 10 times, the Liturgy of James once, the *Apostolic Constitutions* once—yet not at the liturgy, but at the mealtime prayer—and the homilies of Chrysostom more frequently [^2128].

### The Great Litany at Vespers

The employment at Vespers and Matins of a prayer with such content as the Great Litany is based upon the well-known exhortation—expressed, moreover, with particular force (*parakalō*—"I beseech", I entreat)—of the Apostle Paul: "first of all that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks be made for all men: for kings, and for all that are in authority" [^2129]. "What doth this signify," asks St. John Chrysostom, "when the Apostle saith 'first of all'? It meaneth—at the daily assembly. The faithful know this, when morning and evening they offer their prayer for all men upon earth, for kings and all that are in authority, for the faithful" [^2130].

### Prayers for Peace and the Emperor at Ancient Vespers

Yet not from Chrysostom alone did there enter into custom among Christians a daily morning and evening prayer of such all-encompassing content, with the turning, moreover, of special attention therein unto

=== LEAF p634 ===
persons in authority. Already in the Old Testament a special significance was attached unto prayer for civil authorities. According unto the testimony of the Prophet Baruch, the Babylonian Jews sent unto Jerusalem unto the High Priest a sum of money for sacrifices and prayer for King Nebuchadnezzar and his heir Belshazzar, "that their days may be upon earth as the days of heaven" [^2131]. According unto Josephus Flavius, in Jerusalem twice daily a sacrifice was offered for the Roman Caesar [^2132]. Unto the custom of daily, and moreover twice-daily, prayer for the whole world and for sovereigns the ancient Christian apologists appeal, for instance Tertullian (see Introductory Chapter, p. 84), in refutation of rumors concerning the misanthropy and unpatriotic character of Christians. St. Cyprian says that Christians "daily in the morning at morning worship and in the evening at evening worship pray for sovereigns" [^2133]. Against the Donatists, who had discarded prayer for kings and authorities, Optatus of Milevis says: "Entirely rightly doth Paul teach to pray for kings and for all authorities, even if the king were a pagan; all the more so if he *(Orig. p. 531)* be a Christian" [^2134] (the same thought is expressed by St. John Chrysostom in his homily upon the corresponding passage of 1 Timothy). With the adoption of Christianity by Constantine the Great, the names of the emperors began to be entered into the diptychs, consequently, to be commemorated at the liturgy before or after the hallowing of the Gifts; thus, the name of Constantine the Great was entered into the diptychs of the Constantinopolitan Church of the Holy Apostles built by him [^2135]; upon a column of the ancient Constantinopolitan Church of St. Laurence near the ambo were inscribed the names which the deacon read therefrom at the litany, and at the head of them stood the name of the emperor, and thereupon the bishop. Popes Felix III and Gelasius I (5th c.) state that the names of emperors were entered in the West, as in the East, into the diptychs [^2136]. When certain men "condemned the Emperor Anastasius as an adversary of the Council of Chalcedon, they excluded him from the sacred tablets" [^2137]. Maximus, Abbot of Chrysopolis (St. Maximus the Confessor, 7th c.), says against the Monothelites: "Among the sacred offerings upon the Holy Table, after the high priests, priests, and deacons, and the whole consecrated rank, there are commemorated with the laity the emperors, when the deacon saith: 'and those who have fallen asleep in the faith, Constantine, Constans, and the rest'; likewise he commemorates the living emperors after all the sacred persons" [^2138]. In the most ancient Roman sacramentaries—for example, that of Gregory the Great—in the prayer at the Canon of the Mass there is read: "*pro pontifice nostro N et pro rege nostro N*". Charlemagne at the Diet of Worms of 781 based the exemption of bishops and priests from military service upon the premise that "they must offer for the King and his army prayers, masses, and litanies" [^2139], and in his capitularies required that all priests "should offer constant prayers for the life

=== LEAF p635 ===
and reign of our lord the Emperor, and the health of his sons and daughters" [^2140].

In the course of time, however, in the West there disappeared from the Canon of the Mass the commemoration of the king, perhaps with the appearance of heterodox monarchs in many states (or because the reading of the diptychs during the liturgy ceased altogether), for which reason Pope Pius V did not include this commemoration in his edition (1570) of the Missal (*Sluzhebnik*), reviewed and approved at the Council of Trent [^2141]. Nor is this commemoration present in the Canon of the present Latin Mass; nevertheless, upon royal anniversaries a special mass is celebrated for the king or queen, even should they be heterodox. Yet at the beginning of the liturgy, in the collect after the Gloria, *(Orig. p. 532)* as well as in a special Sunday and festival prayer, the sovereign is commemorated—in some countries alone, in others with his consort and family [^2142]—wherein the psalmic words are employed: "O Lord, save our King—or Emperor—N., and hear us in the day whensoever we call upon Thee" (Ps. 19:10 [LXX 19:10]).

The East, on the other hand, in this regard remained more faithful unto the Apostolic precept. In all Eastern liturgies there are petitions for the sovereign and civil authorities; only in the Coptic Liturgy of Basil the Great is this petition absent from the intercessory prayer after the hallowing of the Gifts, being present instead in the prayers of the liturgy prior unto its Canon; whereas in all the rest such a petition is found within the intercessory prayer, whether it was uttered after the hallowing of the Gifts (as in the Armenian Liturgy, in the Coptic of Gregory the Illuminator, in the Jerusalem Liturgy of the Apostle James, and in the Liturgies of Basil the Great and John Chrysostom), or immediately prior unto the hallowing of the Gifts (as in the Alexandrian Liturgy of the Evangelist Mark, in the Abyssinian, in the Coptic of St. Cyril of Alexandria, and in the Mesopotamian of the Apostles Addai and Mari) [^2143]. The omission of the petition for the sovereign and authorities in the intercessory prayer of certain liturgies caused this petition, in the Great Litany compiled from this prayer, to be placed after the petition for the clergy and the people. At present, petitions for the sovereign in litanies are omitted only in Turkey. Thus, in the *Hieratikon* of the Constantinople edition of 1895, at the Great Litanies of the Liturgy, Vespers, and Matins, there is set in place of the petition for the sovereign: "For pious and Orthodox Christians, let us pray unto the Lord." The petition for sovereigns is placed after the archbishop in brackets; in the Liturgy of Basil the Great it is absent. In the *Euchologion* of the Athens edition of 1902, in the Great Litany there is a petition for sovereigns, while in the Augmented Litany there is none.

## The Place of the Great Litany at Ancient Vespers

=== LEAF p636 ===
Inasmuch as Vespers and Matins borrowed their litanies from the Liturgy, at the former the Great Litany possessed precisely the same composition as at the latter. Yet not always at Vespers did the Great Litany, or the prayer corresponding unto it, occupy its present place—the very beginning of the service. Even at the Liturgy originally it stood not at the beginning, but in the middle—after the readings from Holy Scripture; so also in the *Apostolic Constitutions*; so also in the Liturgy of the Apostle James, where it hath its complete form after the Creed, while at the beginning of the Liturgy it stands in an abbreviated form. At the Vespers of the *Apostolic Constitutions* the above-cited litany hath its place after a series of litanies for the catechumens, the possessed, the candidates for illumination, and the penitents, immediately before the Litany of Supplication; at 4th-century Jerusalem Vespers—after the readings and the entrance of the bishop into the sanctuary (Introductory Chapter, pp. 136, 142). There are monuments even from the 16th century where Vespers begins with three antiphons with Little Litanies, and only after the prokeimenon hath a litany that represents the present Augmented Litany with an opening from the Great Litany, approximately in that form which the Augmented Litany hath in the Liturgy of the Apostle James (see Introductory Chapter, *(Orig. p. 533)* p. 377; see below, "The Augmented Litany"). Such, presumably, was also the case at the ancient Vespers of the Church of Constantinople, or the Chanted Rite (*Asmatike Akolouthia*); yet already under Symeon of Thessalonica (15th c.) even Chanted Vespers hath the Great Litany at the beginning. Vespers of the Studite-Jerusalem type received the Great Litany into its opening section, in all likelihood, much earlier: the Studite-Alexian Typikon of the 11th century presupposes it, it appears, in its present place.

### Who Pronounces the Great Litany at Vespers?

Although the litany is a diaconal prayer, nevertheless the present Typikon directs the priest to pronounce the Great Litany, as also the two following Little Litanies. And only the third Little Litany—after the 3rd antiphon of the Kathisma—is, according unto the Typikon, pronounced by the deacon. Having spoken of the reading of the Prayers of Light by the priest, the Typikon continues: "And when the psalm is finished, he saith the Great Litany: **In peace let us pray unto the Lord**, and after the litany the exclamation: **For unto Thee is due**." In this manner, according unto the mind of the Typikon, the participation of the deacon in the celebration of Vespers, which imparts unto the service a special solemnity, ought to begin only from **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Gospodi vozzvakh*), just as at Matins it begins from the Polyeleos or from the reading of the Gospel, if there be no Polyeleos (see below). In view of this, the initial censing at the All-Night Vigil likewise takes place without a deacon, whose duties are fulfilled by the paraecclesiarch. The requirement of such a late appearance of the deacon at Vespers

=== LEAF p637 ===
emanates from the *Diataxis* of Patriarch Philotheus (14th c.), wherein it is stated: "Upon the completion of the Prayers of Light, the priest says the Great Litany, and the deacon vests for the third antiphon of the Psalter and says the Little Litany." Yet this requirement is foreign unto ancient Greek and Slavic manuscripts of the Typikon, which assign all the litanies unto the deacon: "The Great Litany is by the deacon; and the priest exclaims: **'For unto Thee is due...'**, and at each antiphon (of the First Kathisma) he makes the Little Litany, and the priest exclaims" [^2144]. So also in the Georgian manuscript, and in the printed Greek editions [^2145]. Yet in later Slavic manuscripts and the Old Believer Typikon: "The priest or the deacon says the Great Litany" [^2146].

## The Kathisma at Vespers

After the Great Litany at Vespers there almost always followeth a Kathisma, which is omitted at Vespers on the Sundays and feast days themselves on account of fatigue from the vigil (Typikon, ch. 17 and September 15).

*(Orig. p. 534)*
With Saturday Vespers begins the weekly cycle of Kathismata, for which reason at this Vespers the First Kathisma is appointed, at Sunday Matins the 2nd and 3rd, and so forth. Although the selection of Kathismata for each day of the week, and even for the great feasts falling thereon, doth not correspond with the commemorations of that day, but followeth a simple turn of order—replacing thereby the singing of the entire Psalter within twenty-four hours, bequeathed by the ascetics—nevertheless scarcely could any other section of the Psalter be more suitable for Saturday-Sunday Vespers than the First Kathisma. Probably at the beginning of the Psalter, which was compiled after David by the collectors of his psalms and his imitators, the most poetic psalms were placed. Almost all the psalms of the First Kathisma (Psalms 1–8) admit of a Messianic application, foreshadowing under the image of the persecuted David the sufferings of the Savior. Certain of the psalms of this Kathisma, among other things on account of their singular loftiness, are taken as introductory psalms for Church services: the 3rd is the first in the morning Six Psalms; the 4th is the first at Great Compline; the 5th is the first at the First Hour; the 8th Psalm, concluding the First Kathisma, stands out among the rest by the loftiness of its poetry. Especially appropriate unto the present Vespers is the First Stasis (*Slava*) of the First Kathisma, containing Psalms 1–3, which stand in close connection one with another. "While the 1st Psalm praiseth the true servant of God, not determining whether this description is only ideal or whether in reality there existeth such an ever-green tree, and thereupon depicts the contrast unto him, without indicating the possibility and manner of salvation for those who walk along the false path unto destruction, the 2nd Psalm in expressions reminiscent of the 1st" [^2147] pointeth out in the invincible Anointed One of God and His dominion the sole salvation for the world; herein, unto the description given in the 2nd Psalm of the external activity of this Anointed One, in the 3rd Psalm there is added the depiction of His internal state. "The first three psalms of the Psalter," saith St. Symeon of Thessalonica, "refer preeminently unto the Lord; for the incarnate Word of God

=== LEAF p639 ===
Himself was in truth the one blessed man—the nations rose up against Him—they that afflict Him were multiplied, His revilers and persecutors... But He—if He fell asleep by death, yet arose as from slumber" [^2148]. In the Roman Catholic Church Psalms 1–3 are employed alone out of the entire Psalter at Paschal Matins and are repeated at Matins of all eight days of the feast of Pascha [^2149].

## Manner of Execution of "Blessed is the Man"

It is no wonder if for such a Kathisma, and especially for its first antiphon, the Typikon appoints a particularly solemn execution. Passing over in silence the singing, or at least the melodic models, for all the Kathismata, the present Typikon indicates a chant tone only for this one and the 17th Kathisma: "Then we stichologize the Psalter in Tone 8, the First Kathisma, **Blessed is the man**, the first antiphon; the second and third antiphons we sing in the tone of the day." The present Kathisma is appointed, in this *(Orig. p. 535)* manner, to be sung in its diverse sections with diverse melodies, and the first antiphon in Tone 8—that same solemn tone in which the Introductory Psalm of Great Vespers is sung. Moreover, the first antiphon is sung with the refrain *Alleluia*, as are elsewhere only the Polyeleos psalms and sometimes the 17th Kathisma—a refrain employed among the ancient Hebrews at Pascha and the great feasts (see Introductory Chapter, p. 12) and forbidden among Roman Catholics during fasts. Concerning the refrain *Alleluia* unto the first antiphon of the First Kathisma, as also unto the Polyeleos psalms, the present Typikon and liturgical books do not speak, yet its use is founded upon musical tradition transmitted by neumatic and musical monuments. In Znamenny and Greek Chants *Alleluia* at **"Blessed is the man"** is appointed once, in Kievan Chant thrice [^2150].
