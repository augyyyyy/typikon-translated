precious vessel, wrapped in a towel. As though, forsooth, such men render great honor unto the most blessed martyrs, whom they deem in need of being illuminated, whereas they are illuminated by the Lamb Who sitteth upon the throne with all the brightness of His majesty!'

Concerning the vigils in honor of the martyrs, Vigilantius thought 'that they ought not to be permitted, lest they appear frequently to celebrate Pascha' (unto which Blessed Jerome replieth: 'Therefore also on the Sunday day sacrifices ought not to be offered unto Christ, lest they frequently celebrate the Pascha of the Resurrection of the Lord'), and in view of 'abuses of them (the vigils) on the part of youths and wanton women, frequently observed at night' (Blessed Jerome retorteth: 'even at Paschal vigils something similar is frequently observed'); Vigilantius likewise said, having in view vigils for martyrs, that 'except for Pascha, Alleluia ought never to be sung.' An adversary of monasticism and asceticism, Vigilantius 'at secular banquets preached against the fast of the saints, and philosophizing over the wine cups and feasting on pastries, he delighteth in the melody of the psalms, so that only over banquets doth he deign to listen unto the songs of David and Asaph and the sons of Korah' [^1486]. Against monasticism and asceticism in general, the former monk Jovinian also arose (in Rome and Milan); in his treatise against him (composed in 392 AD), Blessed Jerome attributeth unto him, among others, the following theses: 'virgins, widows, and married women, once washed in Christ, if they differ not among themselves in other works, possess equal merit'; 'between abstinence from meats and the receiving of them with thanksgiving there is no difference whatever' [^1487]. Directed against the ascetical tendencies of the time was likewise the Council of Gangra (mid-IV century). The very impotence of these movements and their lack of lasting result (concerning Vigilantius we know solely thanks unto Jerome) demonstrated their incompatibility with the spirit of Christianity [^1488]. — No less important an achievement of the IV–V centuries in the domain of divine worship was the unification of the forms thereof throughout the entire Christian world. Such unity in divine worship had not existed in Christianity either before or after. Eusebius falleth into no ordinary rhetorical exaggeration of his when he thus characterizeth the liturgical services of Constantine the Great: 'Now the inhabitants of the West are edified by his Godly instructions at the selfsame hours of time with the dwellers of the East, and they that populate the North sing the selfsame hymns with the inhabitants of the South' [^1489].

## VI–VIII Centuries

### Character of the Period

=== LEAF p272 ===
These centuries may be denominated the most decisive and definitive period in the formation of our liturgical Typikon. Both the daily office (the diurnal and nocturnal cycle) and the church year now receive in all essentials their present form. Unto the share of subsequent times there remained only the elaboration of both in secondary particulars. Yet our information concerning the energetic labor of this period in the realm of the church rule is exceedingly meager, yielding substantially unto the periods preceding and subsequent (from the IX century onwards).

The chief, if not exclusive, agent in the development of the liturgical Typikon became now monasticism. Being so characteristic a phenomenon of church life in the IV–V centuries, it now acquireth an even stronger influence upon that life; from it, chiefly, are replenished the ranks of the higher hierarchy; it nourisheth enlightenment and literature; it exerteth an influence upon the resolution of the most weighty theological questions. If the monasticism of this period no longer displayeth such giants of the spirit as in its nascent era, it nevertheless continueth to astonish the world with ascetical feats (St. Mary of Egypt, † 522 AD; St. Symeon of the Miraculous Mountain, † 596 AD), inventeth new forms thereof (holy folly for Christ's sake—St. Symeon the Fool, † 590 AD; hesychasm and holy silence—St. John the Hesychast, † 558 AD), and revealeth examples of martyrdom unknown unto the former period (the slaughter of the fathers in Sinai and Palestine) [^1490]. All of this, together with the extraordinary numerical growth of monasticism, renders it a mighty power in the Church, the chief nerve of her life. But almost the greatest influence of monasticism out of all church life was destined to be felt upon divine worship. It is not surprising, therefore, that all the information that hath reached us from this epoch concerning divine worship is confined precisely unto monastic worship. — If a comparative evaluation be made of contemporary monasticism according unto the regions of its dissemination, the first place must be assigned unto Palestinian and Sinaitic monasticism. How much more significant these centers were in comparison with others (for example, with Constantino*(Orig. p. 287)*nople), is evident from the fact that they alone left behind a literary monument. Whereas concerning the monasteries of other places from this epoch we possess almost no information, Sinaitic and Palestinian monasticism of the VI–VII centuries possesseth its own patericon in the form of the *Spiritual Meadow* (*Pratum Spirituale*) of John Moschos, and Palestinian monasticism possesseth a most detailed history covering almost this entire period. The latter, moreover, attracted unto itself the attention, favor, and lavish donations of the Byzantine emperors. Consequently, if the monasticism of this era took upon itself the development of Christian divine worship, then the greatest contribution here was destined to be made by the Palestinian monasteries. Among these latter, the central figure of this epoch is St. Sabas the Sanctified (*prp. Savva Osviashchennyi*). It is not surprising if literary tradition traceth back unto him, above all others, our present type of divine worship.

### St. Sabas the Sanctified

=== LEAF p273 ===
St. Sabas the Sanctified († 532 AD) is the representative of that new type of asceticism and monasticism which preferred active life unto the contemplative ('by action hast thou attained unto contemplation', according unto the expression of the church hymn): the expansion of a network of monasteries and their good ordering—materially, juridically (through petitioning the civil authorities for privileges on their behalf), and internally (in discipline and preeminently in liturgical order)—were the work of his entire life. The *Life of St. Sabas* was described by his disciple, the Palestinian monk of the VI century, Cyril of Scythopolis [^1491]. By origin a wealthy Cappadocian, St. Sabas began his monastic life in an Asia Minor monastery, where he 'learned the Psalter and the structure of the cenobitic rule'; but having visited Palestine when already eighteen years of age, he became a disciple of St. Euthymius, who according unto his custom entrusted him for his initial formation unto the cenobium of St. Theoctistus. Upon the death of the latter, Sabas (now thirty years of age), with the blessing of St. Euthymius, began a solitary life (without quitting the cenobium of St. Theoctistus), spending five days at a time without food in complete solitude; and after the death of Euthymius he chose for his director St. Gerasimus, who received him immediately as a cell-dweller (*kelliotes*) into his lavra. When St. Sabas was keeping one of the Great Fasts in the desert after the example of SS. Theoctistus and Euthymius, the site for his ascetical labors and for his future famous lavra was revealed unto him by a vision: it was a cave situated upon a sheer precipice of rock, such that one had to ascend into it by a rope. After five years of solitary life here, hermits began to flock unto St. Sabas, and when their number reached seventy, he erected a tower and a small church (*eukterion*) with a consecrated altar, in which an invited presbyter celebrated. Soon, however, by a vision there was revealed unto St. Sabas not far from the lavra 'a great and marvelous cave, having within the appearance of a church of God; for on the eastern side there is a God-fashioned apse (*konche theoktistos*—for the sanctuary), and on the northern side a large chamber in the form of a diaconicon, while from the south there is a wide entrance admitting sufficient light; having embellished through God's assistance this *(Orig. p. 288)* cave, he instituted therein divine worship (*ton kanona*) on Saturdays and Sundays.' This church existeth even unto this day, dedicated in honor of St. Nicholas. When the number of the brethren reached one hundred and fifty, a regular administrative economy was established in the lavra, facilitated by large donations. In the *Life of St. Sabas* it is not stated that he subjected those entering his monastery unto a probationary trial (especially unto so severe a testing as was practiced in Egypt); the consequence of this, in all probability, was that a portion of the monks began to act willfully and petitioned the patriarch to give them another hegumen; but the patriarch, instead of doing so, ordained Sabas a presbyter. Following this, there occurred two further instances of rebellion among the brethren against Sabas, after the first of which Sabas was compelled to withdraw temporarily from the monastery, and after the second, sixty monks departed from the lavra and founded another monastery. Perhaps under the influence of these occurrences, St. Sabas instituted in his lavra, after the example of SS. Euthymius and Gerasimus, a cenobium for

=== LEAF p274 ===
novice monks, from whence he transferred them unto the status of cell-dwellers (*kelliotai*) only after they had mastered the Psalter and the rule of psalmody (*kanon tes psalmodias*). Gradually St. Sabas founded three lavras: besides the first, the Great Lavra, also the New Lavra, founded near Tekoa by the disciples of St. Sabas who had willfully departed, but subsequently subjected by him unto his authority; and the Lavra of the Seven Mouths (*Heptastomos*), so named after the torrent beside which it stood; and in addition four cenobia. Attached unto the lavras and cenobia, Sabas established large hospices (*xenodochia*); these latter he founded likewise in Jerusalem (where for this purpose he purchased the cells of the *spoudaioi*, when a monastery was constructed for them by the patriarch) and in Jericho. Besides rich donations (even on the part of the emperors), St. Sabas expended upon this also the entire estate handed over unto him by his mother when the latter embraced the monastic state. St. Sabas was appointed archimandrite of all the Palestinian lavras, and as the most prominent figure in the monasticism of that age, he was twice sent successfully by the patriarchs on missions of the Jerusalem Church unto Constantinople—unto Emperors Anastasius and Justinian.

### The Structure of the Sabbaite Monasteries

Concerning the internal organization of the Sabbaite monasteries there are very few data in his *Life* by Cyril of Scythopolis. Mentioned as officials are: the superiors (*archegoi*) of the monasteries (§ 58), the steward or bursar (*oikonomos*, § 59), and the canonarch, who, among other duties, struck the wooden semantron (*krouein*) for divine service (§ 43) and apparently administered economic affairs jointly with the steward (§ 59). All of them performed all things with the knowledge and counsel of St. Sabas (*ibid.*). He enjoyed such veneration that monks of other monasteries, upon meeting him, would fall down at his feet; he would bless them, raise them up, and embrace them; such was the case, at least, at the first meeting of St. Sabas with Gregory of Scythopolis (§ 75). The striking for service was performed with his knowledge and permission (*gnome*), but at appointed hours (§ 43). The services (*kanon*, *kanon tes psalmodias*) were celebrated by a numerous multitude (*plethos*) of brethren with sweet chanting (*meta melous hedyphonou*), with incensations, and the kindling of candles (*ibid.*). Among the services there is mentioned a certain nocturnal office (§ 43, designated simply as *kanon*—office), apparently Matins, unto which is applied the designation *ta lychnika* (§ 60, see below); there is mentioned next the Third Hour: while residing in *(Orig. p. 289)* the palace as an ambassador of the patriarch unto Emperor Justinian, when the emperor was discussing with the quaestor the petition of the patriarch and Sabas, the latter, 'withdrawing a little distance, was reading secretly the psalms of David and celebrating the divine office (*leitourgian*) of the Third Hour'; and unto the reproach of his deacon for his lack of attention unto the sovereign, he replied: 'They, my son, are doing their business, and we likewise must do ours' (§ 73). Consequently, there were also services of the Sixth and Ninth Hours. They communicated, apparently, daily at the Ninth Hour, after which followed the common table and Vespers: when St. Sabas, with the hegumens of the Monastery of St. Euthymius and of the Patriarchal Monastery at Jericho, attended the last days of the life of Patriarch Elias of Jerusalem († 517 AD), the latter daily from the dismissal of Matins (*apo men tes ton lychnikon apolyseos*) did not show himself unto them until the ninth hour, but around the ninth hour came forth and communicated (*ekoinonei*) with them, partook of food, and after Vespers (*to lychnikon*) retired once again (§ 60). When Armenians began to enter the Lavra of St. Sabas, he initially assigned unto them for divine worship in their native tongue the small church (the first of those constructed), but upon the erection and consecration of the great church (the third church, dedicated in honor of the All-Praised Mother of God and Ever-Virgin Mary), he

> 'transferred them from the small church for the performance of the rule of psalmody into the God-fashioned (second) church, bidding them to recite (*legein*) among themselves, at the Armenian synaxes (*synaxesin*), the Gospel (*to megaleion*) [^1492] and the rest of the order of service, but at the time of the Divine Oblation (*proskomides*) to go unto the Greeks for the reception of the Divine Mysteries; when, however, certain of them

=== LEAF p276 ===
> ventured to utter the Trisagion hymn with the addition contrived by Peter surnamed the Fuller: "Who wast crucified for us", the divine elder, filled with indignation, commanded them to chant this hymn in Greek, according unto the tradition of the Catholic Church' (§ 32).

Here the discourse is clearly concerning the Liturgy of the Catechumens, which consequently appeared as something completely independent alongside the Liturgy of the Faithful. 'Sabas ordained that on Saturday the assembly should take place in the church created by God, and on the Sunday day in the church of the Mother of God, and decreed that in both churches on every Sunday day and Lord's feast there should unfailingly be a vigil from evening until morning (*aparaleiptos apo opse heos proi agrypnian*)' (*ibid.*). On the Sunday day, after the divine service (*he tou Theou leitourgia*), for the fathers assembled from the cells situated in the vicinity of the monastery, a repast (*diaklysma*) was offered by the steward of the lavra, consisting of bread, olive oil, honey, cheese, and other victuals (§ 59).

Among the festivals, mention is made of the commemorations of St. Anthony the Great, St. Euthymius on January 20, Palm Sunday, and Pascha:

> 'As St. Euthymius had the custom on the fourteenth day of the month of January to withdraw into the deep desert and there spend the Forty Days' Fast, so also Sabas, having modified this custom slightly, went forth from the lavra after the passing of that day on which the memory of St. Anthony is celebrated, celebrated the memory of the great Euthymius, which is observed on the twentieth day, and after this departed into the deep desert. There until the feast of Palms he was removed from all intercourse with men. And this he did almost every year.'

Together with him there went forth for the same purpose other ascetics of the lavra *(Orig. p. 290)* inclined toward solitude. 'During all the days of the fast Sabas remained without food, contenting himself with the communion of the Holy Mysteries on Saturday and the Sunday day', and spending all his time in prayer and in the fulfillment of 'his rule of psalmody' [^1493]. On the Feast of the Dedication of the Church of the Resurrection, 'according unto the custom of the hegumens', St. Sabas was accustomed to go with certain brethren of his lavra unto Jerusalem; here on one of the Sundays an anathema against heretics was pronounced: the bishops and hegumens ascended the ambo and proclaimed: 'If anyone receiveth not the four Councils, and likewise the four Gospels, let him be anathema', or 'Anathema unto Severus and his accomplices.' (This ceremony was celebrated in the Jerusalem Church of St. Stephen the Protomartyr, because that church

=== LEAF p277 ===
could contain all the assembling monks, who numbered up to ten thousand; § 56).

### St. Theodosius the Cenobiarch

As his fellow-laborer in planting monasticism in Palestine, St. Sabas had St. Theodosius the Cenobiarch († 529 AD). Likewise a Cappadocian, formerly a reader, who had received a blessing for monastic life from St. Symeon the Stylite, St. Theodosius founded his cenobium not in caves, like SS. Chariton and Sabas, but in an open place, six versts from Jerusalem and from the principal Lavra of St. Sabas: the cenobium of St. Theodosius was more populous than the Great Lavra of St. Sabas, having in his time up to seven hundred monks and distinct churches for the Greeks, the Bessi [^1494], the Armenians, and the possessed; the 'rule of psalmody' was celebrated in each church seven times a day, but the Liturgy only in the Greek church. The successor of St. Theodosius, Abba Sophronius, quadrupled his monastery. In the XII century, unto the Russian pilgrim Daniel it presented the appearance of an entire city [^1495], but was definitively destroyed by the Crusaders, whereas the Lavra of St. Sabas existeth even unto this day. During the lifetime of St. Theodosius, who was appointed alongside St. Sabas as superior of all Palestinian cenobia, his cenobium was a noble rival and complement unto the lavras of St. Sabas, leaving its imprint upon monastic life. Sabas and Theodosius, in the words of the biographer of the latter [^1496], 'were *(Orig. p. 291)* of one soul and one mind, breathing each other's presence more than the air, so that the inhabitants of Jerusalem, seeing their unanimity and accord in relation unto God, denominated them a new apostolic pair, like unto the pair of the Apostles Peter and John' (in character St. Sabas resembled the Apostle Peter, and Theodosius—John). Yet at the same time Sabas was wont to say unto Theodosius: 'Thou art hegumen of boys (*paidion*), whereas I am hegumen over hegumens; for each of my disciples is an independent ascetic and is hegumen of his own cell' [^1497].

Thus the *Life of St. Sabas* confirmeth the later tradition concerning his composition of the so-called Jerusalem Typikon only by: 1) the report that he introduced Sunday and festal vigils which lasted in his monastery from evening until morning, and not merely from midnight, as was the practice in secular churches (for example, in the Jerusalem Church of the Resurrection and in other monasteries, even the austere Egyptian ones); the rule hitherto had known such all-night vigils for the most part only two or three times a year [^1498]; 2) the notice concerning the unfailing exactitude wherewith the liturgical hours were observed under St. Sabas. If this be so, then by St. Sabas was laid the foundation at least for two distinctive features of the later Jerusalem Typikon.

### The Testament of St. Sabas

=== LEAF p278 ===
From St. Sabas there have survived also written rules of monastic life, in all probability those which, according unto his biographer, he delivered before his death unto his successor Melitas: 'he gave him a testament inviolably to preserve the rules (*paradoseis*) introduced by him in his monasteries, delivering them unto him in writing (*engraphos*, § 76).' These rules are of interest as being among the most ancient ktetorial typika given unto monasteries by their founders, which served as models for our Typikon. These rules are placed in a XII-century manuscript Jerusalem Typikon (Sinai Library, No. 1096; and in a XV-century manuscript, No. 531) between its menaion and triodion sections, and fully corresponding unto the data of the *Life of St. Sabas*, they may be recognized as authentic, with the exception of certain interpolations (for example, the addition of 'venerable' unto the name of Sabas, etc.) [^1499]. The rules bear the title: 'The Pattern, Rule, and Law (*Typos kai paradosis kai nomos*) of the Honorable Lavra of St. Sabas.'

> 'It is necessary to observe the rules established by our venerable and blessed father Sabas. — In no case is there to be received into the Lavra either a beardless youth or a eunuch; and women are not permitted to enter for prayer not only into the Lavra, but even into the metochia [^1500], and especially into the second door of the great *(Orig. p. 292)* metochion. Whosoever among the monks of the Lavra shall be observed entering the monastic gynaeceum for the purpose of eating or drinking, or remaining there either under the pretext of guarding it, or for the tonsure, or for confession of thoughts (*logismous*), or who writeth unto women and receiveth letters from them, and in general hath familiarity (*gnosin*) with women, or contracteth spiritual kinship (*synteknias*), such a one must be expelled from the brotherhood, as a cause of offense (*skandalon*) not only unto Christians, but also unto pagans. — Neither Georgians (*Iberas*), nor Syrians, nor Franks (*Phrangous*) [^1501] shall have the right to celebrate the full Liturgy in their churches; but unto those assembling therein it is permitted to chant the Hours and the Typika (*ta typika*), to read the Epistle and the Gospel in their native tongue, and after this to gather in the great church and communicate together with the entire brotherhood of the divine, immaculate, and life-creating Mysteries. — Over the tomb of the saint (that is, of Sabas) the pro-liturgy (*proleitourgian*) is to be celebrated by the presbyter appointed for this purpose. — No one at all shall have the right to depart from the Lavra on Saturday in view of the all-night vigil that is to be celebrated, except for monastic necessity and business; but after the vigil, with the consent of the superior or the ecclesiarch, let him go according unto his necessity. Unto him that was not present at the preceding vigil nothing

=== LEAF p279 ===
> is accepted as an excuse, because the entire preceding week was sufficient for him to attend unto his needs; and this (departure on business), if possible, is to be done once a month. — Those departing into the desert and dwelling therein, if they do so with the consent and blessing of the superior, are to be received according unto their needs; but if they have withdrawn willfully and out of a desire to live according unto their own will (*ex idiorrhythmias*), then upon their return they are not to be received. — Whosoever, by the Providence of God and by the judgment of the patriarch, should be elected either as metropolitan, or as bishop, or as hegumen unto another monastery, or elevated unto whatever rank of the Great Church, shall not have the right to sell or donate outright the cell belonging unto him in the Lavra or in the metochion; but they belong unto the holy monastery, and brethren who are worthy and needy may receive them from the hegumen. This same order must be observed also with regard unto deceased brethren: without the consent and will of the hegumen, no one (among the brethren) hath the right to bequeath unto his disciple his own cell. — If discord should arise among certain of the brethren and the evil should so increase therefrom that someone should dare to strike others and lay hands upon them, then such persons, if they do not reconcile, and even if they do reconcile, yet if it be necessary in the interests of the monastery, are to be cast out as disturbers of peace and adversaries of the commandment of Christ; for "the servant of the Lord must not strive", as saith the divine Apostle [2 Tim. 2:24]. — Whosoever among the brethren shall be detected in drunkenness, or in quarrels with anyone, or in insults against anyone, or in forming cabals (*hetairias*) and factions (*systrophas*), such a one must either be corrected, or else expelled from the monastery. In like manner also he who, during the performance of a monastic obedience, should be detected in theft, must be removed from his *(Orig. p. 293)* obedience, with the assigning unto him of a penance for correction and repentance, and he must not go forth from his cell, except at the appointed hours of liturgical assemblies (*ton synaxeon*) and prayers. — Concerning those who intend to practice hesychia and not appear in church, and likewise concerning those received for complete reclusion, we desire that they assemble with the rest of the brethren solely at all-night vigils; but in cities, villages, at the shrines of holy

=== LEAF p280 ===
> men, and in other notable places, let them be in complete solitude, for silence in deed can bring profit far better than in words. — Guests coming from without, (especially) if any of them desire to make a beginning in the Lavra, we shall receive and in every way console for the space of seven days; if, however, the guests be of those dwelling in the city (that is, Jerusalem) or of the surrounding inhabitants and have come for veneration, then three days are sufficient for them; the cleansing (of the monastic buildings is necessary), in view of the multitude of poor folk arriving daily. — Inasmuch as demons are accustomed to cause harm during the elections of hegumens, and to stir up strife and contentions between the two nationalities, that is, between the Greeks (*Rhomiaioi*) and the Syrians, giving birth therefrom unto scandal, we therefore decree: let no one henceforth from among the Syrians be elevated unto the rank of hegumen; but we ordain and agree that as stewards and docharioi (that is, those having charge of the property of the monastery) and in other obediences, Syrians be preferred, as men capable thereof and active by their very nature' [^1502].

### The Fate of the Sabbaite Lavra and Its Liturgical Significance

The Lavra of St. Sabas even after his death did not lose its central and leading significance in Palestinian monasticism, and consequently in monasticism in general, although it experienced not a few internal and external tribulations. Thus Origenism almost penetrated into it in the person of Hegumen George in the VI century. In 614 AD it was plundered by the armies of the Persian King Chosroes, during which the monks were tortured to extract where the treasures of the lavra were hidden, and forty-four of them were slain; the monks who escaped from the Persians restored the lavra with the assistance of Modestus, Archimandrite of the Cenobium of St. Theodosius (subsequently Patriarch of Jerusalem), who consoled them with the passage from the Wisdom of Solomon, Chapter 3, concerning the untimely demise of the righteous (a passage that formed a paroemia at the service for venerable fathers). Nevertheless, after the Persian devastation, information concerning the lavra until the VIII century almost ceaseth; from this period, however, there is one not unimportant piece of news concerning it: that its Archimandrite John was the first to sign the epistle of the Palestinian monks unto Pope Martin on the occasion of the Lateran Council of 649 AD convened by him against the Monothelites [^1503]. But during this period of its life the lavra rendered the greatest of its services unto the Church, and in particular unto the liturgical Typikon, by nurturing the most renowned hymnographers: St. Andrew of Crete († ca. 712 AD), St. John of Damascus († ca. 780 AD), *(Orig. p. 294)* St. Cosmas, Bishop of Maiuma († ca. 787 AD),
