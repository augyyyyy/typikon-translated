The extent to which this rule was implemented in the Athonite Iviron Lavra even before the appearance there of a complete copy of it, under St. Euthymius, its second hegumen († 1012 AD), is evident from the latter's Life compiled by his successor and translator of the described "Synaxarion", George the Hagiorite (Mtatsmindeli). According to the biographer, Euthymius stood in church without leaning on a staff and without leaning against a wall. The brethren who stood by the wall were likewise forbidden to lean against it. The younger brethren stood in the middle of the church in two or three rows, reverently, with their hands lowered; when prescribed, they sat upon their benches, which likewise stood in the middle of the church in two or three rows. Euthymius appointed two God-fearing epitropoi-overseers: one senior, and the other junior, subordinate to the first. The junior epitropos was dispatched to the cells and brought to the church any brother remaining in his cell. A brother who did not arrive by the Six Psalms Euthymius judged according to the rules of Father [Theodore] in the following manner: one who labored much by day and had no time to sleep he did not punish—his daytime labors were credited to him as a prayerful ascetic struggle. But those having leisure or performing petty tasks, as well as priests, deacons, and readers, he subjected to such punishment: the young he compelled to make 100 prostrations before the altar, while the old and infirm he deprived of wine and food for that day. During divine service the senior epitropos stood at the doors and inquired of any departing brother concerning the reason for his leaving: if no essential need appeared, he did not permit him to leave; but in the event of genuine necessity

=== LEAF p524 ===
he permitted him to leave, only on condition of returning immediately. In the event that one who went out was tardy, he was subjected to a reprimand; if he was tardy a second time, on that day he was not admitted to the refectory, but was kept on dry bread or sentenced to 100 prostrations. To those laboring tirelessly the holy father permitted leaving the church after the Six Psalms and resting, but with the strict obligation to return at the second bell. To the weak among the brethren he allowed standing in the narthex and leaning upon a staff, and to the sick even sitting. Younger brethren who talked, laughed, or took each other by the hand were immediately reproved; if they were caught a second time, they were reported to the holy father, who judged them strictly. One guilty of a quarrel or fight was brought to the cell of the holy father, who commanded him to lie upon the ground; the guilty one, having bowed, lay down; one of the brethren held him by the head, another by the feet, while a third administered to him over his cassock 30, 40, or 60 blows with a belt. If anyone interceded for the one being punished, he was subjected to the very same punishment. The punished, kneeling, bowed to the holy father and with tears in their eyes begged him for forgiveness. After this they lay upon the ground and remained in such a posture for two or three weeks (?). From time to time the holy father came to them and consoled them by word and deed; to the same he also urged other monastery officers. The executor of the punishment likewise consoled the punished one and presented him with a cassock or a shoulder-cloak, or some other necessary item. The punished regarded the chastisement borne by them as if they had endured it from the hands of Christ Himself. Above all, the holy father took care that no one should give false testimony or witness in any matter without knowing its circumstances; one convicted of this he expelled from the monastery. The blessed father provided the brethren with everything, down to needle and thread, and thereby deprived everyone of occasion to sell or acquire anything without his command; a brother could not transfer his property to another without the consent of the holy father. If anyone transgressed the will of the holy father and acquired clothing or anything valuable, he strictly rebuked him. More than once we saw how he burned a shoulder-cloak, a garment, or a cowl in the monastery precinct. Often he said to the brethren: "The saints denied themselves even water. Is it not enough for us to take food twice a day, and that in abundance—with wine and varied dishes?" Therefore if anyone purchased wine and oil, the entire purchase was poured into the refuse pit.
