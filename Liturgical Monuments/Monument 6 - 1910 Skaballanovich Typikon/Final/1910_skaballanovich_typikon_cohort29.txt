### The Sunday Vigil on Sinai and in Palestine: The Visit to Nilus by Moschus and Sophronius

The Life of St. Sabas, like all accounts concerning Palestinian monasticism—in contrast unto reports concerning, for example, Egyptian monasticism—containeth absolutely no data concerning the divine services there. This can apparently be explained solely by the fact that the latter in Palestinian monasteries, as is directly known concerning the Syro-Mesopotamian monasteries [^1504], followed the customary rite, the order of secular parish churches. And this latter rite, as we have seen, even in the V century was already quite close unto the present one. For example, at Vespers there was already Ps. 140, the Great Litany and the Litany of Supplication, **"O Gladsome Light"** (*Phos Hilaron / Svete Tikhiy*), **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Kataxioson, Kyrie / Spodobi Gospodi*), **"Now lettest Thou"** (*Nyn apolyeis / Nyně otpushchayeshi*); at Matins, Ps. 62 and Ps. 50, certain Old Testament and New Testament canticles, the Lauds (*Ainoi / Khvalitny*) psalms, **"Glory to God in the highest"** (*Doxa en hypsistois Theo / Slava v vyshnikh Bogu*), and the same litanies. Unto these invariable portions was conjoined a series of hymns, sometimes termed troparia, corresponding unto the given day, as well as corresponding readings from the Old and New Testaments [^1505]. Such, at least, was the order of the divine office in the churches of Syria and Palestine—that is, precisely those in whose bosom arose that branch of monasticism which is regarded as the progenitor of the present Typikon. Into this already established order of divine worship, monasticism needed only to insert the Psalter, inasmuch as the Psalter from antiquity was regarded as the handbook for the monk. This insertion could be effected either in such manner that the hitherto existing psalm element in divine worship might be stretched and expanded, or, without disturbing that element, the Psalter could be externally inserted into divine worship, fashioning from it as it were a distinct monastic office upon the ordinary secular service. The former was accomplished in the liturgical rite of the Western Church; the latter, in the Eastern Typika by the system of kathismata and the kanon (understanding by the latter the Biblical canticles). As unto how this addition was originally effected in the VI century, a precious report concerning this hath been preserved in the narrative of the visit unto the Sinaitic Abba Nilus [^1506] by the Palestinian monks John Moschus, author of the *Spiritual Meadow* (*Pratum Spirituale*), and Sophronius, subsequently Patriarch of Jerusalem—a visit which took place ca. 606 AD. This narrative hath been preserved in certain ancient Paterika (in manuscripts of the Vatican Library) [^1507] and in Nikon of the Black Mountain [^1508]. We cite the narrative in the first recension, with variants from Nikon in parentheses (*italics denote that which is absent in Nikon*):

"Abba John and Abba Sophronius related unto us: before the holy Sunday (*at the dawning of the Lord’s *(Orig. p. 295)* Day*), we went unto Abba Nilus upon Mount Sinai. The elder was up upon the summit

of the mountain with two disciples. Inasmuch as we arrived for Vespers, the elder began: **"Glory to the Father"** (*Slava Ottsu*), and the rest; and after they had said: **"Blessed is the man"** (*Blazhen muzh*) and **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Kyrie ekekraxa / Gospodi vozzvakh*) without troparia, and **"O Gladsome Light"** (*Phos Hilaron / Svete Tikhiy*), and **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Kataxioson, Kyrie / Spodobi Gospodi*), they began **"Now lettest Thou"** (*Nyn apolyeis / Nyně otpushchayeshi*) and the rest. Upon the conclusion of Vespers he set before us a refection. After the refection we began the kanon (rule); and after the Six Psalms (*Hexapsalmos*), having said **"Our Father Who art in the heavens"** (*Otche nash, Izhe yesi na nebesech*), they began the psalms chanted melodiously. And when they had said the first stasis of 50 psalms, the elder began **"Our Father Who art in the heavens"** (*+ the elder began to recite the Epistle*), fifty times **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Gospodi pomiluy*); and when they had sat down, one of his disciples read the Catholic Epistle of James. Rising up, we again began the second stasis of 50 psalms; and upon the completion of the 50 psalms he gave [the book] unto another brother, and that one read from the same book the Catholic Epistle of Peter. And rising up, they began the 3rd stasis; and upon the completion of the 150 psalms, and after they had said **"Our Father"** and **"Lord, have mercy"**, they sat down, and the elder gave me the book, and I read the Catholic Epistle of John. And rising up, they began the Canticles (*ōdas*) of Moses (softly) chanted melodiously without troparia; and neither at the third nor at the sixth did they make an intercalary hymn (*mesōdion*), but **"Our Father"** and **"Lord, have mercy"**; and having said the Lauds (*Ainoi / Khvalitny*) psalms without troparia, they began **"Glory to God in the highest"** with the **"I believe"** (*+ in one God*) and **"Our Father"** and **"Lord, have mercy"**. The elder then added: 'O Son and Word of God (*+ Lord*), Jesus Christ, our God, have mercy upon us, and help, and save our souls!' And when we said 'Amen', we sat down. And the elder saith unto me: 'He that preserveth not the order of the Catholic and Apostolic Church, let him be anathema both in this age and in that which is to come!' And I say unto him: 'How then dost thou not recite (*+ from the night*) at the Vespers of the holy Sunday troparia at **"Lord, I have cried"**, nor a troparion at **"O Gladsome Light"** (*+ at **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"***), nor at the kanon **"God is the Lord"**, nor at the psalmody of the Sunday kathisma psalms, nor troparia at the Song of the Three Holy Children, nor at the Magnificat (*at the Gospel*) **"Let every breath"**, nor at the Doxology **"The Resurrection of the Savior"** (*+ which we praise*)?' Unto the inquiries of the visitors the elder replied that the reason for such departures from the general ecclesiastical Typikon in his prayer rule was his lack of any sacred orders. 'All that thou hast spoken is the office of reader and chanter, subdeacon and presbyter, and in general of those possessing ordination; whereas those not possessing the latter ought not to dare to perform this. For

this very reason the ecclesiastical order appointeth the reader and the chanter, the subdeacons and the presbyters: the chanter, in order to sing and to lead off with singing and voice and chant, and "to guide the people in the **'Holy God'**, and the prokeimena, and the precenting, and the sanctuary hymns, and at the entrance of the Mysteries the Cherubic Hymn, and the Communion verses"; the reader, for the paroemias (proverbial), prophetic, and apostolic books; the subdeacons and deacons, to minister at the altar; and the presbyters, to celebrate the sacred mysteries, to accomplish the rites, and to baptize. I too will ask of you an 'ecclesiastical head' [capital question]: tell me, in the divine offices, evening supplications, night vigils, and morning services, who ought to precent and lead off the alleluia, the introits, the sessional hymns, the readings, and the antiphons?" We said unto him: "The readers, chanters, and subdeacons." The elder said: "Rightly have ye spoken. We said unto him: "This is the office of presbyters; in the ecclesiastical tradition we have received that this be proclaimed by the priests." Adding then that he in this manner doth not cast aside the ecclesiastical order, but on the contrary by his manner of action expresseth a singular reverence toward it, looking upon it as something analogous unto the heavenly hierarchy, the elder noteth in passing also (like the aforementioned Egyptian fathers) that "unto hesychasts and those weeping for their own sins, troparia are not fitting"; yet he repeateth that he singeth them not principally because, in his judgment, they usurp the priesthood, like King Uzziah and Uzzah (who upheld the falling Ark), who, lacking ordination, "proclaim the psalms with song and utterance, and the people at the ends of the verses respond with voice and melody, usurping the prokeimena and sanctuary precentings, and appointing themselves chanters, readers, and subdeacons."

This narrative affordeth us a conception simultaneously of three types of divine worship of that period: the Sinaitic eremitic—and monastic Sinai was the spiritual offspring of Egypt; the Palestinian monastic; and the cathedral-parish type. The first is described in all completeness of detail. Concerning the composition of the second, one may deduce from that which arouseth perplexity in the first on the part of

=== LEAF p284 ===
the visitors: unto them it seemeth strange that there should be an absence of "troparia" at **"Lord, I have cried"** (stichera), at **"O Gladsome Light"** (the prokeimenon), at **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (the aposticha), as well as the absence of **"God is the Lord"** "at the beginning of the kanon" (of Matins), of Sunday sessional hymns, of intercalary hymns (*mesōdia*) at the 3rd and 6th canticles (sessional hymns or something similar), and furthermore the absence of troparia at the Biblical canticles—especially at the Song of the Three Children and the Magnificat (presently the 8th and 9th odes of the kanon), of **"Let every breath"**, and of the Sunday hymn (**"The Resurrection of the Savior which we praise"**) at the Doxology. In Palestinian and other monasteries, consequently, all this was already in existence. But the most valuable indications are afforded us by the narrative concerning the cathedral-parish, secular divine service—valuable in view of the fact that no other information concerning it hath survived from this era. According unto the narrative, this divine service was reduced principally unto singing: there were sung—and evidently unto artful and intricate melodies—a) psalms, b) prokeimena and verses corresponding unto them: alleluia, antiphons, **"God is the Lord"**, **"Let every breath"**, c) stichera, sessional hymns, and troparia. The chanting of all this was initiated before the altar by the chanter, who was followed by the people singing along on appointed verses. Festal divine service differed from weekday service both in composition (for example, by the fact that instead of **"God is the Lord"**, alleluia was sung) and in the celebrants: whereas on weekdays the chanting of the most important portions was initiated by the chanters, readers, and subdeacons, on feasts it was begun by the priests. The subdeacons appear chiefly in the role of chanters. Reading was almost analogous in performance unto chanting. All these are characteristics of the so-called Chanted Office (*asmatike akolouthia*), detailed orders of which have been preserved from the IX and subsequent centuries, whose essence consisted precisely in the solemn singing *(Orig. p. 297)* of psalms, antiphons, prokeimena, and similar short verses, in frequent prayers (broad participation of the presbyter in the divine service), and in the congregational execution of diverse refrains unto the psalms. This office, especially in the form in which it is presented by the present narrative, hath much in common with the Western liturgical worship of that era. One of the common features of both, as we shall see, was the paucity of hymns of Christian composition (the dominance of biblical material): throughout the entire divine service there would occur only one or two such hymns (troparia), or canticles. Yet the divine worship in the homeland of the actual narrators already stands upon the transition unto the later type (the present one): in it are found many hymns of the latter kind—stichera, sessional hymns, and troparia for the kanon.

### Kontakia and Kanons

As a transitional stage from the earlier chanted type of divine worship (with the predominance of psalms and their verses in the form of antiphons, etc.) unto the new type with the predominance of stichera, one must suppose the existence of the kontakarian system of divine worship. Unto the most ancient and solitary hymn at

=== LEAF p285 ===
divine worship—the troparion, which expressed in brief the essence of the liturgical celebration and was sung as a refrain unto the verses of certain psalms (for example, Ps. 50, Ps. 117 [LXX], etc.)—they began to annex a similarly concise hymn, but executed already independently of the psalm and having following upon it a series of similar songs: the kontakion with a series of ikoi. The first creator of kontakia was a reader or chanter of St. Sophia in Constantinople, St. Romanus the Melodist (*ho Melodos*, † first half of the VI century). He composed kontakia and ikoi, in all probability, for all the most important feasts of his era (with great certainty one may ascribe unto him this composition for at least 15 feasts and commemorations of the year) [^1509]. The number of ikoi in St. Romanus for each feast vacillateth between 24 and 14. Consequently, this was a long series of hymns, which in the services of that time occupied the place of stichera and the kanon: in liturgical books containing stichera and the kanon, only a single kontakion and ikos are ever indicated, rather than such a long series thereof. Stichera and especially the kanon appeared already to replace the kontakarian services, and as one of the first creators of kanons one may recognize St. Andrew of Crete († ca. 712 AD).

### The Horologion of the VIII Century

More detailed information concerning the aforementioned third type of divine worship—cathedral-parish worship—from the era of the VI–VIII centuries doth not exist. Only from the IX century and subsequent centuries have offices come down unto us, yet already in conjunction with later accretions—for example, stichera and kanons—which cannot be isolated with scholarly certainty, and therefore a reliable conception of this type of divine worship for the present period cannot be formed. Nevertheless, the exposition of the Chanted Office (*asmatike akolouthia*) of the IX–XVI centuries will simultaneously *(Orig. p. 298)* afford us subsequently certain conjectural suppositions concerning the form which it possessed at this time.

Meanwhile, concerning Palestinian-monastic divine worship of the era under examination, information hath been preserved for its further course as well—from the VIII century. This is the Greek "Horologion according unto the Typikon of the Lavra of Our Holy Father Sabas" (*Hōrologion kata kanona tēs lauras...*) in a manuscript of the VIII–IX century [^1510]. The Horologion was intended for cell use; for which reason it doth not contain not only all parts of the service (for example, litanies, priestly exclamations), but even certain services, such as Matins. Apparently, in lieu of Matins there stands at the beginning of the Horologion the first kathisma. Then follow the services for the 1st hour of the day, the 3rd, 6th, and 9th, the service for Communion of the Presanctified Gifts (corresponding unto the Typika / *Izobrazitel'ny*), Vespers, and the service for the 1st hour of the night (corresponding unto Compline), upon which the Horologion breaketh off. The services are very close unto the present ones. Thus Vespers (*ta hesperina*) consisteth of Ps. 103, the 18th kathisma with 3 *Glorias*, Ps. 140 with **"Glory... Now and ever..."** at the end, **"O Gladsome Light"** (*Phos Hilaron / Svete Tikhiy*), the Alleluia with diverse verses for

=== LEAF p286 ===
different days (at first verses from the present prokeimenon **"The Lord reigneth"** [*Kyrios ebasileusen / Gospod' votsarisia*]), **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Kataxioson, Kyrie / Spodobi Gospodi*), **"Now lettest Thou"** (*Nyn apolyeis / Nyně otpushchayeshi*), and the Trisagion (*Sviatoe Trishchastie*). The service of each hour consisteth of psalms, the number of which is distinct for different hours: for the First—8; for the Third—7; for the Sixth—6; for the Ninth—4; the Alleluia with verses; the troparion (such as exists at present for each hour, excluding the 1st), before which and after which is written its conclusion, which must have served as its refrain; then the verse of the hour, such as exists at present (that is, for the 1st hour [the printed Russian edition has '10-го часа', an obvious misprint for the 1st hour]: **"Order my steps"** [*Stopy moia*], etc.) with the tripling of its first half (as at present at the 1st Hour in Great Lent); and finally—the Trisagion; consequently, that part of the hour following upon the Trisagion did not yet exist. The service for Communion (= the Typika / *Izobrazitel'ny*) consisteth of the Beatitudes with the refrain **"Remember us, O Lord, when Thou comest"** (*Pomiani nas, Gospodi, egda priideshi*), the **"Heavenly Choir"** (*Lik nebesnyi*), the Symbol of Faith (**"I believe"** [*Veruiu*]), **"Our Father"** (*Otche nash*), **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Gospodi pomiluy*) thrice, **"One is Holy"** (*Heis Hagios / Yedin sviat*), **"O taste and see"** (*Geusasthe kai idete / Vkusite i vidite*), Ps. 33—**"I will bless the Lord"** (*Blagoslovliu Gospoda*), prayers after Communion with the intermediate verse: **"Blessed be the name of the Lord"** (*Eiē to onoma Kyriou / Budi imia Gospodne*). The extant portion of Compline hath the following composition: **"Come, let us worship"** (*Priidite poklonimsia*), **"Having traversed the day, I glorify Thee"** (*Tēn hēmeran dielthōn / Den' preshed blagodariu Tia*), Pss. 4, 12, 26, **"Glory..."**, Pss. 30, 90, 6, **"Glory..."**, **"God is with us"** (*Meth' hēmōn ho Theos / S nami Bog*), the Alleluia with verses (for example, **"Look upon me and hear me, O Lord my God"** [*Prizri, uslyshi mia Gospodi Bozhe*]) and troparia changing according unto the days, **"Lord, have mercy"** thrice, and **"Deliver not unto the beasts the soul that doth praise Thee"** (*Ne predazhd' zverem dushu ispovedaiushchuiusia Tebe*).

## Non-Chalcedonian and Oriental Divine Services

During the period of the VI–VIII centuries, the daily office assumed its present form not only in the Orthodox Church, but also in the heretical communities that separated from Her during this time—the Nestorians, Monophysites, and Monothelites, as well as throughout the entire western half of the Christian world. Acquaintance with the liturgical rites of all these churches sheddeth much light upon the history of our divine worship, all the more so since in the period under consideration the latter *(Orig. p. 299)* possessed much that was similar across diverse churches, and in the less cultured lands of the East could be preserved more invariantly than our own.

### Nestorian Divine Service

The Nestorian divine service possesseth almost no invariable elements. Vespers and Matins begin with **"Glory to God in the highest"** (*thrice*) **"and on earth peace, good will among men"**, and **"Our Father"**; then follow the appointed kathismata (the Psalter is divided into 20 kathismata, while the 21st is constituted by the 2 Canticles of Moses) and the so-called "Tone of Matins", that is, according unto the appointed tone (of which there are only two) one of the 2 kathismata—the 12th (with Ps. 67) or the 21st, with hymns assigned unto it on feasts, and on weekdays with litanies and dismissal prayers—all according unto the tone. Besides

=== LEAF p287 ===
this, every Matins hath also Ps. 50, while Sunday Matins hath a Six Psalms (*Hexapsalmos*) composed of Pss. 99, 90, 103 (vv. 1–16), 62, 149, and 150. At every service the Nicene Creed is recited [^1511]. Concerning the composition of the Hours we possess no information.

### Divine Service of the Copts and Abyssinians

The daily office of the Copts (Monophysites) consisteth of Matins, the 3rd, 6th, and 9th Hours, Compline, Vespers, and the Midnight Office. The services begin: "In the name of the Father...", **"Glory to the Father..."**, **"Our Father"**, and the verse "We give thanks unto the Beneficent, the Merciful One", and each consisteth of an initial series of psalms, the Gospel, troparia, and prayers, with certain additions unto this composition for the more important services. Matins hath 19 psalms: 1–6, 8, 11, 12, 14, 15, 18, 24, 26, 62, 66, 69, 112, 142; the Gospel of John 1:1–16; a hymn similar unto our Doxology; the Symbol of Faith; a series of short prayer exclamations; and 2 prayers. Vespers hath 10 psalms: 129, 130, 131, 136, 137, 140, 141, 145, 146, 147; the Gospel of Luke 2:25–30 (concerning the Meeting of the Lord with the Canticle of Symeon the God-Receiver); the Trisagion; the Creed; **"Our Father"**; and 2 prayers. The Midnight Office—7 psalms: 3, 6, 69, 85, 116, 117, 118; the Gospel of Matthew 25—the parable of the 10 virgins; troparia: **"Behold, the Bridegroom cometh"** (*Se Zhenikh griadet*), **"That day"** (*Den' on*), **"An invincible bulwark"** (*Tebe neoborimuiu stenu*); the Trisagion; the Gospel of Luke 7:36 ff. concerning the woman who was a sinner; 3 troparia which are met with among us as penitential sessional hymns; the Gospel of Luke 12:35 ff. concerning the expectation of the Second Coming; penitential troparia; laudatory verses unto the Most Holy Theotokos **"Rejoice"**, resembling our Akathist; and a lengthy prayer. Conversely, Compline (celebrated prior unto Vespers) consisteth solely of psalms and the Gospel. At the Hours are almost all of our troparia and, for the most part, as at Compline, 12 psalms (at the 9th Hour—11) [^1513]. Consequently, for the majority of the services the system of 12 psalms is adopted—as we have seen, the distinctive characteristic of ancient Egyptian monastic worship. — In doctrine and ecclesiastical structure, *(Orig. p. 302)* the Abyssinian Christians, likewise Monophysites, are very close unto the Copts. The divine worship of the two differeth somewhat. Yet the divine worship of the Abyssinians, both contemporary and especially ancient, possessed several types, a complete conception of which is not yet accessible unto European scholarship—owing unto the fact that in Abyssinia liturgical books are in manuscript and only certain of them have been published in recent times. Among others, there hath been published also the Horologion of the Abyssinian Church [^1514], yet in the form which it received by the XIV century under Coptic and Greek influence [^1515]. The services in it are very close unto the Coptic, but more extensive than they, and in places approach our own more closely than the Coptic. Besides this Horologion, in Abyssinia even at the present day horologia of other types are in use, in all probability more ancient. One of these horologia, presently in use in only a single Abyssinian monastery, presenteth services consisting entirely of metrical hymns in honor of the Holy Trinity, the Mother of God, and diverse choirs of saints (at Vespers venerable monastics are praised; at the 1st Hour, the Apostles, etc.) and a multitude of biblical and edifying readings, yet possessing no psalms whatever; in another, in use in certain monasteries, the services consist of psalms, priestly prayers, and biblical readings. It must be noted that the Abyssinians in general take a rather free approach toward the order of divine service, and, while observing its general structure (scheme), fill it with hymns from their diverse collections and with improvisations; for this reason hymnography at their services occupieth an overwhelming position. There exist immense collections of these hymns: the *Deggwa*, the *Psalter of Christ*, the *Psalter of the Virgin*; the hymns are for the most part in verse, some of them resembling our Akathists—presenting a series of praises (*malk'e*), sometimes addressed unto diverse parts of the saint's body and beginning with the word "peace" ("Peace unto thy head, which...", "Peace unto thy hands...") or "rejoice". These are the so-called "likenesses" (*effigies / portraits*); furthermore there are distinguished also antiphons—refrains unto psalms and biblical canticles, and the *wazema*—akin unto our stichera. The greatest stamp of antiquity among Abyssinian liturgical orders is borne by the All-Night Vigil set forth in the book *Me'eraf* ("chapters", that is, Typikon) and the *Deggwa*. Its composition is: Ps. 23 [LXX Ps. 23 / Masoretic Ps. 24] **"The earth is the Lord's"**, titled among the Abyssinians, like Ps. 103, "the beauty of creation"; Ps. 92 **"The Lord reigneth"**, and Ps. 140 with *wazema* (stichera). The psalms are separated by litanies; then follow the appointed section of the Psalter (it is divided into 10 decades), the evening prayer of the priest, paroemias from the Apostolic Epistles, the prokeimenon, and the Gospel. The morning prayers of the priest; (p. 337) the prayer **"Save, O God, Thy people"** (*Spasi Bozhe liudi Tvoia*); the morning Litiya (litany petitions); the Four Psalms: Pss. 62, 91, 5, 64; the appointed section of the Psalter: Ps. 50; the Biblical canticles with refrains from the *Wazema*—namely: the Prayer of Manasseh, the Canticles of the holy Habakkuk, of the Three *(Orig. p. 303)* Children, and of Zacharias; the Lauds (*Ainoi*) psalms with refrains; the Synaxarion (*Senkessar*); Theotokia; the Gospel; the reading of the "Miracles of Mary" (*Ta'amra Maryam*); the Song of Songs (in Lent); improvised or collected refrains (*mawwaddes*) for Sunday (the Great Doxology at the 1st Hour) [^1516].
