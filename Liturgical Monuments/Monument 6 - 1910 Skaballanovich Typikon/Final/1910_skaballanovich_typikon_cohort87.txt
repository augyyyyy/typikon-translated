The *Apostolic Constitutions*, in the appendix unto Book VII, provide the following text of the hymn, entitling it in certain manuscripts "Morning Doxology":
"**Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will among men** (*Slava v vyshnikh Bogu i na zemli mir, v chelovetsekh blagovolenie*). **We praise Thee, we sing unto Thee, we bless Thee, we glorify Thee, we worship Thee, through the great High Priest, Thee, the true God, the only unbegotten, the only unapproachable, for Thy great glory. O Lord, Heavenly King, God the Father Almighty; O Lord God, Father of Christ, the spotless Lamb Who taketh away the sin of the world. Receive our supplication, Thou that sittest upon the Cherubim. For Thou only art holy, Thou only art the Lord Jesus, Christ of the God of all created nature, our King; through Whom unto Thee be glory, honor, and adoration**" [^2651].
In this manner, the monument refers even the second "**O Lord God**" unto God the Father and not unto Christ, and by virtue of this and the omission of "**O Holy Spirit**", it directs the prayer solely unto the Father [^2652]. In the Latin Church, where the Great Doxology *(Orig. p. 712)* is appointed according unto all rites at the beginning of the Mass after the Introit, almost immediately before the Epistle (in the Mozarabic rite also as a hymn for the Nativity of Christ), it likewise corresponds unto the first strophe of ours (concluding with "**in the glory of God the Father**") and contains the following variants compared with ours: "good will unto men" (*hominibus bonae voluntatis*); (**we praise**—*laudamus*, **we worship**—*adoramus*); unto "**we give thanks unto Thee**" the Mozarabic adds: "we speak a hymn unto Thee", and in the same: "for Thy great glory"; "**O Lord**" (1st time) + "**God**" (**Almighty**—*omnipotens*, as in the Creed); "**O Christ**" Mozarabic + "**Most High**" (*altissime*); there is no "**O Holy Spirit**"; instead of "sin"—"sins"; "**Thou only art Lord**" = Mozarabic + "**Thou only art Most High**"; instead of "**Jesus Christ**" (2nd time) the vocative case + "**with the Holy Spirit**". The hymn is contained in the *Codex Alexandrinus* of the Holy Scriptures and others as an appendix unto the Psalms, and in the ancient "Order" [*Posledovanie*] for the feast of Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian, and John Chrysostom [^2653] in the present festal form with the following variants: "sin"—"sins"; "**have mercy on us, Thou that takest away the sins of the world, receive our prayer**" is absent from the "Order"; instead of "**in this day**"—"this day" in the codices; "**Let Thy mercy, O Lord, be upon us, according as we have hoped in Thee**" is found only in the "Order"; "**Blessed art Thou, O Lord**", as also at present, thrice.
In the most ancient Slavic manuscripts it is not indicated that "**Blessed art Thou, O Lord**" is said thrice [^2654].
According unto the Studite Typika of certain recensions, the Great Doxology on feasts as well consisted of two parts, as at present on weekdays, and these parts

were separated by the Augmented Litany, after which was sung the portion beginning from "**Vouchsafe, O Lord**" (*Spodobi, Gospodi*) (Introductory Chapter, 368). A trace of such a practice is found also in early-printed Books of Hours [*Chasoslovy*], for instance Moscow 1602 (= Old Believer) and Lviv 1642, where the doxology is given only in our present weekday form (though, of course, entirely continuously), with the rubric (in the second Book of Hours) beneath "**Blessed art Thou, O Lord**": "if the doxology be sung, thrice thus; but if it be read, say this: **Blessed art Thou, O Master... Blessed art Thou, O Holy One...**" [^2655].
Nevertheless, the festal form of the doxology is, apparently, more ancient, and the morning hymn "**Vouchsafe, O Lord**", detached from it, is indebted, presumably, unto imitation of Vespers. The festal form could have developed within the confines of the Great Church of Constantinople and the Holy Sepulchre, and therefore prior unto the Studite Typikon, certain recensions of which know it, while the Studite-Alexian applied it, presumably, only on Great Saturday and Pascha; from the practice of the Holy Sepulchre this form was transmitted unto the Jerusalem Typikon.
The text of the Great Doxology brought from Greece by Arseny Sukhanov lacked "**have mercy on us; Thou that takest away the sins of the world**" [^2656]; the present Greek *Horologion* is as ours, differing only in punctuation: "... **Son of the Father,** *(Orig. p. 713)* **Thou that takest away the sin of the world, have mercy on us; Thou that takest away the sins of the world, receive our prayer; Thou that sittest at the right hand of the Father, and have mercy on us**" [^2657].
Slavic manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon: "Thou that takest away the sins of the whole world—Thou that takest away the sins of the world" and have no amen [^2658].
Early-printed Books of Hours have "sins" in the plural both times [^2659].

### The Trisagion (Great)

Begun with an angelic hymn, the Great Doxology likewise concludes with an angelic (according unto tradition) hymn: "**Holy God...**" (*Svyaty Bozhe*). Apart from the Divine Liturgy, this is the only place in the church service where the Trisagion is sung rather than read, independently and according unto a special order: not only thrice, but with the refrain **Glory... Both now...**, and with a repetition one and a half times more after this refrain. The absence of the prayers that customarily accompany it, even the **Our Father**, concentrates upon the hymn all attention and answers unto the laudatory and

=== LEAF p863 ===

hymnographic coloring of this entire portion of Matins (cf. its designation in Western worship—*Laudes*).

### History

The Trisagion is not appended unto the Great Doxology by the aforementioned biblical codices that provide the latter, but only by the "Order" [*Posledovanie*], without the designation "thrice".
In one recension of the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople this Trisagion, at least on Great Saturday, was sung in Tone 2 [^2660].
According unto the Studite-Alexian Typikon this Trisagion occurred with the **Our Father** at the very end of Matins before the troparion and dismissal (as at Vespers) [^2661].
Hence Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon state: "The Trisagion without the **Our Father**"; Slavic manuscripts: "afterwards in the tone, thrice **Holy God**, **Glory... Both now...**, **Holy Immortal**. Then again **Holy God** once. Then the priest the exclamation: **For Thine is the kingdom**." The Old Believer Typikon: "The Trisagion with singing without the **Our Father**, the priest saith: **For Thine is the kingdom**" [^2662].

### The Singing of the Great Doxology

In the present place of the Typikon nothing is said regarding the manner of executing the Great Doxology—whether it ought to be read or sung. Its chanting is indicated in the order for the Exaltation of the Cross: "while the Great Doxology is sung." That it must be sung is demonstrated also by its designation as "great", since in volume it is shorter than the weekday doxology, as well as by the existence from antiquity of a distinctive melody for it. The melody for the Great Doxology is idiomelon [*samoglasen*]; in it resounds a sense of exceptional tranquility, peace, and contentment in God. It is a quiet and slow melody. Concerning the singing of the Great Doxology on August 1 and on the 3rd Sunday of the Fast it is stated: "quietly and with voice," "in a quiet voice" [*tikhoglasno*].

*(Orig. p. 714)*

### According unto the Ancient Typika

The well-known description of Sinai Matins (Introductory Chapter, 295) mentions the solemn singing of the Great Doxology on feasts, when the priest began it. With exceptional solemnity

=== LEAF p864 ===

it was sung according unto the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, where the domesticus began it with a cross in his hands, taken up first by the priests, and then by the chanters (ibid., 347). But according unto the Studite Typikon of the Alexian recension it was chanted [^2663] in recitative ("*vly*"), like the Six Psalms, the Introductory Psalm, **"O Gladsome Light"** (*Svete tikhiy*), **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Spodobi, Gospodi*), and **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** (*Nyne otpushchaeshi*) with the Trisagion and troparion [^2664], while it was sung musically only on Great Saturday (Introductory Chapter, 368), but at Pascha as always ("quietly" [*potikhu*]) [^2665]. This singing was close unto reading; for this reason, even with the ascendancy among us of the Jerusalem Typikon, as a survival of the Studite the custom of reading the Great Doxology on feasts as well persisted stubbornly, concerning which Tsar Ivan IV complained unto the Stoglav Council, noting that they sing it in required cases only in such cities as Novgorod and Pskov [^2666].
Nikon of the Black Mountain considered the singing of the Great Doxology to be one of the principal peculiarities of the Jerusalem Typikon (Introductory Chapter, 368), which preserved these features from ancient cathedral practice, perhaps through the medium of the Typikon of the Holy Sepulchre. Yet Western Studite Typika likewise possess the term "Great Doxology" (see ibid.), as does the Evergetis Typikon, which remarks that it must be sung *litos kai hesychos* [λιτῶς καὶ ἡσύχως] (simply and quietly) [^2667].
Georgian manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon: "**Glory to God in the highest** with the voice"; Greek manuscripts: "the Great Doxology"; the most ancient Slavic manuscripts cite it in full (perhaps because in the Books of Hours it stood in weekday form), and one (Moscow Synodal Library No. 329/384): "the Great Doxology is sung in Tone 6" [^2668].
The "Order" [*Diataxis*] of Patriarch Philotheus: "with the beginning of the doxology the priest enters within the sanctuary, while the deacon at its conclusion pronounces the litany" [^2669].

### The Sunday Troparion

After the present Trisagion, which, in contrast unto all other Trisagia in the daily services, might be called great, at Sunday Mat*(Orig. p. 715)*ins, as at any festal Matins, a troparion follows, just as a troparion or kontakion is sung after every Trisagion (excluding that which forms part of the Usual Beginning): at the conclusion of Vespers and weekday Matins, at the royal portion of Matins, at all the Hours, at the Typika, at Small Compline, and at all three parts of Great Compline. This is already a second singing of the troparion during Matins, whereas the troparion is customarily sung during each daily service only once. Yet Matins is an exceedingly long service, and each of its three parts must possess a troparion or kontakion. In musical terms, Matins returns by the present troparion unto its beginning. Because

=== LEAF p865 ===

the troparion at Matins is sung already for the second time, and because it takes place at the final and briefest portion of Matins, it is sung not according unto the full order, but according unto an abbreviated order, that is, not twice or thrice, but once; and the Sunday troparion, moreover, is sung without a Theotokion. The absence of the latter places the present Sunday troparion on a par with the troparia of the Twelve Great Feasts, which do not require a Theotokion alongside them. This troparion occupies an exceptional position among not only Sunday troparia, but also all other troparia in that it permits no combination with any other troparion whatsoever: even upon concurrence with a Great Feast of the Theotokos, "the troparion is of the Resurrection only" (see Typikon, September 8, "if on Sunday"). This is, presumably, in view of the special dignity of the present troparia, distinguished by a majestic simplicity and a certain triumphant quality. Examining the Resurrection of the Savior from the interior aspect (especially the 1st troparion), they provide a complete catalogue, potent precisely by its brevity, of all the boundless fruits of this event for us—a catalogue so fitting now, in conclusion of all the Sunday hymns of Matins. The 1st troparion is as it were more forceful and ecstatic, while the 2nd is fuller and more concrete. And of the melodies for the troparia, Tones 1 and 2 were selected. The 1st is sung under the odd tones, of which the 5th is close (in Greek, "plagal" / indirect) unto Tone 1; the 2nd under the even tones, of which the 6th is its "plagal". Moreover, the melodies of these troparia are idiomela [*samoglasny*], for which reason their tones are not indicated in the Book of Hours and the Octoechos, but only in the Typikon. In that they scarcely follow the tones, they are ranked higher than the "dismissal" (= also at **"The Lord is God"**) Sunday troparia, though, of course, yielding unto the directly Paschal hymns of the Vigil, **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** (*Voskresenie Khristovo videvshe*) and **"Jesus having risen from the tomb"** (*Voskres Iisus ot groba*), which do not follow the tone of the Sunday at all.

### Their History

According unto the inscription in the Greek *Euchologion*, the troparia belong unto St. John of Damascus. According unto the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th century, the 1st troparion at Paschal Matins served as one of the only two troparia and hymns here in honor of the feast in general, being appointed specifically at Psalm 50, which occupied a central place at the Vigil (the other troparion, **"Unto the creature that boweth down"**, was the introit troparion); **"Having risen from the tomb"** (*Voskres iz groba*) stood at the Matins of Bright Monday. Exactly thus is it also in the Sinai Kanonarion. In both monuments the 1st troparion is in Tone 3 [^2670]. *(Orig. p. 716)* In the Jerusalem "Order" the 1st troparion at Paschal Matins occurs after the stichera **"A sacred Pascha"** (*Paskha svyashchennaya*) and **"Christ is risen"** (*Khristos voskrese*) (thrice) with the following variants: "hath come to pass" [*byst*] and "our" [*nasheya*] are absent; unto "of life" is added "unto Christ God"; absent is

"by death" [^2671]. Regarding the order of singing troparia at the Asmatic Matins—Introductory Chapter, 347.
The Studite-Alexian Typikon knows these troparia and their present alternation, appointing them under the title of "the Sunday troparion" after the aposticha of Matins [^2672].
The Evergetis [Typikon prescribes] only **"Today salvation is come unto the world"** (*Dnes spasenie miru*) [Tone 3].

### The Concluding Litanies of Matins

### The Augmented Litany at Matins

The conclusion of Matins possesses the same composition as that of Vespers, yet this is fully the case only at weekday Matins in comparison with weekday Vespers. The conclusion of festal, and consequently of Sunday, Matins differs from the conclusion of such Vespers in that the Augmented Litany and the Litany of Supplication are not separated here by anything, but follow immediately one after the other, by virtue of which they merge into one great and majestic prayer, wherein the second complements the first, supplicating of God in detail all that is necessary for the persons for whom the Augmented Litany prays. Since these are secondary litanies at the Vigil, the Augmented Litany is shortened by the omission of the first two petitions, which contain an amplification of the thought of the 3rd (in the Great Litany and the Litany of Supplication, which likewise recur at the Vigil, such an abbreviation is impossible). Like the Great Litany and in contrast unto the less prominent Litany of Supplication, the Augmented Litany never changes its exclamation, even in the present case.

*(Orig. p. 717)*

### Its History

The "Order" [*Diataxis*] of Patriarch Philotheus and certain Georgian monuments (Introductory Chapter, 378) do not have the Augmented Litany at Matins, but only the Litany of Supplication [^2679]. When the Gospel was read at Matins after the Great Doxology, the Augmented Litany began with **"Let us all say"** (*Rtsem vsi*) [^2680] (cf. at present on Great Saturday).
Early-printed and present Greek *Euchologia*: "**Have mercy on us, O God... Again we pray for our brethren... Again we pray for the blessed founders... Again we pray for mercy, life, peace... For a merciful...**" [^2681].
In the Moscow *Sluzhebnik* of 1602 (= Old Believer), the *Sluzhebnik* of Peter Mohyla, Moscow 1647 and 1658, and the *Hieratikon*—as at Vespers, only beginning from **"Have mercy on us, O God"** [^2682]. The *Hieratikon*, concerning the founders, adds "blessed".

### Its Pronouncement

The Typikon appoints the Augmented Litany at Matins to be pronounced by the priest or the deacon (*Sluzhebnik*: deacon or priest)—the former,

=== LEAF p868 ===

presumably, in the absence of a deacon.

### According unto the Ancient Typika

Manuscript Typika and the Old Believer Typikon—unspecified [^2683].
The "Order" of Patriarch Philotheus and the *Sluzhebnik* of Peter Mohyla—the deacon.
Greek printed *Euchologia* and the Moscow *Sluzhebnik* of 1658: "the deacon, if there be one" [^2684].

### The Litany of Supplication at Matins

The exclamation of the Litany of Supplication at Matins is no longer that of Vespers, just as in general the Litany of Supplication is always employed with a new exclamation. Its exclamation at Matins: "**For Thou art a God of mercy, compassion, and love for mankind**" (*Yako Bog milosti, shchedrot i chelovekolyubiya yesi*) contains the same thought as the vesperal exclamation ("**For Thou art a good God and lovest mankind**"), but in an intensified degree, and is close unto the exclamation of the Augmented Litany. After itself the Litany of Supplication here also, as always—at Vespers and the Divine Liturgy—has the bestowal of peace and the bowing of heads with the secret priestly "prayer at the bowing of heads," distinct from the corresponding prayer at Vespers and the Divine Liturgy, and containing within itself a petition for the forgiveness of sins voluntary and involuntary and for the granting of peaceful and supramundane blessings (greater than at Vespers, where it prays for preservation from sin, the devil, and evil thoughts). The exclamation of this prayer amplifies unto the ultimate degree the thought of God's mercy that was steadily heightened by the preceding exclamations: "**For Thine it is to have mercy and to save**" (*Tvoe bo yest yezhe milovati i spasati*).

*(Orig. p. 718)*

### Historical Note

At the Matins of the *Apostolic Constitutions* (4th–5th centuries) the Litany of Supplication is even more adapted unto the morning than the present one, and apart from this differs, though insignificantly, from the vesperal litany (Introductory Chapter, 137); the prayer at the bowing of heads there is of an entirely different content from the present one (137); yet in all manuscript *Euchologia* and other monuments the prayer at the bowing of heads is the present one (see above, p. 622).

### The Dismissal [Apolysis]

### The Order of the Dismissal

The order of the Dismissal (*apolysis* [ἀπόλυσις]) begins. For it the Holy Doors are opened (having been closed with the carrying of the Gospel into the sanctuary after the veneration before the kanon) [^2685], concerning which the Typikon makes no remark here, yet which is indicated in Chapter 7 in the order of Vespers (in ancient Typika this is noted here as well). The dismissal of Matins, which is at the same time the dismissal of the entire Vigil, must in its solemnity and scope correspond unto this longest of services, and therefore surpasses not only the dismissal of the Hours, Small Vespers, and Small Compline (termed the small dismissal), but also the dismissal of the Divine Liturgy.

### Preliminary Exclamations and Prayers

The order of the dismissal is preceded, like all the most important parts of the service (the reading of Holy Scripture, the prokeimenon, and the Eucharistic Canon of the Liturgy), by the exclamation: **"Wisdom!"** (*Premudrost*). This exclamation is pronounced here, as in other instances, by the deacon, concerning which, as something self-evident, the Typikon says nothing. That which enters into the order of the dismissal justifies the appropriateness of this exclamation here: the prayers chosen for it are the most sublime.
Unto such an admonition of the deacon regarding the approach of the time of dismissal, the choir musically requests the priest to begin the dismissal. Because the dismissal is a blessing upon the departure from the temple and a concluding glorification of God, the petition is expressed by the word: **"Bless!"** (*Blagoslovi*), the very word by which the priest is invited to begin the service. Only here this invitation is made not by the deacon, but more solemnly—by the choir, just as before the beginning of Matins for the exclamation: **"Glory to the Holy..."** (*Slava Svyatey...*).
After the deacon and the choir, the word belongs unto the priest, and in response unto the petition for a blessing the priest answers with a blessing, first of all, of the source of all blessing for us, Christ God, wherein the priest attributes unto Him, for the only time in the entire cycle of services, the greatest and most mystery-filled name, "**He Who is**" (*Sy* [ὁ ὤν]), Jehovah—that name which the ancient Hebrew feared to pronounce, substituting for it in the reading of *(Orig. p. 719)* Holy Scripture other names. In Christian boldness the priest, having cleansed his lips by so many prayers, now reverently and aloud pronounces this Name. In this manner, this final exclamation of Vespers and Matins, being almost literally identical with the initial exclamation of the church services:

=== LEAF p870 ===

"**Blessed is our God always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages**," introduces into it two additions that render it still more majestic and tender: "**He Who is**" and "**Christ**". The union of "**He Who is**" with "**blessed**" was made, presumably, on the basis of 2 Corinthians 11:31: "**The God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, Who is blessed unto the ages, knoweth**."
Having heard of the self-existence of Christ ("**He Who is**"), boundless for our intellect, the source and foundation of His eternity ("**always, now and ever**"), the choir, after the customary response unto the priest's exclamation, "**Amen**," prays for the manifestation on earth, in the degree possible for it, of this divine attribute, for its reflection in us through the "establishment of the Emperor," as the defender of the faith and of right order, and through him of the "holy Orthodox faith" and "Orthodox Christians."
The thought of Christ and of His self-existence cannot fail to evoke the thought of His birth on earth and of the Author of His Incarnation, Who is able also to assist by Her intercession our prayers unto Her Son. The priest accordingly turns unto Her for this assistance with the exclamation: "**Most Holy Theotokos, save us!**" (*Presvyataya Bogoroditse, spasi nas*), which, in addition to prayer, contains within itself also the greatest glorification of Her, since of Her we ask, as of Her Son, directly for salvation (which is promised in the dismissal), and not merely for prayers on our behalf and for our salvation (which alone we ask of other saints). Unto this laudatory prayer the choir answers already with a direct glorification of the Mother of God, and that with the most ecstatic of all hymns and the one most pleasing unto Her [**"More honorable than the Cherubim"**], substituting this hymn for prayer unto Her, just as the priest substitutes prayer for praise unto Her. The inverse of what preceded: there the priest praises, while the choir prays.
Now both unite in praise that returns unto Christ, and through Him unto the All-Holy Trinity. In view of the fact that the dismissal lying before the priest is directed toward instilling in those about to depart from the temple a steadfast hope in Christ, reaching unto full assurance of salvation ("**He will have mercy and save**"), the priest prepares for it: ("**Glory to Thee, O Christ God, our hope...**"). The choir takes up this glorification ascending heavenward and lifts it as it were still higher, unto the Tri-Hypostatic God Himself ("**Glory to the Father...**"), expanding this glorification likewise unto infinity ("**both now and ever**"), and joining unto it a final and fervent prayer unto God for mercy (**"Lord, have mercy"** thrice) and a second request unto the priest for a blessing upon departing from the temple: **"Bless!"** (*Blagoslovi*).

### The Dismissal in the Proper Sense

The priest makes the dismissal in the proper sense, consisting
