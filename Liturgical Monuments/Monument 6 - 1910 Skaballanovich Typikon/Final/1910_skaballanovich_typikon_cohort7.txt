### The Paschal Fast

This testimony of St. Irenaeus allows us to behold also the first seed of our present Holy Quadragesima (Forty Days' Fast / Great Lent), which at that time, being restricted unto one or two days, was regarded not as a pre-Paschal fast, but as a Paschal fast—as Pascha itself. The Paschal fast, according to this testimony, lasted in some places a single day, in others two, in others still longer; while in yet others it embraced an interval of forty hours. Three of these four specifications are clear. Only the last is expressed in such a manner that it admits—or at least once admitted—a different interpretation. Namely, Rufinus († 410), the first and most ancient translator of Eusebius's *History* into Latin, a younger contemporary of its author, renders the concluding phrase of Irenaeus thus: *"quidam vero quadraginta, ita ut diurnas ac nocturnas horas connumerent diei"* [some indeed forty, so measuring their day by counting diurnal and nocturnal hours]. Consequently, Rufinus discerned in Irenaeus here an indication of a forty-day duration of the pre-Paschal fast, achieving this simply by placing a punctuation mark after the word *tessarakonta* [τεσσαράκοντα; "forty"] and seeing in the remaining

---

=== LEAF p62 ===
words the thought that they fasted not only during the daylight hours, but also during the night. Yet for such a thought the concluding words of Irenaeus would provide an exceedingly strange and indeed downright impossible expression. If the Holy Father had desired to express the idea that Christians fasted not merely until evening, as the Jews did on the Day of Atonement, but continued the fast until late at night, he ought to have stated that Christians fasted throughout the day and part of the night. Why should there be mention here of "hours" if their quantity is not indicated? Why the unusual term "reckon" (literally, "commensurate," *symmetrousi*)? What meaning attaches unto the addition of "their" unto "day"? And indeed, was it possible for ordinary mortals to fast throughout forty days around the entire clock without food? In the lives of the holy ascetics such prolonged fasting is recounted as an exceptionally rare phenomenon even among them, for which there was truly required a fortitude of spirit akin unto that of Moses, Elijah, and Christ.

All these peculiarities in St. Irenaeus's expression fall away if one perceives in this passage a reference unto the custom of certain Christians to fast before Pascha neither one day nor two, but precisely forty hours. While some—indeed the majority—measured (*symmetrousi*) their fast by days, others measured it by hours, fasting in return not for one or several such units of time, but for the symbolic number forty, sanctified *(Orig. p. 66)* by so many lofty examples of fasting, and substituting, by reason of their infirmity, hours for days. The words "hours" and "measure," which are so difficult under Rufinus's interpretation, receive under this true understanding their most natural sense, as does also the expression "their day": a forty-hour fast may be called a single day of fasting, only a day of a peculiar sort ("their own"). Furthermore, under such an understanding there will not be between the fasting practice of different Christians (probably Christians of different local Churches) that impassable disparity which would exist if in one place they fasted one, two, or "more" days, while in others an entire forty. The very word "more" (*pleionas*) in juxtaposition with one and two cannot signify more than three, four, and the like [^326]. Nevertheless, at this time, so to speak, the idea of a forty-day fast in imitation of Christ's fast was already in the air among Christians.

It is characteristic that the Asiatics ended the Paschal fast on that (*kata tautēn*) day [^327] whereon other Christians, according to Irenaeus, began it, that is, on the day of the Jewish Passover. In the time of St. Epiphanius of Cyprus there existed a branch of the Quartodecimans (as were called the partisans of celebrating Pascha together with the Jews on the 14th of Nisan, who separated themselves from the Church on this account; in Latin, *quartodecimani*), which restricted unto a single day—the 14th of Nisan—both the pre-Paschal fast and the feast of Pascha itself [^328]. If the Quartodecimans of the 2nd century were correct in their dispute with the Roman Church of that era regarding the antiquity of their practice, it is possible that the branch of Quartodecimans mentioned by St. Epiphanius preserved the most ancient practice of the Paschal fast. Against such a supposition nothing can be argued from the circumstance that the Church condemned

---

=== LEAF p63 ===
the Quartodecimans as heretics (more accurately, as schismatics): she condemned them not for fidelity unto antiquity, but for rupturing ecclesiastical unity on its account. (They met with the fate of our Old Believers). Yet it is also possible that the one-day fast at Pascha on the 14th of Nisan was not an ancient practice preserved by sectarians, but a practice created by them: it was the same one-day Pascha-fast known unto other Christians, only among the sectarians fixed not unto the Friday preceding Sunday, but unto the 14th of Nisan.

Yet however that may be, it is beyond doubt that the pre-Paschal fast developed not out of Christ's forty-day fast in the wilderness, as older liturgical scholars long believed, but out of a one- or two-day fast in honor of the Savior's death on the day of the Jewish Passover [^329]. This fast among certain Churches or Christians was gradually extended unto forty hours (corresponding unto the number of hours Christ remained in the tomb), then unto a week (Passion Week), then unto several weeks, and finally unto forty days.

*(Orig. p. 67)*

### Disputes concerning the Time of Pascha

Given such a character of Pascha in the 2nd century, those vehement controversies which were then waged over it become entirely intelligible. The dispute concerned whether to unite with the commemoration of the Savior's death (by fasting) the commemoration of His resurrection (with joy), or not to unite them; and if they were to be united, unto which of these two commemorations preference should be accorded: whether unto the commemoration of the Savior's death, which fell on the 14th of Nisan (on whatever day of the week this date might occur), or unto the commemoration of His resurrection, which necessarily fell on the day of the week whereon it occurred, that is, on Sunday (whatever numerical day of the month this Sunday might have, provided only that it followed upon the 14th of Nisan). The former was maintained by the Churches of Asia Minor; the latter, by the Roman Church and the majority of other Churches. The chief literary champion of the Asia Minor practice regarding the time of Pascha was Melito, Bishop of Sardis, who composed *Two Books on the Pascha* (c. 170). Its literary opponents were Apollinaris, Bishop of Hierapolis, Clement of Alexandria, and St. Hippolytus, Bishop of Rome. In Palestine, Rome, Pontus, Gaul, and Greece councils convened and pronounced in favor of the Roman practice. Pope Victor (c. 196) threatened the Asiatics with excommunication. The aforementioned epistle of St. Irenaeus had as its very purpose to reconcile the contending parties. Apparently the dispute continued down unto the Council of Nicaea, which adopted the Roman practice as universal. The Council of Antioch (341 [cited in the original text as 333]) excommunicates from the Church those **"who celebrate Pascha together with the Jews"** [^330]. ### Wednesday and Friday

In the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* (*Didache*) we find an injunction regarding the fast of Wednesday and Friday: **"Let not your fasts be with the hypocrites"** (that is, the Jews, specifically the Pharisees), **"for they fast on the second and fifth days of the week; but fast ye on the fourth and the preparation day [Wednesday and Friday]"** [^332]. *The Shepherd* of Hermas mentions a fast bearing the designation of "station" (*statio*) [^333]. Since later writers designate by this name perhaps Wednesday and Friday [^334], this passage of Hermas is regarded as the most ancient witness unto these weekly fasts. It is remarkable that the *Didache* makes no mention of Pascha and its fast.

*(Orig. p. 68)*

### Pentecost

In a fragment from the writings of St. Irenaeus [^335] we find mention of the celebration in his time (that is, at the end of the 2nd century) of Pentecost, during which, as on Pascha and Sunday, they did not bend the knee. All other feasts in honor of the Savior took their rise later (the most ancient of them being Theophany / Epiphany in the 3rd century).

### Commemorations of Martyrs

From the 2nd century we possess the first notices concerning the liturgical veneration of martyrs. The most ancient of such accounts is found in the martyrdom acts of St. Ignatius (107 or 115): **"having spent several days beside his tomb in the singing of hymns, the Christians resolved also in the future to hymn his life and passion, and at his memory to glorify the Lord Jesus"** [^336]. Eusebius cites the epistle of the Church of Smyrna unto the Church of Philomelium (in Asia Minor) on the occasion of the martyric demise of St. Polycarp († 167); therein it is related that the proconsul, on the counsel of the Jews, burned the body of Polycarp, lest it become an object of worship for the Christians; **"but we gathered up his bones... and laid them where it was fitting; thither, so soon as it may be possible, we shall assemble with jubilation and joy, and the Lord will permit us to celebrate the day of his martyric nativity (*genethlion* [γενέθλιον])"**. Remarkable is this perspective upon the demise of a martyr as his birthday (*dies natalis* / *natalitiae*). This view was the reason why the ecclesiastical year, even in subsequent times, possessed no birthdays of saints whatever, with the exception of the Savior, the Mother of God, and John the Baptist; and why even these birthdays, not excluding the Nativity of Christ, began to be celebrated comparatively late. The cited

---

passages speak of the celebration of martyrs' commemorations in general terms, without showing wherein this celebration consisted. Yet a number of other martyrdom acts demonstrate that the latter consisted primarily in the holding of agapes (whenever possible, at the sites of their martyrdom), which were styled *agapae natalitiae* [^337]. In certain of these acts there is mention of their being read aloud over the tombs of the martyrs on the day of their commemoration; for which reason unto the very lives and passions of the martyrs there was subsequently assigned the designation *legenda* [^338] ("things to be read") in the West, and in the East "Prologues" (introductory readings for divine service) and "Synaxaria" (readings at solemn assemblies) [^339].

The commemorations of martyrs were probably only lo- *(Orig. p. 69)* -cal, and not universal across the Church. Yet certain martyrs, through the circumstances of their sufferings, acquired widespread renown and became venerated, in all likelihood, even beyond the boundaries of their homeland; such were, for example, St. Ignatius the God-Bearer, who prior unto His passion accomplished, as it were, a triumphal progress throughout all the Christian regions of the Roman Empire, and St. Polycarp of Smyrna, of whom the Smyrneans in the epistle on His demise testify that he is especially honored among the martyrs and known even unto the pagans of many places [^340]. In general there were martyrs whose tortures caused the entire Christian world of that time to shudder, rendering them, perhaps, universally venerated. The commemorations of such martyrs were close, even in the 2nd century, unto becoming universal for the whole Church. Thus the commemorations of martyrs are the oldest feasts in the Church in honor of saints. Following the example of the martyrs, commemorations of the Apostles also began to be venerated. Yet concerning such apostolic commemorations from the 2nd century no records exist.

## III Century

### Sources

Notices concerning the divine worship of this century are scattered, first of all, in the writings of ecclesiastical authors of that era; such are: Clement of Alexandria († 217), *Stromata* and *Paedagogus*; Tertullian († 220), *Apology*, *De Oratione*, *De Jejuniis*, and others; Minucius Felix, *Octavius* (early 3rd cent.); Origen († 265 [254]), *Contra Celsum*, *De Oratione*; St. Cyprian, Bishop of Carthage († 257 [258]). Accurate accounts of the worship of this century could also reach the ecclesiastical historians of the 4th and 5th centuries—Eusebius, Socrates, Sozomen—as well as other writers of that period, namely the Holy Fathers (SS. John Chrysostom, Basil the Great, Blessed Augustine and Theodoret).

Yet perhaps the most important source for the history of 3rd-century divine worship is the series of canonical monuments of this era, now subjected by Western scholars unto the most detailed and thorough analysis. This analysis, however, has not yet succeeded in firmly establishing the chronology of these monuments so as to permit their dating unto the 3rd century with certainty. We refer unto a series of monuments which, together with the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* (*Didache*, a monument almost certainly of the 2nd cent.), formed the foundation of the so-called *Apostolic Constitutions* [^341].

### The Apostolic Constitutions as a Redaction of III-Century Canonical Monuments

This monument, which attributes itself unto the Apostles and unto St. Clement of Rome, manifestly betrays a non-apostolic origin: therein are encountered such strange and non-Orthodox notions as, for example, the comparison of bishop, presbyter, and deaconess unto God the Father, the Son of God, and the Holy Spirit [^342], or the granting unto the deaconess of the right to baptize women [^343]. Nevertheless, in many portions of the monument and in its general tone there resounds a profound antiquity: from it there breathes palpably the spirit of the 3rd century—the spirit of the rigorism of that day, the immediacy of religious feeling; furthermore, in the *Apostolic Constitutions* there is mention, as of an existing *(Orig. p. 71)* and frequent phenomenon, of confessorship under persecution, of certain spiritual gifts and ministries of the apostolic era and the 2nd century; it is characterized by the complete unsettledness and embryonic state of theological terminology, and the like. Nonetheless, in places the *Apostolic Constitutions* bear the marks of the hierarchical and liturgical order of the 4th century (the offices of subdeacon,

---

=== LEAF p67 ===
and doorkeeper of the church; there is mentioned the feast of the Nativity of Christ, which appeared in the East at the end of the 4th cent.). At all events, the *Apostolic Constitutions* could not have originated later than the beginning of the 5th century: they contain no echoes of the Nestorian and Eutychian controversies [^344]. Their author reveals himself to be a Syrian (using Syrian names of the months), and in his theological (christological) views a Semi-Arian or Apollinarian [^345]. This latter circumstance, together with the aforementioned strange ideas of the monument, was the reason why the Council in Trullo, notwithstanding the familiarity of this work in certain parts at least unto ancient Fathers (for instance, St. Epiphanius) and the enduring respect for it in the East, resolved not to recognize for it the same authority as for the *85 Canons of the Holy Apostles*, and decreed concerning it:

**"Forasmuch as in these canons"** (that is, the Apostolic Canons) [^346] **"it is commanded us to receive the Constitutions of the same Holy Apostles transmitted through Clement, into which long ago heterodox persons, unto the injury of the Church, introduced certain spurious matters foreign unto piety, which have darkened for us the fair beauty of divine teaching, we have prudently rejected such Constitutions of Clement for the edification and protection of the most Christian flock, by no means admitting the offspring of heretical falsehood, nor mingling them with the pure and perfect apostolic teaching"** [^347].

Scholars long ago directed attention unto the heterogeneous and multitemporal origin of the *Apostolic Constitutions* in their various parts. Gradually it became possible to discover the ancient sources of this compilation. In 1854 P. de Lagarde published the *Syriac Didascalia* (which had become known unto scholars even earlier) and demonstrated that it corresponds unto the Greek text cited by St. Epiphanius, which had previously been considered a borrowing from the *Apostolic Constitutions*, proving thereby that the first six books of the *Constitutions* represent a redaction of this *Didascalia* in its non-extant Greek original. Scholars unanimously date the *Didascalia* unto the 3rd century—some unto its first half, others unto the second [^348]. The *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* (*Didache*, *(Orig. p. 72)* 2nd cent.), discovered by Bryennios in 1883, proved to be the source of the first part of Book VII of the *Apostolic Constitutions*. As regards Book VIII of the *Constitutions*, its first two chapters probably represent a reworked version of the lost treatise of St. Hippolytus of Rome, *On Spiritual Gifts* (*De charismatibus*), while the remaining portion of the book (with the exception of chapters 35–39, which set forth the order of Vespers and Matins and are not found in all manuscripts of the *Apostolic Constitutions*) is closely related unto the three following

---

=== LEAF p68 ===
ancient monuments: 1) *The Testament of Our Lord Jesus Christ* (*Testamentum Domini nostri Jesu Christi*), discovered in a Syriac manuscript by the Roman Catholic Patriarch of Antioch, Rahmani, and published by him with a Latin translation in 1899 [^349]; 2) *The Canons of Hippolytus*, preserved in Arabic and published in Latin translation by Haneberg in 1870 and by Achelis in 1891 [^350]; 3) *The Ecclesiastical Canons of the Holy Apostles*, or *The Egyptian Canons*, *The Egyptian Church Order*—a monument preserved in Greek, Ethiopic, Coptic, and Arabic recensions, published in Ethiopic with a Latin translation by Ludolf in 1691, in Coptic with an English translation by Tattam in 1848, and by De Lagarde (without translation) in 1883, and in the Greek recension by Bickell in 1856 [1843] (the Arabic text remaining unpublished [^351]). All these monuments are in places so close unto one another and unto Book VIII of the *Apostolic Constitutions* that between them there cannot fail to exist a genetic dependency. Yet in defining this dependency, and consequently the comparative antiquity and date of origin of these liturgically critical monuments, scholars diverge. Achelis regards the *Canons of Hippolytus* as the most ancient of these monuments (not counting the *Testamentum*, which was not yet known unto him), assigning them unto the time of Tertullian, the first quarter or half of the 3rd century; the *Egyptian Canons*, according to Achelis, are a redaction thereof, and Book VIII of the *Apostolic Constitutions* is a redaction of the *Egyptian Canons*. Rahmani considers the most ancient to be, naturally, the *Testamentum* discovered by himself, assigning it unto the end of the 2nd century; the *Egyptian Canons*, in his view, are a rework of the *Testamentum*, while the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Apostolic Constitutions* are two parallel redactions of the *Egyptian Canons*. Funk regards Book VIII of the *Apostolic Constitutions* as the oldest of the three monuments, dating it, like the others, unto the 4th century; in its shorter recension (the so-called *Epitome*) he sees an extract from this book, and in the *Egyptian Constitutions* and the *Canons of Hippolytus* an abridged reworking of this extract, while in the *Testamentum* a redaction of one of these latter monuments, dating the *Testamentum* unto the beginning of the 5th century.

*(Orig. p. 73)*

### The Testament (Testamentum) of Our Lord Jesus Christ

In view of the richness of liturgical material in these monuments, especially in the *Testamentum*, it is by no means indifferent for the liturgist whether this latter monument must be assigned unto the 2nd or 5th century, and likewise whether the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Constitutions* are older than the *Apostolic Constitutions*, that is, whether they may be referred unto the 3rd century. Therefore, the polemic between Funk and Rahmani [^352] possesses great importance for us. Funk refutes all of Rahmani's arguments in favor of the origin of the *Testamentum* in the 2nd century, yet not all of his objections are equally forceful, and in any event they are not capable of removing this

---

=== LEAF p69 ===
monument and those related unto it not only unto the 5th century, but, in our opinion, even beyond the end of the 3rd century.

In the *Testamentum*: 1) mention is made not only of confessors and martyrs, but of catechumens who are bound in chains and condemned unto death prior unto baptism; 2) no mention is made of the division of catechumens into classes, concerning which St. Gregory of Neocaesarea (233–270) already speaks; 3) nothing is said concerning the imposition of the Gospel Book upon the head of a bishop being consecrated—a custom that arose in the 3rd century; 4) the reader is sometimes placed above the deacon, whereas the epistle of Pope Cornelius unto Fabian [Fabius] of Antioch in 251 places him even below the subdeacon; 5) the creed consists solely of questions put unto the person being baptized prior unto immersion in water, and in these questions nothing is said of the resurrection of the body, which, as a great mystery, is imparted only unto the newly baptized; the sign of the cross is likewise regarded as a mystery (*arcanum*); 6) soldiers receiving baptism are required to renounce military service, in place of which the *Apostolic Constitutions* require only that which John the Baptist demanded according unto the Gospel of Luke 3:14; 7) mention is made of three spiritual gifts (*charismata*) as continuing to exist in the Church: the gifts of healing, knowledge, and tongues, whereas the *Egyptian Canons* know only the gift of healing, and the *Apostolic Constitutions* at this point mention merely exorcists [^353]. Certain of these arguments Funk does not refute, selecting for refutation others that are less cogent. Against these latter he makes, for instance, such objections: 4) in the hierarchy of ecclesiastical offices there was not yet uniformity; in Syria the reader might rank higher, while in Rome the subdeacon did; 6) the demand made of soldiers in the *Testamentum* is milder (!); 7) the mention of spiritual gifts is a survival from ancient writings utilized by the compiler of the *Testamentum*; and indeed even later there were individuals in the Church who considered themselves charismatics [^354].

Leaving it unto the reader to judge of the strength of these objections, we shall point out further the following indicators which do not permit dating the *Testamentum* later than the end of the 3rd century or the very beginning of the 4th: the cycle of feasts in it consists solely of Pascha, Saturday, Sunday, Epiphany, and Pentecost [^355]; *(Orig. p. 74)* there is no feast of the Nativity of Christ, whereas it exists in the *Apostolic Constitutions*, which arose in the same Syria [^356]; no mention is made of feasts in honor of the Apostles and the Protomartyr Stephen, which are present in the *Constitutions*; the Quadragesima (Forty Days' Fast) is spoken of only in connection with baptism, as a fast preparatory unto the latter [^357]. From a comparison of the liturgical practice of the *Testamentum* with the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* we shall become convinced that the former monument frequently presents a less complex liturgical practice in comparison with the two latter, which according unto all indications and the virtually unanimous consensus of researchers date no later than the beginning of the 4th century.

On the other hand, to consider the *Testamentum* (with Rahmani) older than the second half of the 3rd century is precluded by the presence therein of the feast of Epiphany, which had its beginning in the 3rd century, and of the Quadragesima, the earliest mentions of

---

=== LEAF p70 ===
which we find in writers only of the second half of the 3rd century. Nor does the plan of the Christian church building provided by the *Testamentum* allow this, wherein there are already present such components as a solea, a veil [curtain], three entrances, a diaconicon, a baptistery, an annex (nave / vestibule?) for catechumens, a chamber for recording those who offer gifts, and a gazophylakion (treasury). Certain of these elements undoubtedly arose at the close of the 3rd century, during the period of relative respite from persecutions (let us recall the church at Nicomedia). Moreover, even in the catacomb churches (crypts) of St. Agnes (in the Roman catacombs) and of St. Januarius (in Naples) there exist compartments for catechumens, traces of pillars whereon a curtain could hang, and a diaconicon. In an underground Cappadocian church there is a secret room which could serve both for hiding from persecution [^358] and as a treasury. A treasury attached unto a church is mentioned also by St. Justin Martyr and Tertullian [^359].

Certain features in that description of the church building given by the *Testamentum* bear the stamp of greater antiquity than the church plan in the *Apostolic Constitutions*. Thus the *Testamentum* does not demand of the church a basilican form, as do the *Apostolic Constitutions*; it indicates the ambo/pulpit for the reader near the altar, and not in the midst of the nave; it assigns for the bishop, presbyters, and widows special chambers within the church itself, which presupposes a church within an ordinary house; and it says nothing concerning the consecration of a church, which is known already unto St. Ephrem the Syrian (4th cent.) and the author writing under the name of St. Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 5th cent.) [^360].

Thus nothing in the content of the *Testamentum* prevents assigning it at least unto the end of the 3rd century. In any event, among the enumerated canonical monuments, the *Didascalia* is undoubtedly of the 3rd century, the *Testamentum* is almost certainly of the 3rd century, the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* are probably of the 3rd century, while the *Apostolic Constitutions* stand close unto the 3rd century and have preserved in large measure its *(Orig. p. 75)* liturgical practice. The picture of divine worship afforded by them will in many respects corroborate this conclusion.

### The Agape in the III Century

The agape at the beginning of the 3rd century continues to occupy the chief place among ecclesiastical services after the Eucharist, although it already begins to lose its significance in favor of Matins, and by the end of the 3rd century becomes a secondary and infrequent service. Almost every one of the 3rd-century writers and every one of the described canonical monuments of this century contains discourse concerning the agape. Here are the testimonies concerning it in approximate chronological order. Each of them points out a new feature in the ritual of the agape, and taken together they provide an exceedingly complete picture of this unique service in the century of its greatest flourishing.

### The Agape according to Clement of Alexandria
