Following the psalm, the order of thanksgiving for the meal, conforming to the type of the briefest services (the Inter-Hours, etc.), has the Trisagion and, following **“Our Father”**, troparia (not designated as such here, and indeed receiving no designation whatsoever). With these specified troparia concludes the third part of the Order of the Panagia—the thanksgiving for the meal—and together with it this entire rite. Following the troparia comes the dismissal. The dismissal here, naturally, must be of the small dismissal type; yet even the small dismissal here possesses an abbreviated and modified form. Namely, without the exclamation of the priest **“Glory to Thee, Christ God...”**, there is pronounced (by all, as is everything preceding): **“Lord, have mercy (3). Bless”** (the preparatory *Glory... Both now...* was already between the troparia). The priest, in place of **“May Christ our true God...”**, says: **“Blessed is God Who hath mercy upon us and nourisheth us...”**

#### History of the Order of the Panagia
The prayerful sanctification of the monastic meal attains its culmination in the “Order of the Panagia”.

Ecclesiastical tradition attributes directly apostolic antiquity unto this rite. This tradition is placed in the *Sequenced Psalter* immediately following the exposition of the rite, but it also enters as an integral component into that narrative concerning the Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos which is provided by the *Chet'i-Minei* (Menologia). According to this tradition, when the Apostles, following the descent of the Holy Spirit upon them and prior to their departure to preach in diverse lands, lived together, they were accustomed at the midday meal to leave an unoccupied place at table for Christ, placing there a head-cushion with

=== LEAF p942 ===

a fragment of bread (*ukrukh*). Upon the conclusion of dinner and after the prayer of thanksgiving, they elevated this bread fragment with the words: **“Glory to Thee, our God, glory to Thee. Glory to the Father and to the Son and to the Holy Spirit. Great is the name of the Holy Trinity. Lord Jesus Christ, help us”** (replacing the two final exclamations during the Paschal days with **“Christ is risen”**). This rite the Apostles performed also following their parting from one another—each one separately. Gathered miraculously for the translation of the Mother of God and having performed Her burial, on the third day they were sitting together at the refectory table. When after dinner they, elevating according to custom the bread fragment in remembrance of Christ, pronounced: **“Great is the name...”**, they beheld in the air the Most Holy Theotokos, surrounded by Angels and promising to abide with them always; involuntarily the Apostles then exclaimed instead of *(Orig. p. 780)* **“Lord Jesus Christ, help us”**—**“Most Holy Theotokos, help us.”** Having departed following this unto the tomb of the Mother of God and having opened it, the Apostles did not find Her most pure body there, and became convinced that She had been taken up into heaven unto Her Divine Son.[^2810]

The prayerful blessing of the meal the Christian Church could have inherited from the Old Testament—Jewish—tradition. What form it had in the latter can be judged in part by the rites of late Judaism (ritual being generally stable): at an ordinary meal, composed, however, of not fewer than three persons, the head of the family or the most honored person recites the formula of blessing over the bread, slices and distributes the pieces unto his fellow-diners; a portion of the bread remains until the conclusion of the meal as a symbol of the presence of God; in like manner also the wine is blessed, wherein the chalice is lifted above the table; over each separate course blessings are recited.[^2811] The Christian agape meals, especially when they were not combined with the Eucharist, without doubt gave birth to many rites of the Order of the Panagia.[^2812] It is remarkable that the most important of the prayers of this rite in nearly their present form are provided by monuments of the 4th century: in St. John Chrysostom and in the work of Pseudo-Athanasius, *On Virginity*. It is precisely here that there is given as the prayer before dinner the prayer: **“Blessed is God Who hath mercy upon us and nourisheth us”**, only in Chrysostom it is in the singular, without *“Who hath mercy”* and *“worship”*, and in Athanasius without *“ages”*. The prayer **“Glory to Thee, O Lord”** is found only in Chrysostom without differences from the present text.[^2813]

With the emergence of monasteries, the refectory meal within them immediately received an especially prayerful setting and was regarded as a brief church service. In the Pachomian monasteries this service consisted of a psalm and a prayer, sometimes of several psalms and prayers.[^2814]

The oldest monument of the Studite Typikon, the *Hypotyposis*, says concerning the meal: *“When the brethren go to the morning meal, upon their lips is*

=== LEAF p943 ===

*a verse (from a psalm, of course); they sit at tables by nines. The taxiarchs perform the service at the table decently, without noise. There is also a reading, at which the brethren sit during winter in head-coverings (koukoullia / kuvuklia) upon their heads. The sign, moreover, of the end of this reading is, at the last course, the sound of spoons cast together by all into the dish. In like manner also for the mixing (of wine) and for the courses there is (a signal) by a stroke.”*

According to the *Diatyposis*, *(Orig. p. 781)* beyond this: *“the brethren go unto the refectory at the sound of the bell... with a verse upon their lips, and likewise when they arise (from the table), until they reach the narthex, in order to perform thanksgiving for that which they have received. There is also a reading by appointment of the ecclesiarch, who also takes care that that which they did not have time to finish reading in church should not be neglected,”*[^2815] that is, for the refectory there was its own cycle of readings and, in addition, at it were completed the readings appointed for the daily services that were not finished in the church.[^2816]

A further stage in the development of the Studite refectory rite in surviving monuments may be recognized in the order provided by the Synaxarion of the Mount Athos Iviron Monastery in a Georgian manuscript of the early 12th century. Here, first of all, is the most ancient brief notice concerning the refectory meal: *“The brethren enter into the refectory, having upon their lips a song, and seat themselves without dispute at the refectory tables by nine men. *“Upon the conclusion of the Liturgy the leaders of the choir (the singers) enter into the diaconicon unto the presbyter (who celebrated), whom the ecclesiarch (as head of the singers and their representative) greets according to rank. The presbyter and the brethren gather in the narthex; the presbyter pronounces: **‘Glory to Thee, Christ our God, glory to Thee’**; all harmoniously and compunctiously begin the psalm: **‘I will exalt Thee, my God’**, and proceed into the refectory. Following the blessing (of the table) they seat themselves by nine men at the table without dispute and noise. When the reader names the title of the reading, the refectorian proclaims aloud: ‘Lord, bless. Before each course the refectorian says: ‘Lord Jesus Christ.’”*

As the testimonies of Blessed Symeon of Thessalonica and Mark of Ephesus demonstrate, in Greece in the 15th century there existed an Order of the Panagia which was, in all probability, entirely identical with the present one. Symeon of Thessalonica mentions in it **“Our Father”**, the prayer **“Glory to Thee, O Holy One, glory to Thee, O King...”**, four exclamations **“Great is the name”**, etc., the hymn **“All generations call thee blessed...”**, and **“It is truly meet”**. This rite had there even a broader application than at present: it was celebrated not only at monastic meals, but by many priests also during the Liturgy following **“Especially concerning the Most Holy...”**[^2825] either constantly or at the request of certain persons. Blessed Symeon *(Orig. p. 787)* of Thessalonica says concerning himself that he *“established that in cathedral churches this mentioned rite should be celebrated daily, namely, that the priest at every Matins at the end of the 9th Ode should elevate this bread.”* There was elevated in Greece not the entire prosphora, but a three-sided particle (in honor of the Holy Trinity) extracted from it. At the monastic table a lamp was placed before the vessel containing the panagia. The elevation was performed by the reader who read during the refectory meal.[^2826]

The order of the refectory meal and the elevation of the panagia among the Greeks is described also by Arseny Sukhanov in his *Proskynitarion* (17th c.). Upon the conclusion of the Liturgy they bring into the refectory the “bread of the Mother of God”, and a “simple elder” or a priest simply extracts a particle from it; when the patriarch, having rested from the Liturgy[^2827] in his cell, comes into the refectory and sits down, the priest of the week strikes the *kandya* and says **“Our Father”**; the patriarch blesses the table while seated. He sits at a separate small table, and dishes are served unto him by deacons, and wine by archdeacons, who in his presence do not sit at table, but dine afterward.
