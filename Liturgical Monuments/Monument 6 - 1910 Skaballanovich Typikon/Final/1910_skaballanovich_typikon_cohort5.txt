=== LEAF p41 ===
lyrical [^224];—in order that there should be no overloading of the liturgical material with homogenous content: **"And if any man speak in an unknown tongue, let it be by two, or at the most by three, and that by course; and let one interpret... Let the prophets speak two or three, and let the other judge"** [^225], in order that there should not be simultaneous speaking [^226] of several persons (and "that by course" is a requirement not very insistent). In all these requirements the Apostle refers to the fact that **"so it is in all churches of the saints"** [^227]. Consequently, throughout the various churches a more or less uniform order of divine worship was already beginning to be established. It took place decently (*euschemōn*, elegantly, aesthetically) and in order (*kata taxin*, in well-ordered sequence) [^228]: the former depended upon the latter.

It is possible that the order in which the ministrations of the charismatics alternated during divine worship is indicated by the following words of the Apostle: **"When ye come together, every one of you hath a psalm, hath a doctrine, hath a tongue, hath a revelation, hath an interpretation. Let all things be done unto edifying"** [^229]. The tongue and revelation (prophecy), as the most intense degrees of inspiration, are set in the middle: they are prepared for by inspired chanting ("a psalm") and by the teaching of the instructor (*didaskalos*) and conclude with the equally calm speech of the "interpreter" (interpretations for speaking with tongues and, presumably, prophecy). All this "charismatic" worship could be prefaced and prepared for by the ordinary service, consisting of the reading of Holy Scripture, hymns, and prayers. This same passage from the Apostle Paul, apparently, speaks in favor of the fact that in the divine worship of that time there predominated the performance of liturgical material by individual persons, and not by the entire assembly [^230]. In favor of this also speaks the remark of the Apostle Paul: **"Else when thou shalt bless with the spirit, how shall he that occupieth the room of the unlearned say Amen at thy giving of thanks, seeing he understandeth not what thou sayest?"** [^231]. The participation of the entire congregation in divine worship *(Orig. p. 44)* is represented here under the form of listening to that which is chanted and spoken by the charismatics, with the expression of this participation by the word "Amen."

### Divine Worship according to the Apocalypse

How well-ordered and firm a structure had developed in divine worship toward the close of the apostolic era, and what beauty this imparted unto worship, is clearly seen from the Apocalypse, a book which in many places is a reflection of the liturgical practice of that time. The Apocalypse first of all allows us to ascertain a decisive break, so far as this was possible, between Christian worship and that of the synagogue. The synagogue is directly termed therein "of Satan" (*synagōgē tou satana*, assembly of Satan) [^232]. It is rightly thought that at the foundation of the principal apocalyptic vision (ch. 4) of God sitting upon the throne and the Lamb with the twenty-four elders lies the picture of the contemporary

---

=== LEAF p42 ===
Liturgy celebrated by a bishop with a college of presbyters falling prostrate before the throne of God and the Lamb in the midst thereof; beneath the altar are the souls of them that were slain for the word of God—the tombs of the martyrs, upon which the early Christians celebrated the Eucharist. It is characteristic that the elders worship God and the Lamb when the Cherubim sing: **"Holy, holy, holy, Lord..."** and that unto their singing they add: **"Thou art worthy, O Lord, to receive glory and honour and power: for Thou hast created all things, and for Thy pleasure they are and were created"** [^233]. All this recalls liturgical hymns and prayers. Throughout the entire Apocalypse there is scattered also an array of other unmistakably liturgical formulae. Such, for example, is the doxological formula, frequently varied: **"To Him be glory and dominion unto ages of ages. Amen"** [^234] or: **"Blessing, and honour, and glory, and power, be unto Him that sitteth upon the throne, and unto the Lamb unto ages of ages"** [^235]. Sometimes unto these and similar formulae there is added beforehand or behind *Alleluia* and *Amen*, *even so, Amen* [^236]. In various places the Apocalypse gives up to ten hymns in honor of the Lamb [^237] and mentions the song of Moses the servant of God [^238]. In the words: **"Blessed is he that reads, and they that hear the words *(Orig. p. 45)* of this prophecy"** [^239], the Apocalypse is directly designated for liturgical use, wherein by him who reads, *ho anagignōskōn* in the singular (*anagnōstēs*), is meant the church reader (*lector*), who read the Scripture unto the assembly of listeners (*hoi akouontes*) [^240].

### Feasts in the Apostolic Century

Alongside the development of the daily cycle of divine services, the apostolic century laid the foundations also of the Christian church year. Initially the Apostles and believers observed Jewish feasts, presumably with the same zeal with which they visited the Temple daily. Even the Apostle Paul, that preacher of complete freedom from the Jewish and legal yoke, strives not to spend outside Jerusalem any of the great Jewish feasts, interrupting for them his missionary journeys and hastening unto them from Asia Minor and Greece into Palestine [^241]. This close connection of apostolic Christianity with the Jewish calendar, however, was bound to become weaker and weaker. If for pagan converts circumcision was not considered necessary, then still less were Jewish feasts. Nevertheless, Paul had even in this regard to protect his neophytes from Judaizing Christians: **"Let no man therefore judge you in meat, or in drink, or in respect of an holyday, or of the new moon, or of the sabbath days"** [^242]—a passage demonstrating that the Jewish ecclesiastical year was considered non-obligatory for all Christians without exception.

### The Day of the Lord (Sunday)

Yet the celebration of Sunday laid the beginning of the Christian church year, which began gradually to eclipse the Jewish [^243].

---

=== LEAF p43 ===
The first definite indication of the celebration of Sunday during the time of the Apostles is found in the First Epistle to the Corinthians, written presumably around A.D. 58 at the close of the Apostle Paul's two-year stay in Ephesus during his third missionary journey. The Apostle counsels the Corinthian Christians to make the charitable collection for the benefit of the Palestinian brethren on the first day of the week, as he had previously appointed in the churches of Galatia. On this day each believer is to lay by him in store, as God hath prospered him, for the poor and keep it until the arrival of the Apostle to hand over unto him [^244]. Here, consequently, is an indication of the sanctification of Sunday solely by charity. Yet from the very same year we have an indication in Acts of the sanctification of Sunday by a liturgical assembly as well. When the Apostle Paul from that same *(Orig. p. 46)* missionary journey was returning to Jerusalem and made a halt in Troas, then **"upon the first day of the week, when the disciples came together to break bread, Paul, ready to depart on the morrow, preached unto them, and continued his speech until midnight"** [^245]. The assembly was not on account of the impending departure of the Apostle Paul, but for the sake of Sunday; otherwise the writer of Acts would have said not "upon the first day of the week," but "on the eve of departure." Sunday is sanctified in this case by the Eucharist (*breaking of bread*), which is celebrated in the evening; in the upper chamber where the believers were gathered, the writer of Acts remarks, **"there were many lights"** (*hikanai lampades*): it was not merely illuminated on account of the evening hour, but brightly illuminated, evidently on occasion of the feast and, perhaps, following the example of the Jewish Sabbath: divine worship is prolonged from evening until dawn.

There is reason to think that the veneration of Sunday by the Apostles began long before the indicated time. It is remarkable that on the first Pentecost after the Ascension of the Lord, the Apostles and believers go neither to the Temple for the solemn morning sacrifice, nor to the synagogue, but arrange their prayer assembly in a private house. And Pentecost in that year fell upon the first day of the week [^246]. Nevertheless, at least initially, the new Christian feast could hardly have completely displaced the Sabbath from the liturgical practice of believers. Eusebius says of the Ebionites that they, "calling the Apostles apostates from the law, observed the Sabbath and in general led a manner of life similar to the Jews; yet, even as we, celebrated also Sundays, for the commemoration of the resurrection of the Lord" [^247]; in this practice of the Ebionites one may see a trace of the primitive practice of Jewish Christians [^248]. In the Apocalypse Sunday, which the Gospels and Epistles still call one of the sabbaths (*hē mia* or *prōtē tōn sabbatōn*) [^249], already receives the distinct name of the Lord's Day, *hē kyriakē hēmera* [^250].

### Pascha and Fasting

---

=== LEAF p44 ===
With Sunday, it seems, the cycle of feasts of the young Church was exhausted. Hardly in the words of the Apostle Paul: **"For even Christ our Passover is sacrificed for us: therefore let us keep the feast, not with old leaven..."** [^251] can one see an indication of *(Orig. p. 47)* Christian Pascha in contradistinction unto the Jewish. Rather can such an indication be seen in the thoroughness with which the Evangelist John notes the coincidence of Christ's death with the Jewish Passover [^252]. The insistence with which Christian tradition has always attributed to the Apostles themselves the institution of Great Lent allows us to seek at least its rudiments in this period. It is possible that the words of the Savior: **"When the bridegroom shall be taken away from them, then shall they fast,"** cited by so ancient a writer as Tertullian as the foundation for the Paschal fast, were understood in this sense by the Apostles themselves and prompted them to sanctify by fasting, which they in general loved [^253], the annual anniversary of the Lord's death. Since this day fell upon the Jewish Passover, when the observance of Jewish feasts ceased among Christians, the latter could easily come to the thought of sanctifying by fasting precisely the day of the Jewish Passover in commemoration of Christ's death. Under the form of such a fast Pascha initially existed, as we shall see from the primitive testimony concerning it in the second century in St. Irenaeus. Even in the third century Pascha was reduced to a fast, was a Pascha of the Cross (*Pascha staurosimon*), alongside which even then there scarcely began to emerge as an independent feast the Pascha of the Resurrection (*Pascha anastasimon*)—under the guise of the solemn conclusion of the Paschal fast. This fast under the Apostles was probably broken by some on the very day of the Jewish Passover, and by others on the Sunday following it. According to the testimony of Socrates (5th c.), those who celebrated the feast of Pascha on the fourteenth day of Nisan, that is, on the same day as the Jews, asserted that this had been handed down unto them from the Apostle John, while those who celebrated Pascha on the Sunday following the Jewish Passover said that they had received their custom from the Apostles Peter and Paul. Yet neither the one nor the other, the historian remarks, can produce written testimony for this [^254].

## The Second Century

However much in common the second century of church history has with the third in the external life of Christianity (persecutions), and in its internal life (in the state of enlightenment, for example), nevertheless it (the 2nd c.) bears upon itself, especially in its first half, in the era of the Apostolic Fathers, a distinct impress bringing it somewhat closer unto the apostolic age. Nor could the divine worship of this century fail to have a distinct character. For divine worship this was a transition from apostolic freedom and primitive formation to those firm norms and stable rites (*chinov*) which the third century already presents unto us in abundance, especially in its canonical monuments. Therefore the second century fully

merits independent examination by the historian of divine worship.

### Sources

Indications concerning the divine worship of the second century can be found first of all among the writers of that era, that is, in the writings of the Apostolic Fathers and Apologists. Such are the Epistle of the Apostle Barnabas (A.D. 70–100), the First Epistle of St. Clement, Bishop of Rome (c. A.D. 96), the *Shepherd* of Hermas (A.D. 100–150), the Epistles of St. Ignatius the God-Bearer († 107 or 115), the Epistle of St. Polycarp of Smyrna († 167), the *First Apology* of St. Justin Martyr (A.D. 139), the *Epistle to Diognetus* by an unknown author, and the writings of St. Irenaeus of Lyon († 202). Important information concerning divine worship has also been preserved in the writings of pagan authors of that time—especially in the letter of Pliny to Emperor Trajan and in the satire of Lucian of Samosata (in the second half of the 2nd c.), *On the Death of Peregrinus*. Yet almost the most important liturgical monument of this epoch is the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* (*Didachē tōn dōdeka apostolōn*). The discovery of this monument by Metropolitan Philotheos Bryennios of Nicomedia in 1875 in the library of the Jerusalem Metochion in Constantinople and its publication by him in 1883 created an epoch in the study of the earliest history of Christianity. Traces of familiarity with this monument exist in Clement of Alexandria († 217) [^255]. Eusebius of Caesarea assigns it to the number of disputed and spurious books of the New Testament [^256], while Athanasius the Great assigns it to the non-canonical, but readable books—alongside the Wisdom books, Esther, Judith, and Tobit [^257]. Diverse scholars assign the date of composition of the monument to various years, ranging from A.D. 70 to the 5th–6th centuries; the majority and the most authoritative scholars date it to A.D. 100–135 (Funk, Harnack) [^258]. In favor of the great antiquity of the monument speaks the existence in its era of the ministries of apostles, prophets, and teachers, and *(Orig. p. 49)* the extraordinary simplicity of the monument. This monument lies at the foundation of Book VII of the *Apostolic Constitutions*.

### Divine Worship according to Pliny's Letter

As regards the cycle and composition of the divine worship of that time, the most circumstantial and valuable account concerning this we have in the well-known letter of the Bithynian proconsul Pliny Secundus (the Younger) to Emperor Trajan from A.D. 111–115 [^259]. Having conducted by order of the emperor an inquiry concerning the Christians, their teaching, and their conduct, Pliny reports [^260]:

"They (the Christians) affirmed that the whole of their guilt, or their error, was that they were accustomed on a stated day to meet before dawn (*stato die ante lucem convenire*) and to sing alternately among themselves a hymn to Christ, as to a god (*carmenque Christo quasi Deo dicere secum invicem*), and to bind themselves by an oath (*sacramento se obstringere*) not to any crime, but that they would not commit theft, robbery, or adultery, nor break their pledged faith, nor refuse a deposit when called upon. When this was done, it was their custom to depart, and to meet together again to partake of food, which was, however, ordinary and innocent (*promiscuum tamen et innoxium*). But even this they ceased to do after the issuance of my edict, by which, according to your commands, I had forbidden secret societies (*hetairias*)."

The letter is cited in Tertullian, wherein the passage concerning liturgical assemblies is rendered: "a pre-dawn meeting for singing (*ad canendum*) unto Christ and God and for a joint commitment to discipline (*ad confoederandam disciplinam*)" [^261], and in Eusebius, where this same passage is rendered thus: "the Christians arise very early and sing praise to Christ as God" [^262]. Pliny's testimony shows that among the Christians of that time there were, at least on feast days, two liturgical assemblies—one at early morning, the other by day or in the evening.

### Matins according to Pliny's Letter

The first assembly described by Pliny represents the most ancient type of morning divine service, Matins (*orthros* / *utrenya*). The assembly begins while it is still night, but evidently not long before dawn. By such an assembly the Christians partly violated the prohibition of nocturnal *(Orig. p. 50)* assemblies that was already contained in the Laws of the Twelve Tables. Yet it is possible that they were also forced into nocturnal gatherings by persecutions for the faith. However, Christians had also other, stronger motives for nocturnal prayer. Night by its mysteriousness in general disposed unto prayer and was chosen for

---

=== LEAF p47 ===
worship both by paganism [^263] and by Judaism [^264]. On the basis of certain evangelical sayings Christians could expect the Second Coming of Christ at midnight—a motive very strong for nocturnal assemblies, if one bears in mind the then intense expectation of this Coming. Of this motive Lactantius (4th c.) directly speaks: "We keep vigils at night on account of (*propter*) the coming of our King and God" [^265]. But, it seems, the strongest motive for nocturnal worship was the circumstance that the Resurrection of Christ took place at night, before dawn, and was discovered in the deep morning while darkness yet was. Every Matins in general was regarded by the ancients, as other monuments of the 2nd–3rd centuries testify (as we shall see below), as a service in honor of the Resurrection of Christ; especially was Sunday Matins bound to have such a meaning.

The composition of the morning service in the account of the pagan, unto whom, of course, Christian liturgical terminology was alien and completely incomprehensible, and who translated it into ordinary language, presents itself under the form of two parts: *carmen* and a certain oath, *sacramentum*.

***Carmen***—ordinarily means *verse*; by this word was designated everything written in verses: inscriptions, oracle pronouncements, laws, prayers, songs. Therefore here also this word could encompass both prayers and hymns in honor of Christ; it indicates that both were brief and possessed metrical form. The subject of these verses is Christ and the glorification of His divinity—which may indicate the predominance in the hymns of a purely Christian element and the insignificance of the Old Testament element (a conclusion in agreement with Ephesians 5:19 and the 3rd-century monument *Testamentum Domini Nostri Jesu Christi*, concerning which see below). The mode of execution of the "verses" is designated by the word *dicere*, which, although it can signify singing (it is so rendered by Tertullian), points rather to chanting alternating with reading or resembling it, recitative (an indication of the simplicity of primitive Christian singing). More particularly the manner of this reading or singing of the verse in honor of Christ is defined as mutual in the assembly—*secum invicem*. This expression is understood in the sense that the believers alternately sang each his own hymn—a practice unto which the Apostle Paul points in 1 Corinthians 14:26 (**"every one of you hath a psalm"**) and which still existed in the time of the Council of Laodicea (which by its 59th canon prohibited this improvisation by laymen of their own hymns) [^266]. But the addition to *invicem* of *secum* also rather indicates a joint reciprocal singing—antiphonal, hypophonic (with the humming / chanting of the final words by the entire *(Orig. p. 51)* assembly), or epiphonic (with the accession unto the singing on the part of the whole congregation of a special refrain [^267]). The testimonies of other monuments concerning the existence at that time of these genres of singing render such a sense most probable for

---

=== LEAF p48 ===
this expression. The circumstance that in a brief and official report this feature of Christian chanting is noted testifies that in divine worship antiphonal chanting was already beginning to win for itself that prominent place which it received later (in the 4th–6th centuries) in the so-called Sung Office (*asmatike akolouthia*) with its three-antiphon structure.

As regards the other part of the Christian morning service, for Pliny's enigmatic expression concerning it: "they bind themselves by an oath..." there has not to this day been found in scholarship a satisfactory explanation. To understand this literally of an oath is precluded by the general view of Christianity concerning oaths and the absence of any reports concerning the use of a common oath during divine worship. For baptismal vows [^268] this expression is too strong, and baptism could not be administered at every morning service. Rather artificial also is the explanation that here is meant the reading of the Word of God, in particular the Decalogue, containing precisely the prohibition of all the sins enumerated here and in the same order, a reading unto which was joined an exhortation to keep these commandments, unto which exhortation (sermon) the assembly responded "Amen," and this was a kind of oath; to designate the reading of Holy Scripture and preaching by such a term Christians could because they loved to call themselves the "host / warfare of Christ," *militia Christi*, and *sacramento se obstringere* ordinarily signified the military oath of allegiance. Perhaps here is meant some rite that subsequently fell out of use, yet a rite, apparently, exceedingly profound and awe-inspiring. Furthermore, this expression stands in connection with the accusation against Christians of forming secret societies (*hetairiai*), whose members bound themselves by an oath for solidarity in the execution of their partisan enterprises.

### The Agape according to Pliny

As regards the second assembly of Christians "on a stated day" (Sunday), Pliny's expression "to partake of food" leaves no doubt that here the Eucharist or the Agape is meant. The time of day at which the second assembly took place is not indicated; but its contrast unto the morning service and the practice of the apostolic era and the third century compel us to assign it to the evening, earlier or later. In the morning in antiquity they generally took no food, with the exception of the aged and young children; still less could there be a common banquet in the morning [^269]. A certain difficulty is presented by the closer definition given by Pliny of the food that Christians partook of at *(Orig. p. 52)* this assembly, that it was "ordinary and innocent." Hitherto this expression was understood as a counterweight unto the accusation against Christians of slaughtering children for the agapes [^270]. But such accusations against Christians began much later than Pliny: they are not mentioned by the earliest apologies: the *Epistle to Diognetus*, or the *Apology* of Aristides, written unto Trajan's successor [^271].

---

=== LEAF p49 ===
The first to mention such an accusation is St. Justin Martyr, wherein the invention and propagation of this accusation he attributes unto the Jews [^272]. The expression "ordinary food (*promiscuum*—public and common, *epikoinos* and *koinos*) and innocent" points unto the simplest and most modest food and is intended to show that Christians by their repasts do not violate the entire series of Roman laws issued not long before this time against luxurious banquets (*leges sumptuariae*), that is, those very *hetairiai* (or *sodalitates*) mentioned by Pliny [^273]. In particular, "innocent (*innoxius*) food" is frequently applied by writers of that time to fasting food without meat [^274]. Unto a special inclination of Christians toward such food point the Apostle Paul [^275] and Clement of Alexandria. Thus Pliny's testimony in such an understanding sheds curious light for us both upon the very food of Christian agapes and upon the first rudiments of Christian fasting diet; the agapes were modest and fasting repasts, perhaps with fish [^276]. According to the 2nd-century apocryphon *Acts of Paul and Thecla*, at the tomb there was "great joy" (*agapē pollē*, *gaudium magnum*): the youth brought "five loaves of bread with salt, and besides herbs and water" [^277]. Such food was more worthy of partaking with the Eucharist than flesh-meat.

### The Agape according to the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* and Other Monuments

Pliny's report leaves no doubt that at this time the Eucharist was celebrated jointly with the agapes. Unto the same practice points another monument of the same era, the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*. Here is found the *(Orig. p. 53)* prayer before the Eucharist over the cup and the broken bread, and the prayer after being filled (chs. 9 and 10). Both the very content of these prayers and the title of the second clearly demonstrate that the Eucharist was united with an ordinary repast. It is remarkable that in the *Apostolic Constitutions* (a 4th-century monument) these same prayers are cited in an altered form adapted to the Eucharist alone without a repast, wherein the superscription "after being filled" is replaced by "after communion" [^278]. The indicated prayers in the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* are accompanied by the observation that the prophets may "give thanks" (that is, pronounce prayers at the Eucharist) "as much as they will" [^279]. Consequently, since the monument had only local significance, the models of Eucharistic prayers given in the monument can be viewed as an individual attempt to replace prayer improvisation with a uniform formula [^280]. If such an attempt was being made regarding the Eucharist, then for other church services there was long bound to exist the former freedom in prayers and psalmody.

The celebration of the Eucharist jointly with the agapes in this era is confirmed also by St. Ignatius the God-Bearer: "It is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast," he writes

---

=== LEAF p50 ===
unto the Smyrnaeans [^281]; with baptism could be correlated only a sacrament.

Of other writers of that time, the author of the *Epistle to Diognetus* mentions the agapes, but too briefly for one to be able to extract new data on this subject: Christians "have a table in common, yet not an ordinary one" [^282]. The expression may speak for the inseparability of the Eucharist from the agape.

### The Liturgy and the Agape according to St. Justin Martyr

St. Justin Martyr in his *First Apology* makes it perceptible that in the mutual relation of the Eucharist and the agape there occurred by his time an essential change. The Eucharist had become so segregated into an independent service that in describing it St. Justin makes no mention whatever of any repast. Yet in connection with the Eucharist he mentions aid on the part of the wealthy unto the poor, which is ordinarily understood as an indication of the agape, which had already assumed a purely charitable character.

Here are the passages from St. Justin relating hereto. The newly baptized
"we bring unto those who are called brethren, into the common assembly, in order with all earnestness to offer common prayers both for ourselves and for the illuminated [newly baptized] and for all others everywhere, that we may be accounted worthy, having learned the truth, to be found by our works also good citizens and keepers of the commandments, so that we may obtain eternal salvation. Having ended the prayers, we greet *(Orig. p. 54)* one another with a kiss. Then there is brought unto the president of the brethren bread and a cup of water and wine." There follows a description of the Eucharist and an elucidation of its significance and institution, after which the Apologist continues: "And those of us who are well-to-do help the poor, and we always hold together with one another. For all things wherewith we are supplied, we bless the Maker of all through His Son Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit. And on the day called Sunday [day of the Sun] there is an assembly in one place of all who live in cities or in the country, and the memoirs (*apomnēmoneumata*) of the Apostles or the writings (*syngrammata*) of the Prophets are read, as long as time permits (*mechri enchorei*). Then, when the reader (*ho anagignōskōn*) has ceased, the president verbally instructs and exhorts to the imitation of these good things. Then we all rise together and offer up prayers. And, as we said before, when our prayer is ended, bread and wine and water are brought, and the president in like manner offers prayers and thanksgivings, according to his ability."
