## The Litiya according to the Present Typikon of the Great Church

The litiya according to the present typikon of the Great Church departs considerably from the usual type. Its order is given not in the Typikon, but in the *Hieratikon* [Gr. Ἱερατικόν] (the priestly *Sluzhebnik*).

For the litiya the priest with the deacon go forth into the middle of the temple, where the loaves are already prepared for the blessing. The deacon, upon the completion of the "litiya troparion of the saint celebrated on the morrow," says: "Have mercy on us, O God... Lord, have mercy (thrice). Again let us pray for pious and Orthodox Christians. Again let us pray for our Archbishop N. Again let us pray for mercy, life... of the servants of God who celebrate this holy feast and His servants—and commemorates the names of those who have offered the loaves.—Again let us pray that this holy church or monastery and this city, or village, or island, and every city and country may be preserved from famine... Each choir chants 'Lord, have mercy' thrice 12 times. Again let us pray that He may hear... Lord, have mercy 3." The bishop, vested in epitrachelion and small omophorion, or the priest says: "Amen. Hear us, O God...". The choir: "Amen. Peace be unto all. Bow your heads...". At the bowing of the heads of all, the bishop or priest says: **"O Master Great in Mercy"**, with the mention following the apostles: *(Orig. p. 596)* "our fathers among the saints, the great hierarchs and universal teachers Basil the Great, Gregory the Theologian, and John Chrysostom, Athanasius and Cyril, Patriarchs...

=== LEAF p713 ===
...of Alexandria, Nicholas of Myra, Spyridon Bishop of Trimythous the Wonderworker, the holy glorious great martyrs George the Trophy-bearer, Demetrius the Myrrh-streamer, Theodore of Tyre and Theodore the General, Menas the Wonderworker, and the Hieromartyr Charalampus", martyrs, venerable monastics, Joachim and Anna, "Saint N., whose memory we also celebrate." Immediately thereafter, upon the singing of **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** [*Bogoroditse Devo*], the blessing of loaves takes place, and Vespers concludes with the dismissal troparia of the Sunday and of the saint [^2340].

## The Return from the Litiya

The litiya returns into the temple in the same order in which it went forth thence, during the singing of the aposticha sticheron: "having raised ourselves upright (rising from knees or lifting the head after the bowing of heads), we sing the Sunday sticheron and enter into the temple, the candle-bearers going before, singing also the remaining stichera, which are alphabetical." The stichera are sung by both choirs together (see below), for which reason they stand in the middle of the temple in a semicircle before the table with the loaves. The priest, "preceded by lamps, goes through the Royal (that is, Beautiful) Doors and, as it were, enters into heaven. After the superior, under his leadership, the others follow, walking as if after Jesus Christ. And both choirs, having united into one and standing in the midst as a sign that our heaven has been opened and through Christ the Angels have entered into communion with us, chant the stichera of the aposticha" [^2341].

### According unto Ancient Typika

More detailed is the *Sluzhebnik* of Peter Mohyla: "the priest with the deacon, lamps going before them, enter into the church, and the brethren and all the people follow them." The Moscow *Sluzhebnik* of 1647: "the priest with the deacon depart into the holy altar and close the doors", that is, the Holy Doors. Yet the *Sluzhebnik* of 1658 generally states "enters into the church." In agreement with the *Sluzhebnik* of 1647 is the Old Believer *Sluzhebnik*, but their Typikon is like ours [^2342].

# The Aposticha (*Stikhovny* — *Eis ton stichon*)

The stichera "at the verse" (*eis ton stichon* [Gr. εἰς τὸν στίχον], more rarely *ta aposticha* [Gr. τὰ ἀπόστιχα]), or "aposticha" [*stikhovny*], represent the second and concluding series of festal chants at Vespers, which for this reason must already be elevated above the preceding series—the stichera at *Lord, I have cried*. Just as the prayers of Vespers continually increase in intensity with its progression, so also do the chants. Indeed, the aposticha stichera more fully extol the cele*(Orig. p. 597)*brated event by the very fact that they are joined not unto a daily psalm, but unto special verses (adapted unto the feast), whence their name is derived. Furthermore, they are called so also because at the litiya the stichera are without verses [^2343]. They dominate over the psalm (that is, over these verses) by opening their series without a psalm verse. On many feasts these stichera are more ecstatic than those of *Lord, I have cried*; for example, on Pascha they are the famous stichera **"A sacred Pascha"** [*Paskha svyashchennaya*]; they are chanted in the more intricate tones (3, 5, 7) and possess the most artistic and moving melodies (**"O House of Ephratha"**, **"When from the Tree"**). It is for this reason that they are performed by both choirs united in the middle of the temple, which for Sunday Vigil follows naturally from the preceding description (for ordinary Vespers this is specifically noted—see Chapter 9 of the Typikon).

## History of the Aposticha

An aposticha sticheron, as yet only one, is presumably intended in the famous narrative of the visit unto Nilus of Sinai by Sophronius and Moschus (7th c.) under the name of "troparion at *O Gladsome Light* or at *Vouchsafe, O Lord*": troparia are there called also the stichera at *Lord, I have cried*, while the term "sticheron" does not yet exist in this monument (Introductory Chapter, 295). Under their present name and in the quantity of several, the aposticha stichera are mentioned by the *Hypotyposis* (9th c.) under the name *stichēra tou stichou* [Gr. στιχηρὰ τοῦ στίχου] [^2344], by the most ancient Studite typika, and by the Evergetis Typikon; the most ancient Greek manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon calls them "stichera *tou stichou*" [Gr. τοῦ στίχου] [^2345].

## Sunday Aposticha

The aposticha stichera at Sunday Vespers are appointed in the number of 4, greater by 1 than for all other feasts, even the Twelve Great Feasts, excluding Pascha. They have their own Theotokia, extolling the Incarnation of Christ [^2346] or...

=== LEAF p715 ===
...containing supplications unto the Mother of God for deliverance from afflictions—without relation unto the event of the Resurrection, even as the Dogmatika—which is why they serve, like the latter, as Theotokia also for the feasts of saints (polyeleos and vigil rank); the melodies appointed for them are more solemn than for weekday Theotokia, close unto the melodies of the Dogmatika. As verses for the aposticha stichera there serve the same verses of Psalm 92 from which the Sunday evening prokeimenon is composed, that is, **"The Lord is King..."**, **"For He hath established the world..."**, **"Holiness becometh Thine house..."** (see above, p. 567). In the series of Sunday aposticha stichera, the first of them are noticeably distinguished from the remainder: they are homogeneous in structure and content with the first three stichera at *(Orig. p. 598)* *Lord, I have cried* and, in any case, more ancient than the other aposticha. These latter, called "alphabetical stichera" [*stikhiry po alfavitu*], as beginning in succession with the letters of the alphabet, constitute thanks to this for all tones one unified whole, divided into 8 sections. They are somewhat longer and more detailed than the first stichera. Ancient manuscripts of the typika attribute them unto St. John of Damascus.

### History of Sunday Aposticha

The Studite-Alexis Typikon knows only one Sunday apostichon, naturally, the 1st of the present ones; for the Sundays of the Triodion it prescribes doubling it and singing after it the Triodion sticheron; consequently, on ordinary Sundays it was tripled. Studite typika of the Calabrian and Sicilian recensions, besides this sticheron, name also 3 alphabetical stichera, the former also naming the author: "John." The Evergetis Typikon indicates after the 1st two alphabetical ones, designating them by the name of "John the monk of Damascus." The Georgian manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon indicates 3 alphabetical stichera with the name of St. John, assigning the 3rd at "Glory". Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon call them *anatolika ta kata alphabētou* [Gr. ἀνατολικὰ τὰ κατὰ ἀλφαβήτου], assigning unto them also the present verses [^2347]. The alphabetical arrangement of the stichera suggests the thought that they were originally all intended for a single service and, presumably, played in it the same role as kontakia. Their Theotokia are called by Western Studite typika *tou alphabētou* [Gr. τοῦ ἀλφαβήτου], and by the Evergetis Typikon *deuterotheotokia* [Gr. δευτεροθεοτόκια].

# "Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart" (*Nunc Dimittis* — *Nyně otpushchayeshi*)

Having provided a series of psalms both specially evening ones (103 and 140 with the following), regular ones (the 1st Kathisma), and specially festal ones (verses of Psalm 92), and having joined unto the psalms Christian hymns adapted both unto the evening hour (**"O Gladsome Light"**, **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**) and unto the feast (stichera), Vespers, nevertheless, would not have exhausted its psalmodic-hymnographic material had it not added unto all this yet something from the cycle of the so-called Biblical Canticles, which at Matins under the form of the Canon occupy so prominent a place.

*(Orig. p. 599)*

This lack Vespers supplies by the prayer-canticle of St. Simeon the God-Receiver from Luke 2:29–32: **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** (*Nunc Dimittis* / *Nyně otpushchayeshi*) [^2348]. Full of thanksgiving and tranquility before the face of death, flowing from a constant thought not of oneself, but of the world and the people, of their enlightenment and salvation, this "prayer" of Simeon is fitting for the evening, which reminds us of the twilight of our life (like **"O Gladsome Light"**, it calls the Savior the Light). Notwithstanding the highly poetic character of this prayer-canticle, the Typikon appoints for it not singing, but reading (as, presumably, the righteous Simeon himself performed it: "blessed God and said"). The Typikon acts likewise with other frequently repeated hymns (see above, p. 560). In return, it is "spoken by the superior", whereas the subsequent Trisagion is read by the reader (at daily Vespers it is permitted that the reader pronounce it as well). According to Symeon of Thessalonica, "one of the officiants, as it were Simeon the God-Receiver, pronounceth it with reverence" [^2349].

## History of Its Usage

**"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** is mentioned among the evening hymns immediately following the "prayer" close unto our **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** by the *Apostolic Constitutions* [^2350], and in the present place of Vespers—in the famous narrative of the visit unto Nilus of Sinai (7th c.) and in Horologia of the 8th c. It is possessed by Coptic and Abyssinian Vespers. Armenian Vespers, like its other services, likewise hath a Biblical Canticle. Roman Catholic worship hath this canticle at Compline with the antiphon ("Save us"), while at Vespers it hath the Canticle of the Theotokos (Introductory Chapter, pp. 295, 300, 301, 317). Anglican worship, which hath no Compline, placed the *Nunc Dimittis* at Evening Prayer [^2351]. According unto the Studite Typikon, *Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart* was chanted "in a raised voice" [*v"ly*] (see above, p. 560).

=== LEAF p718 ===
The most ancient manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon say nothing concerning the manner of executing *Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart*; yet judging by the fact that they attach this hymn unto the preceding account of the singing of the aposticha stichera without any intermediate rubric or with "then" (*eita* [Gr. εἶτα]), they presumably assume its singing.

## The Trisagion

Unto *Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart* is joined the Trisagion and its accompanying prayers through the **"Our Father"**, which thus conclude the service, its prayerful portion. This is also natural for such a prayer as the "Our Father" (cf. the Divine Liturgy), while the preceding prayers prepare for it. These prayers are pronounced already by the reader.

### The Trisagion at Ancient Vespers

With these prayers Vespers concludes (without a troparion) in the most ancient Horologion of the Sinai Library of the 8th–9th centuries (Introductory Chapter, 298). According unto the most ancient *(Orig. p. 600)* Roman rite, services concluded with the "Our Father" as a collect (the general dismissal prayer—ibid., 308). Germanus of Paris († 576) in his *Expositio brevis antiquae liturgiae gallicanae* [Brief Exposition of the Ancient Gallican Liturgy] observes that the "Our Father" occupies the final place at the Liturgy, "so that our every prayer may be concluded by the Lord's Prayer" [^2352]. The Studite Typikon appoints for these prayers the same singing as for *Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart* and the troparion (see above, p. 560). The Jerusalem Typika act as with *Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart* (see above, p. 599).

## The Dismissal Troparion of Vespers

The chants of Vespers, increasing in their solemnity, conclude with the highest of all kinds of ecclesiastical hymnody—the troparion, the very name of which (*troparion* [Gr. τροπάριον], from *tropos* [Gr. τρόπος] = *ēchos* [Gr. ἦχος], tone, melody) points unto the special complexity and richness of melody for it. In each tone the troparic melody constitutes the crown of all other melodies, of which perhaps only the prokeimenon can vie with it. Therefore the troparion in all services among the series of other hymns occupies the most honorable place, opening their series, as at Matins, concluding it, as at Matins and Vespers, or replacing all other kinds of hymnody, as at the Divine Liturgy and at the Hours (at...

=== LEAF p719 ===
...the latter together with the kontakion). By the place which the troparion occupies at Vespers, it is called "dismissal" (lit. *apolytikion* [Gr. ἀπολυτίκιον]). "Dismissal" troparia sometimes differ from the ordinary ones—thus, besides Sunday, in which also the final troparion of Matins is special, on Great Friday, where the dismissal troparion—of Matins, however—is **"For our sake crucified"**, while the ordinary one is **"When the glorious"**.

### History of the Dismissal Troparion

The troparion is the most ancient ecclesiastical chant, wherewith the history of ecclesiastical hymnody began its development (Introductory Chapter, 328–329). In the typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, which preserves the greatest number of traces of ancient practice, this is even the only chant. By such poverty of hymnographic material this typikon resembles Roman worship, wherein there is usually one hymn for each service and no more than two or three for each of the most important feasts. The hymn (*hymnus*) of Roman Catholic worship thus corresponds unto our troparion: it is there the only hymn not borrowed from Holy Scripture; very rarely, however, do the antiphons in Roman worship also present verses of original composition, not biblical.

The dismissal troparion in Patmos *Euchologion* No. 105 of the 13th century is appointed after a special "prayer of dismissal", and this prayer was adapted unto the feast; thus, the *Trebnik* of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 371/675 gives a series of such prayers for different choirs of saints and Twelve Great Feasts, calling them "prayer after the dismissal" or "dismissal prayer" [^2353].

# "Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos" (*Bogoroditse Devo*)

The troparion of Sunday Vespers (**"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** [*Bogoroditse Devo*]), according to the custom of the Orthodox Church which concludes each series of chants with a hymn in honor of the Mother of God, is dedicated unto Her. This is done also upon the same foundation on which the katavasia on Sunday is usually Theotokion (see below). For such a troparion there were chosen the most joyful words heard by the Mother of God and handed down unto us—the salutation unto Her of the Angel and of righteous Elizabeth; consequently, the troparion is composed of biblical (Luke 1:28, 42) divinely inspired words, which, nevertheless, are surrounded and interwoven with ours ("Theotokos [^2354] Virgin", "Mary", "For Thou hast borne the Savior of our souls"). The fundamental thought of the hymn, inviting the Mother of God unto joy ("rejoice" in the Hebrew and Greek languages, however, had the meaning generally of a greeting, corresponding unto Latin *ave* and Russian *zdravstvuy*, even as this word is rendered here in the Latin translation), is not without relation unto the event of the Resurrection, for which reason it has been assigned exclusively unto Sunday Vespers and unto Lenten Vespers—upon different grounds. There might also have been intended the ancient tradition that the Annunciation took place in the evening [^2355]. The Sunday troparion is not sung also on account of the conjunction of Vespers with Matins, at the very beginning of which it is appointed.

## History of the Troparion "Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"

The prototype of the troparion **"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"** is given in the commemoration prayer at the ancient Liturgy of the Apostle James; in the series of various commemorations (after the transubstantiation / consecration of the Gifts) in the most ancient copy of this Liturgy (Messanensis of the 10th c.) there is also this: "Remember, O Lord, the archangelic voice saying: Rejoice, Thou that art full of grace, the Lord is with Thee, blessed art Thou among women, and blessed is the fruit of Thy womb"; in later copies (Rossanensis of the 11th c.) unto these words there is added: "For Thou hast borne the Savior of our souls"; in still later ones (Paris Library No. 2509 of the 14th c.) before the angelic words there is omitted: "Remember, O Lord, the archangelic voice saying"; in still later copies (Paris Library No. 476 of the 14th c.) before the words, which had thus taken the form of an independent chant-prayer, there is added the rubric: "the priest subjoins (*synaptei* [printed: *synantei*, Gr. συνάπτει]) and says this thrice." Since these words at the Liturgy of the Apostle James were pronounced by the priest immediately before **"Especially for the Most Holy..."**, the Liturgies of Basil the Great and John Chrysostom derived therefrom in...
