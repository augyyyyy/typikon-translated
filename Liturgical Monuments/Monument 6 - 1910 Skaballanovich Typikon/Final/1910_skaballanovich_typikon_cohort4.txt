### Celebrants of Apostolic Worship

Prior to speaking regarding the employment of these gifts during divine worship, the Apostle enumerates them and their bearers ("And God hath set some in the church, first apostles, secondarily prophets, thirdly teachers...") [^128], thus presenting the latter as the celebrants of divine worship [^129]. This list of these liturgists alone can sufficiently characterize the divine worship of that time.

These were: 1) *Apostles*. Thus were designated not only the twelve closest disciples of the Lord, but also other persons who felt within themselves a calling from the Holy Spirit to preach the Gospel among unbelievers, and who must have been or*(Orig. p. 31)*dained unto this by the Church in a manner similar to that which was done, according to the Book of Acts, with Barnabas and Saul (through the laying on of hands of the charismatic persons of the Church) [^130]. Their purpose was the foundation of new churches; having accomplished this, they departed unto other places unenlightened by the Gospel, only visiting for a brief time the churches founded by them and existing churches in general for their confirmation [^131]. According to a monument of the second century, the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*, true apostles—who consequently existed also at that time—could be distinguished from false ones precisely by the fact that they did not remain in a single church for more than one, or at most two days, and took nothing from the community except bread in an amount necessary until the place of their next lodging [^132] (complete disinterestedness). The instructions given in the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* regarding false apostles [^133] demonstrate that in the second century this ministry was already close to cessation.

2) *Prophets*—persons capable of special revelations from God regarding the future [^134] and the secret thoughts of man [^135], that is, capable of "speaking in the Spirit" (a requirement not demanded of apostles), and who occupied themselves not with missionary work, but with preaching in already existing churches. The Book of Acts names the following prophets: Barnabas, Judas, Silas, and the four daughters of Philip the Deacon [^136]. A model of a prophet of the post-apostolic era is Hermas, the author of *The Shepherd*. The Apostle Paul insisted upon the broadest participation of prophets in divine worship [^137]. Prophets particularly astonished people during divine worship by their gift of exposing the secret thoughts of those present [^138]. This ministry enjoyed scarcely less reverence than the apostolic: "Prophets are your chief priests," says the *Teaching of the Twelve* [^139]. And according to the satire of Lucian (second century), the prophet among the Christians was a "thiasarch," the leader of sacred offerings [^140]. Unto prophets the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* permits at the Liturgy "to give thanks (*eucharistein*) as much as they will" [^141], whereas for ordinary celebrants of the Liturgy prayer formulas had already begun to be established. According to the same monument, a prophet "in the Spirit"

---

=== LEAF p32 ===
could "appoint a table," that is, require the celebration of an agape on an unappointed day. It was forbidden to test or judge a prophet speaking in the Spirit; even a prophet who teaches not to do what he himself does was not to be judged, because *(Orig. p. 32)* he has his judgment with God [^142]. The *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* reveals already many abuses in this charismatic ministry; nonetheless, the prophetic ministry existed somewhat longer than the apostolic [^143]. St. Irenaeus († 202) still heard prophets [^144]. In connection with prophecy the Apostle Paul places the gift of the discernment of spirits—receptivity toward prophecy, the ability to penetrate into the prophetic state and distinguish true prophecy from false in one or in diverse persons, to distinguish pure seizure by the Spirit of God from the impure admixtures of natural human or demonic excitements [^145]. Such discernment was necessary so that on account of false prophecies true prophecy might not be suspected: **"Despise not prophesyings; prove all things; hold fast that which is good"** [^146]; **"Believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God"** [^147].

3) In the Epistle to the Ephesians, the Apostle Paul, in enumerating charismatic ministries, places *evangelists* after apostles and prophets, before pastors and teachers. If by them are not to be understood evangelists in the contemporary sense—three of whom by this time had become known through their Gospels—it is difficult to say what ministry is meant. Perhaps these were the closest assistants of the Apostles in preaching the Gospel. Thus Timothy is also termed an evangelist [^148].

4) *Teachers*, in whose case "there was joined unto revelation the labor of personal penetration into divine truths" [^149], on which account teachers could assume this title according to their own personal judgment [^150], as is evident from the admonition of the Apostle James: **"My brethren, be not many masters [teachers]"** [^151]. In the gift of teaching the Apostle Paul distinguishes two kinds: the *word of wisdom* (charismatic eloquence) and the *word of knowledge*; by the former is perhaps meant practical or holistic, general, more subjective knowledge, while by the latter is meant theoretical, detailed, objective knowledge [^152]. The charismatic ministry of teaching was preserved also in the post-apostolic era down to the third century inclusive. In Origen there are frequent mentions of "ecclesiastical teachers," "persons who among us (in Alexandria) wisely fulfilled the office of teacher, and they are placed alongside presbyters" [^153]. Dionysius of Alexandria (second half of the third century) in an epistle states that he convoked "presbyters and teachers" of the surrounding vil*(Orig. p. 33)*lages. And upon teachers (*didaskaloi*) of the post-apostolic era people looked as upon especially graced persons: St. Ignatius the God-Bearer and Dionysius of Alexandria caution that they write not as authoritative teachers [^154].

All these extraordinary ministries (apostleship, prophecy, and teaching, joining here also the following—the gift of wonderworking; see below) were not limited unto any single local church, but extended

---

=== LEAF p33 ===
unto the whole Church, and were not so localized as the ecclesiastical hierarchy. The apostles, prophets, and teachers, who moved from one place to another and enjoyed high esteem in all communities, explain to us how diverse churches possessed among themselves so much in common in order and life, even though they were organized with complete autonomy [^155]. By their liturgical activity one may explain the affinity of liturgical forms in diverse churches which we observe in the third and fourth centuries.

Continuing the enumeration of charismatic ministries in the Church, the Apostle, after teaching with its gifts, names as a charismatic gift: 5) *Faith*—faith, as the nearest definition of this gift in other passages demonstrates, that works wonders, that "mother of signs," according to Chrysostom, the accomplishress of that which is beyond human powers. This charismatic gift, according to the Apostle, divides into the gift of healings and of powers [^156] ("powers"—wonders in general). According to St. John Chrysostom, he that possessed the gift of healings was able only to help the sick, but he who received the "operation of powers" was able also to punish [^157], as, for example, the Apostle Peter punished Ananias and Sapphira. The gift of wonderworking in apostolic times was a rather frequent phenomenon: the Apostle Paul indicates its presence also in the Galatian Church: **"He therefore that ministereth to you the Spirit, and worketh miracles [powers] among you, doeth He it by the works of the law, or by the hearing of faith?"** [^158]. Origen was a witness of many healings [^159]. A particular species of this gift was perpetuated in the Church by the Mystery of Holy Anointing (*Euchelaion*) [^160]. This charism must have provided the foundation for the establishment in the early Church of the office of exorcists, who adjured evil spirits (as the causers of diseases), and for the introduction into church services (not only the Liturgy, but also Vespers and Matins) of special prayers over the possessed (*energoumenoi*) or tempest-tossed (*cheimazomenoi*—that is, by unclean spirits).

6) The gifts of *helps* (*antilēpseis*) and *governments* (*kybernēseis*), which pertained to the permanent and local church hierarchy (the former to deacons with their care for the poor and the sick, the latter to presbyters and bishops). This ministry was not inferior to those enumerated above in general, yet during divine worship those extraordinary gifts and their bearers stepped into the foreground. Even in the second century a prophet was permitted at the Liturgy to improvise the Eucharistic prayer [^161], whereas a bishop and a presbyter were required to adhere to an established pattern. The *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles*, where these rules are given, in general lets it be seen that in its era (the beginning of the second century) the pastoral ministry had begun to replace the extraordinary ministries—apostolic, prophetic, and teaching. "Appoint (*cheirotonēsate*) [^162] therefore for yourselves," the monument exhorts, "bishops and deacons; for they also minister (*leitourgousi*) unto you the ministry (*leitourgian*) of prophets and teachers. Therefore despise them not, for they are your honorable men along with the prophets and teachers" [^163]. A passage testifying, moreover, unto what

7) and 8) The most extraordinary for our time, yet frequent in apostolic times, were the gifts of tongues and the interpretation of them.

### The Gift of Tongues according to Acts and 1 Corinthians in Apostolic Worship

The gift of tongues (glossolalia) was first imparted unto the Apostles, and perhaps unto the other believers, on the Day of Pentecost at the descent upon them of the Holy Spirit. This gift manifested itself in a certain extraordinarily inspired speech (in some measure recalling the speech of a person in a state of intoxication) ("to speak, to utter," *lalein, apophtheggesthai*) "of the wonderful works of God" "with other tongues" (*heterais glōssais*), such that listeners of fifteen nationalities heard from the mouths of the Apostles their own native dialect (*dialektos*) [^165]. The Spirit of the Lord, encompassing within Himself humanity in its nations and languages, and bearing within Himself the power of uniting them, produced in the innermost foundations of the soul an instantaneous removal of the national boundaries of language that most sharply divide humanity, symbolically prefiguring this future gracious unity of mankind.

A similar gift was imparted in the apostolic Church repeatedly after Baptism. Thus the gift of tongues was received by Cornelius and his household after Baptism, and by the disciples of John the Baptist through the laying on of hands of the Apostle Paul [^166]. It is not insignificant that the most powerful echo of the great wonder of Pentecost is found by us in so central and prominent a Gentile-Christian church as the Corinthian [^167]. In the Corinthian church this miraculous gift *(Orig. p. 35)* received the broadest employment during divine worship. It manifested itself, apparently, only at liturgical gatherings, "in the churches" [^168].

The content of the speech of the Corinthian glossolalists was prayer [^169], of a predominantly laudatory and somewhat mystical character [^170]. The tongue in which the glossolalist spoke was incomprehensible unto those present [^171], and, apparently, not entirely comprehensible unto the glossolalist himself (though his "spirit prayed" at that time, his "understanding was unfruitful") [^172], for which reason the speech of the glossolalist required interpretation [^173]; yet the interpretation of his speech likewise required a special gift of the Holy Spirit [^174], which the glossolalist was far from always possessing [^175]. Speaking with tongues was a coveted gift ("I would that ye all spake with tongues"), edifying the glossolalist [^176]; the degree of this gift depended upon the moral stature of the possessor, which is why, for example, the Apostle Paul possessed it more than all the Corinthians [^177].

#### Diverse Conceptions of the Gift of Tongues
Such are the biblical data concerning the gift of tongues. By them the question is not resolved

---

=== LEAF p35 ===
whether the gift of tongues was always identical, and in what the Corinthian glossolalia, which received so broad an employment during divine worship, actually consisted. The Holy Fathers inclined toward the opinion that the gift of tongues in Corinth, and in general in all instances where an account of its manifestation is given (that is, in the two other mentioned instances), was identical with the glossolalia of Pentecost—that is, that all glossolalists received the ability to speak in existing foreign languages. Thus thought Saints Justin, Irenaeus, Cyril of Jerusalem, John Chrysostom, Gregory the Theologian, and Augustine, as well as Tertullian and Origen [^178]. Against such a conception it is objected that for the Corinthian Christians there was no necessity of speaking in foreign languages such as existed on the day of the first Christian Pentecost; that tongues are not here termed "other"; that the word often stands in the singular and is not glossed as in Acts by *dialektos*; and that the Savior promised unto believers speaking with "new" *(Orig. p. 36)* tongues, and not foreign ones. Others [^179] see in the speech of the glossolalists a primitive tongue, a composite language containing elements or rudiments of diverse historical languages (a rudimentary language) and serving as a type for the universality of Christianity; the ground for this conception is the use of *glōssa* in descriptions of the gift of tongues sometimes in the singular. Yet the singular can signify some single one of existing languages—a sense confirmed also by the absence of the article before it. On the ground that *glōssa* or *glōssēma* among classical authors signifies a provincial, archaic, or unusual word (an idiom) requiring explanation (a gloss) [^180], it was thought that the language of the glossolalists consisted precisely of such words [^181]: now archaisms, now neologisms, now foreign expressions. Yet the New Testament knows no such use of the word *glōssa*; such speech is no higher than ordinary speech, and in any case presents no extraordinary miraculous phenomenon. It is thought further that the speech of the glossolalists consisted of inarticulate sounds (it is compared by the Apostle Paul with the sounds of a trumpet) [^182] or a scarcely audible prayerful whisper [^183] (1 Cor. 14:2: **"For he that speaketh in an unknown tongue speaketh not unto men, but unto God: for no man understandeth [heareth] him"**), or, conversely, of ecstatic exclamations and fragmentary doxologies (the speech of a glossolalist is called for the most part not *logos*, a word, the instrument of thought, but only *glōssa*, a tongue, sound in itself) [^184]. Yet the Apostle explicitly states that glossolalists pronounced "words," albeit "unintelligible"; he compares them with high-sounding cymbals and trumpets.

This latter comparison, repeatedly employed by the Apostle, gave occasion to understand glossolalia in the sense of some special kind of chanting. All the terms employed by the Apostle Paul concerning glossolalia can bear also a musical sense. *Glōssa* was the name given to the body of a flute [^185]; by "kinds" (*genē*) ("kinds of tongues") classical authors [^186] designated diatonic, chromatic, and enharmonic melodies, and in such an application this word is more natural than when applied to languages, which the ancients did not know how

to classify; *hermēneuein*, "to explain," is used among classical authors also of musical interpretation [^187]; in the expression **"and yet shew I unto you a more excellent way"** [^188], "way" (*hodos*) and "more excellent" (*kath' hyperbolēn*) likewise served as musical terms: the former signified the transition from one set of tones to another, while *hyperbolaion* was the name given *(Orig. p. 37)* to the tetrachord upon the fifth, highest string of the cithara [^189]. Of course, all these terms, besides their musical sense, can have also an ordinary meaning. Yet the persistent comparison of glossolalia with music and an entire array of terms that can have application unto the latter give cause for reflection. Significant also is the expression *apophtheggesthai*, "to utter / give vocal sound," regarding the glossolalia at Pentecost [^190]; according to the opinion of St. Irenaeus, the Apostles on the Day of Pentecost "sang in all tongues a hymn unto God" [^191]. One must also take into account the extensive use of singing in antiquity. Prior to the invention of letters, laws were chanted so that they would not be forgotten [^192]. All this gave occasion unto certain scholars to see in glossolalia singing [^193]. Against such a conception of the gift of tongues—in the sense of singing (hymnographic)—argues, apparently, the fact that regarding this gift the verb "to speak" (*lalein*) is persistently used. Yet, not to mention that this verb is used by the Apostle Paul concerning the execution during divine worship of psalms, hymns, and songs [^194], this objection vanishes if the singing was recitative [^195], as singing in its simplest form in the primitive Church with its as yet undeveloped liturgical forms could naturally be. Even Bl. Augustine says of the singing of his own time that the voice of the chanters resembled accentuated speech rather than singing [^196].

### Essence and Peculiarity of the Gift of Tongues

As we see, all the expounded explanations for the gift of tongues admit of objections against themselves, yet at the same time each of them possesses one or another serious foundation in the text of the New Testament.

All these explanations are reconcilable with one another, and it is possible that each of them points to an actual feature in this extraordinary phenomenon. The diversity of expressions of the Apostle Paul himself regarding the gift of tongues commands attention: "a tongue," "tongues," "kinds of tongues," "to speak with a tongue" or "with tongues," "to speak words in a tongue," "to pray with a tongue," "to have a tongue," "to sing," "to pray," "to bless," "to give thanks in the spirit," "in the spirit to speak mysteries," "to speak not unto men, but unto God" [^197]. This alone demonstrates that the gift of tongues could "infinitely diversify itself" [^198] in its manifestations. In order to judge correctly concerning the character of these manifestations, one must pay attention to the place assigned by the Apostle unto this gift among other *(Orig. p. 38)* spiritual gifts. In his classification of such gifts, the Apostle Paul singles out the gift of tongues with the gift of their interpretation into a distinct category:

---

=== LEAF p37 ===
arranging spiritual gifts according to the degree of benefit that they bring not unto an individual believer, but unto the whole Church, the Apostle in the first place sets the gifts of the intellect (teaching, prophecy), in the second place the gifts of the will (wonderworking, pastoral ministry), and in the last, evidently as manifestations of feeling—the gift of tongues [^199]. Consequently, speaking with tongues was the expression of a vivid inner movement and animation of those who for the first time felt themselves free from condemnation and experienced the unutterable joy inevitably bound up with the first (fresh) consciousness of divine sonship [^200]. This was an exultant joy of new relations toward God and men, of new and complete freedom in Christ, seeking for itself an extraordinary expression unbound by natural necessity. Here the entire matter lay in this inward elevation: how deep it was is evident from the fact that it was comprehensible only unto him who through the Holy Spirit came into inward contact with the one possessing such experiences—unto him who possessed the ability of interpretation. And this latter shared with the former his elevation, yet it took possession of him not to such an extent as of the former, and he was able to discern the divine object by which this elevation was elicited, and to express these feelings in clear words, translating into understandable language the unusual forms of their manifestation. Amid such depth of this feeling, it was natural that it availed itself for its expression of all means possible unto man [^201], choosing among them the most exalted and noble. Language is the most refined means of such expression. Yet, as the grammarian Psellus remarks, among every nation there are divinely transmitted names possessing an ineffable power which they, as it were, lose through translation; such, for example, are the Hebrew names *Seraphim, Cherubim, Michael, Gabriel* [^202]. The ancients ascribed unto foreign words a supernatural power [^203]. Language is no accidental production of a people, but the creation of its genius, the bearer of its ideas. That which was experienced by the early Christians was, evidently, higher even than so great a collective creation as the language of each nation, and needed the ideational wealth of all languages. It stood even above this latter. Bl. Theodoret by the tongues in which glossolalists spoke understands "certain noetic languages wherewith angels chant praises and hymns unto God" [^204]. For this reason the gift of tongues need not necessarily be understood as continuous discourse in foreign languages; the glossolalist could borrow therefrom the most expressive words; unto this, apparently, the expression of the Apostle Paul points: **"Ye have received *(Orig. p. 39)* the Spirit of adoption, whereby we cry, Abba, Father"** [^205]. That elevation in which the glossolalist found himself could impart unto his speech even in his native tongue an obscurity that required interpretation. His speech issued from those depths of the soul that are not illumined by the light of consciousness—from the spirit, and not the intellect. It is possible that this speech was supplemented by other expressions of feeling, for example by sighs, unto which, perhaps, the words of the Apostle point:

---

=== LEAF p38 ===
Paul: **"The Spirit Itself [Himself] maketh intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered"** [^206]. Intense spiritual animation could summon to the aid of the word also the sounds of singing, inasmuch as music in general expresses our feelings more powerfully than the word. (If so, we might comfort ourselves with the thought that not only the content of Christian prayers and hymns, but also their very melody was inspired by the Spirit of God and, of course, has preserved His seal down to our own time).

### Significance and Dignity of the Gift of Tongues

One thing can be stated with certainty concerning the gift of tongues: that from the internal side, as regards the state of the soul, speaking with tongues was a state of special spiritual, profound prayer. In this state man spoke directly unto God, with God, and penetrated into mysteries. This was a state of religious ecstasy [^207], for the accessibility of which unto himself the Apostle Paul warmly thanketh God [^208]. From the external side it was so sublime a phenomenon, entirely worthy of the Spirit of God, that for unbelievers themselves it was a sign demonstrating visually the presence of the Godhead Himself in Christian assemblies [^209]. This was a state of the highest spiritual elevation. Especially sublime in this phenomenon was the fact that, notwithstanding all the force of the feeling that then seized man, he did not lose mastery over himself; he could restrain and regulate the external manifestations of this state—remaining silent while another spoke, awaiting his own turn. **"And the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets"** [^210], testifieth the Apostle Paul. To such a degree did this state stand above any neuropathic-physiological ground.

And yet the Apostle Paul places the gift of tongues below prophecy. In the gift of tongues there was something, as it were, elemental, answering to that stage of development at which mankind then stood. In this gift there was something in common with those primitive forms of prophesying which it assumed, for example, under Saul [^211], and which in turn in its external forms recalled the mantic frenzy of paganism. The Apostle Paul, by his contrast of speaking with tongues unto prophecy, deprives of ground the inclination toward enthusiastic *(Orig. p. 40)* excesses and the darkening of clear Christian consciousness, as these had begun to assert themselves in Corinth. He closes before the Church the road upon which the Gnostics subsequently entered—the road toward the degeneration of this gift into orgiasm and magic [^212]. Through the conjunction of interpretation, speaking with tongues, like prophecy, received edifying power for all. In the time of St. Irenaeus († 202) speaking with tongues, although preserved, visibly transitions into prophecy. At least, the function of a prophet distinctive in the Apostle Paul—"to reveal secrets"—St. Irenaeus ascribes unto the glossolalist: "We have heard many brethren in the Church who possessed

---

=== LEAF p39 ===
prophetic gifts, and through the Spirit spake with diverse tongues, and for the common profit brought forth the secrets of men, and expounded the mysteries of God" [^213].

### Traces of Glossolalia in Ancient and Present Divine Worship

Nonetheless, as the expression of a profound, awe-inspiring religious feeling, the gift of tongues could not fail to exert a great and lasting influence upon Christian divine worship. It created that gracious breath which was preserved in this divine worship, and, when this gift ceased, was preserved by being cast into other forms. In later and even contemporary divine worship one can point out certain particulars that constitute traces of ancient speaking with tongues in divine services. From speaking with tongues one may derive, for example, the ancient liturgical exclamation **"Maranatha,"** appointed at the Eucharist in the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* [^214] and cited, presumably from liturgical practice, by the Apostle Paul [^215]. This exclamation is a Syriac phrase signifying "Our Lord hath come" (prophetic past) or "Come!": *Mar*—Lord, *an* or *ana*—our, *atha*—hath come, or *tha*—come. Thus the exclamation expresses the intense expectation of the near advent of Christ among the early Christians. Exegetes strive in vain to explain the Syriac language of this exclamation [^216]; yet on the supposition that it issued from the mouth of a glossolalist during divine worship, this oddity is explained naturally. Even St. John Chrysostom seeks only traces of glossolalia in the divine worship of his own time: "And now we have only signs of the gifts of that time. Even now we speak two or three and in turn, and when one falls silent, another begins; yet all these are only signs and monuments of the former things. For this reason, when we begin to speak, the people answer: **'And to thy *(Orig. p. 41)* spirit,'** expressing thereby that from antiquity they spoke thus not by their own wisdom, but by the inspiration of the Spirit" [^217].

Traces of speaking with tongues must also be such fragmentary words in the ancient sung office (*asmatike akolouthia*) as "Unto the universe, Alleluia," as well as in contemporary divine worship a certain disconnectedness and abruptness (and the mystery bound up therewith) of the exclamations at the most important part of the Liturgy (its Anaphora / "canon") and at the conclusion of Vespers and Matins. Of the same character is the exclamation **"Wisdom!"** (*Sophia*), indicating the solemnity not of those prayers and hymns before which it is proclaimed, but of the very moment of the church service itself (for example, the Entrance).

It is possible that a centuries-old echo of glossolalia in the former Graeco-Slavonic divine service was the so-called vocalized refrains (*popevki* / *terirems*), or the addition unto the text of a hymn, at especially solemn places, of extraneous syllables—a custom preserved down to the sixteenth century. In the oldest

of Russian typikons, that of the Moscow Synodal (Typography) Library of the 11th–12th centuries, at the Gradual Psalms (*anabathmoi* / *stepenny*) of Sunday Matins, which then replaced the present Polyeleos, there is appointed **"Alleluia"** with such vocal refrains: *"aleneluia aleneve enevene luia aleneve eneve neveneve eneve eneluia"* [^218]. In the Horologion of the Krakow edition of 1491, at the refrains of the Introductory Psalm of Vespers, **"Glory to Thee, O Lord,"** there are appointed such refrains: *"Glory to Thee, O Lord, anene naana neanene naaa inane, Who hast created all things"* [^219]. A trace of glossolalia is likewise the use during divine worship, and that too at its most solemn moments, of Hebrew and Greek words: **"Alleluia,"** **"Amen"** [^220], **"Kyrie, eleison"** [^221]. In glossolalia also has its foundation the aspiration toward creating a distinct liturgical language: in Roman Catholicism this legitimate aspiration, proceeding from the conviction that one must converse with God in a special, angelic language, is carried to an extreme, yet it is not alien unto all the Orthodox Churches: the Greek liturgical language (itacistic) was scarcely ever colloquial (as its etacistic form in the oldest Latin transcriptions demonstrates), nor was liturgical Slavonic [^222].

### Freedom and Norm in Apostolic Worship

With the abundance of spiritual gifts in the apostolic Church and their extensive employment during divine worship, the latter could not fail at that time to differ from our own by considerable freedom and diversity. The content of divine worship was determined by which of the charismatic persons participated in it, and what revelations or what inspiration each received during divine services. But since a distinctive property of Christian inspiration is that the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets—that is, the preservation during inspiration of full consciousness and self-mastery—such freedom and diversity of the divine worship of that time permitted a certain restriction through correct order and ritual (*ordo* / *taxis*), through free submission unto which it only gained in its grandeur and beauty. Thus already in this enthusiastic period of Christianity the foundation was laid for a strictly defined order (*chin*) of divine services. The entire indicated section of the First Epistle to the Corinthians has precisely the aim of introducing more order into liturgical assemblies, and in particular of determining the order of the employment during divine worship of spiritual gifts. The Apostle Paul desires that prophetic revelations, as providing edification not only unto the heart, but also unto the intellect, should prevail during divine worship over speaking with tongues, which yields unto prophecy by its indeterminacy and subjectivity: **"Desire spiritual gifts, but rather that ye may prophesy"** [^223]—in other words, the Apostle desires that the didactic *(Orig. p. 43)* element in divine worship should prevail over
