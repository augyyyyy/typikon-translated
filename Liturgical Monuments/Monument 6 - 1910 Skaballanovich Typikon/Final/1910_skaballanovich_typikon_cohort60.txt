the word *jubete* directors of public games addressed the consuls before opening them [^1991]. In all likelihood, in Byzantium as well a master of ceremonies in antiquity, only when addressing the emperor (or emperors, or the emperor with the empress), said "command" (*повелите*); the emperor, perhaps by a nod of the head *(Orig. p. 494)* or gesture of the hand, gave command, and the court dignitaries went forth from a given hall or entered into it. Yet, owing to the constant use of this word at exits/processions, it subsequently acquired the technical meaning of an invitation to go forth; it was pronounced in the absence of the emperor and was addressed not unto him, but unto such persons from whom the proclaimer not only does not seek permission, but himself invites them unto specific actions [^1992]. In the treatise *De cerimoniis aulae Byzantinae*, ascribed unto the Byzantine Emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus (912–959) and definitively redacted no later than the beginning of the 11th century [^1993], this exclamation has the following instances of application. Upon the conclusion of an audience in the palace (morning or evening), the emperor said unto the papias (the supreme sacristan of the palace): "Go, make the dismissal"; the papias took the keys and jingled them (as a sign of dismissal), a silentiary (one of the masters of ceremonies) entered and said "command" (*повелите*), and the gathering dispersed; thus, here this exclamation is an invitation to disperse. Yet it did not always have such a meaning. On Sunday, after the Liturgy in the palace church, the highest dignitaries (*protospatharioi*), having received an invitation to dinner, went into one hall (the Lausiakos), while those who had not received an invitation went into another (the Justinian, that is, the Hall of Justinian), and there sat waiting for the dismissal; unto the latter (as though from the emperor himself) the master of ceremonies (silentiary) came forth, proclaimed "command" (*повелите*), and they departed. Then the acolyth, at a given sign from the masters of ceremonies, proclaimed, addressing the droungarios (commander of the guard): "command"—the latter bowed unto the masters of ceremonies and took his stand upon an elevation in the hall, as though forming an honor guard of the hall. The acolyth, standing at the door from the first hall (the Justinian) into the other (the Skyla), where the other court dignitaries gathered after the Liturgy, proclaimed unto the *magistroi* and patricians: "command" (*повелите*); they entered into the first hall and sat down. The acolyth said, addressing the lower officials (secretaries): "command, secretikoi" (*повелите, секретики*),—they likewise entered into the first hall and sat down. With the same exclamation the acolyth then invites the military officials from the second hall into the first. Then the list of those invited to the imperial dinner was read; the praepositus (supreme master of ceremonies) permits the dismissal to be made; the silentiary proclaims "command" (*повелите*), the uninvited disperse, while the invited remain for dinner. Thus, most frequently

=== LEAF p592 ===
with the exclamation **"Command!"** (*повелите*) dignitaries were invited to pass from one hall into another [^1994]. The exclamation **"Command!"** (*повелите*) was also used at imperial processions [^1995]. Evidently, at the beginning of the All-Night Vigil this exclamation appeared in view of the usual presence of the emperor at it, and originally could have had the sense of seeking permission from the emperor to begin the service; yet the presented instances of its use permit us also to connect it with the procession of the *(Orig. p. 495)* priest forth from the sanctuary, after censing it, into the midst of the temple: the paraecclesiarch who utters the exclamation corresponds in this case to the court acolyth and, as it were, invites the priest to come forth from the sanctuary. In any case, this exclamation indicates the special solemnity of this moment and of this service (as of the others where it is used). It is characteristic that from the court ceremonial it penetrated even into purely monastic liturgical typika, such as the Jerusalem Typikon. Yet the noted hesitation of the ancient typika between the exclamations **"Command!"** (*повелите*) and **"Arise!"** (*возстаните*) may attest that the second was more ancient in a typikon of Jerusalem origin and was gradually displaced by the first, Constantinopolitan one. The Nikonian correctors, perhaps, had this in mind and wished to return unto the most ancient practice of the Jerusalem Typikon by adopting the reading **"Arise!"** (*возстаните*). In the present Greek Typikon, which follows Patriarch Philotheus in the order of the Vigil, it is *κελεύσατε* [*keleusate*] [^1996].

## Censing of the Nave

After the exclamation **"Arise!"** (*Возстаните!*), the censing of the middle part of the temple (the nave) begins. The priest censes first before the holy doors, then the icons on the right side of the iconostasis, then on the left, and finally the superior and both choirs according to rank (that is, first the right, and then the left); during this, the paraecclesiarch or deacon with a candle precedes the priest. While censing, the priest 'makes a slight bow'; his companion does the same. Since a censer without chains is presumed, a cross-wise censing is required: 'raising the censer straight (vertically) and across (transversely)'; today, as noted above, the cross-wise manner of censing is replaced by its threefold repetition. These directions of the chapter of the Typikon under consideration regarding the censing are somewhat supplemented in chapter 22. Thus, there it is noted that if the superior is not in the church, the icon at his place (above his stasidion) is censed. Concerning the censing of the choirs in chapter 22 of the Typikon, it is noted:

'He censes the entire right choir of the brethren twice, lifting up the censer before each straight and across, forming a cross (that is,

=== LEAF p593 ===
a censing of each of the brethren individually is required); and when he has censed the entire choir and taken his stand directly in their midst, then he forms a cross with the censer toward the entire choir and makes a bow unto them decorously, unto all as one, and they all likewise make a bow unto him; similarly he censes also the left choir and all the brethren standing in the church.'

Concerning the manner of this latter censing of 'the brethren standing in the church,' a more precise indication is given by the note (the second 'Note well' [*zri*]) of chapter 2 concerning the Polyeleos; there it is said that after censing the choirs the priest 'censes passing through (that is, walking through the temple, and not from one place) all the standing people.' This observation evidently presumes the absence in the temple of other icons besides those on the iconostasis, and therefore does not speak of censing them. In practice, however, the priest, walking through the temple, censes not only the standing people, but prior to them also the icons of the middle part of the temple.

*(Orig. p. 496)*

According to St. Symeon of Thessalonica (15th c.),

'through the censing, which begins from the sanctuary (*ἱλαστήριον* / altar), is depicted the fragrant grace of the Spirit extending everywhere (*μεταδοτικήν*)' [^1997].

Among the ancient typika, the briefest observation concerning the censing of the middle part of the temple is given by the *Diataxis* of Patriarch Philotheus:

'preceded by him (the *eutaxias*, corresponding unto the paraecclesiarch) with the candlestick, the priest censes the entire temple according to custom, having gone forth (starting from) from the (holy) doors' [^1998].

It is more complex in the Shiomgvime and Sevastyanov Typika (13th c.):

'having gone forth through the holy doors, *he censes there also cross-wise* (Sevastyanov instead of italics: "and having formed a cross with the censer opposite the sanctuary (*ἱλαστηρίου*, that is, the altar—into the open doors), he departs and censes"). Then *unto the chief* (absent in Sevastyanov) icon of the saint (that is, the patronal icon), likewise unto the hegumen (*τὸν καθηγούμενον*) and the choir *according to custom* (Sevastyanov: unto two choirs according to rank) preceded by the taper-lighter *with a lighted candle* (*τῆς λαμπάδος δηλονότι*); then he goes forth into the narthex...' [^1999].

=== LEAF p594 ===
Characteristic is the silence concerning other icons besides the patronal icon. Subsequent redactions replace this icon with icons in general; according to Slavic manuscripts of the 14th–15th centuries, 'he goes forth from the altar, preceded by a candle, and censes the holy doors cross-wise and the holy icons; thereafter he censes the hegumen thrice cross-wise or his place, and both choirs according to their rank; and he goes forth into the narthex' [^2000]. Subsequent redactions: '...the holy icons + which are on the right side and after them those which are on the left,' while omitting the remarks 'thrice' and 'or his place' [^2001].

The Old Believer Typikon restores the remark concerning the patronal icon:

'after the holy doors he censes the icon of the saint whose temple it is, likewise he censes in order the holy icons which are on the right side, and after them those which are on the left side' [^2002],

being in other respects like our own. Characteristic is the silence concerning the censing of the people, which is preserved also in the present Typikon (only in this chapter): in ancient monasteries all the brethren sang, forming two choirs.

## Censing of the Narthex

After the nave, the censing of the narthex is appointed; the narthex, as noted earlier, often yielded little in size unto the middle part of the temple and had to accommodate all the brethren in view of the fact that many services were celebrated here. In addition, the censing of the narthex is a remembrance of the ancient ecclesiastical institution of catechumens and penitents, who were not admitted into the temple itself. Finally, the censing of the narthex could have in view the departed who were buried here [^2003].

*(Orig. p. 497)*

The brief remark of the present chapter concerning the censing of the narthex: 'he goes forth also into the narthex and censes according to rank also the brethren being there'—is likewise supplemented in chapter 22 of the Typikon: the censing of the narthex, according to this chapter, begins with the priest 'forming a cross with the censer before the Beautiful Gates'—the principal doors from the narthex into the temple—just as the censing of the middle part of the temple began with a similar rite before the holy (royal) doors; 'then he censes on both sides the holy icons and all the brethren according to their rank; and finally, returning unto the Beautiful Gates, facing toward the west, he censes all cross-wise'—that is, a general censing of all is performed, as earlier each was censed individually ('according to rank').

The remark of the present chapter concerning the censing of the narthex

is preserved unchanged in the Typikon from its most ancient manuscripts: *ἐξελθὼν δὲ εἰς τὸν νάρθηκα καὶ θυμιάσας καὶ ἐνταῦθα τοὺς ἀδελφοὺς κατὰ τάξιν ὑποστρέφει* ('and having gone forth into the narthex and censed also there the brethren according to rank, he returns') [^2004]. In the Shiomgvime manuscript 'brethren' is absent [^2005].

# THE OPENING EXCLAMATION

## "Bless, Master!" ("Lord, Bless")

After the censing of the narthex the priest, as the present chapter of the Typikon states, 'having returned again into the church and taken his stand in the midst of both choirs (before the holy doors?), signs a cross with the censer, facing toward the east,' that is, he censes a second time toward the open holy doors, returning for the conclusion of the censing unto its beginning, 'and proclaims in a loud voice: **"Lord, bless!"** (*Господи, благослови*).' [Note: In Byzantine Greek this is *Κύριε εὐλόγησον*, rendered in Slavonic as *Господи благослови* and commonly in liturgical English as **"Bless, Master!"**].

By this exclamation the priest asks a blessing for the beginning of the service from the superior; for this reason, if a deacon concelebrates with him, the latter pronounces this exclamation, asking thereby a blessing from the priest, as before the Liturgy with the exclamation: **"Bless, Master"** (*Владыко, благослови*) [^2006]; by a similar exclamation ("In the name of the Lord, Father, give the blessing" / *Именем Господним благослови, отче*) the reader asks the priest's blessing at the service of the Hours. (In these exclamations there is a certain gradation of address corresponding unto the importance of the service: master [*vladyko*], lord [*gospodi*], father [*otche*] [^2007]).

Concluding the censing, the priest censes a second time all the most important objects with which he began it: after the holy doors, the icons surrounding them, the superior (for the nave), and the holy table (for the sanctuary): 'and immediately he censes the icon of the Savior Christ and of the Theotokos, and the superior at his place, and having entered into the holy altar, and taken his stand before the holy table, he signs a cross with the censer thrice, and then *(Orig. p. 498)* proclaims: **"Glory to the Holy..."**' Thus, the entire censing must conclude, according to the Typikon, by the beginning of the All-Night Vigil, and take place without words and hymns, in reverent silence, concentrating thought upon itself.

This section in the present Typikon has likewise received a certain alteration in comparison with the ancient ones. According to these latter, the exclamation **"Lord, bless!"** (*Господи, благослови*) was pronounced by the priest upon entering from the narthex into the temple at the 'royal doors,' that is, at the principal doors from the narthex into the nave. Thus according to the Sevastyanov MS (13th c.):

after the censing of the narthex the priest *ὑποστρέφει καὶ εἰσελθὼν εἴσω τῆς βασιλικῆς πύλης χαράττει μετὰ τοῦ θυμιατοῦ σταυρὸν καὶ ἐκφωνεῖ μεγαλοφώνως: κύριε εὐλόγησον*—'returns (from the narthex into the temple) and, having entered within the royal doors, signs a cross with the censer and proclaims in a loud voice: **"Lord, bless!"**' [^2008].

=== LEAF p597 ===
According to Slavic typika of the 14th–15th centuries:

'and turning toward the royal doors, he censes cross-wise and proclaims...' [^2009].

Yet according to the order of Patriarch Philotheus it is indefinite:

'he enters through the Beautiful (*ὡραίων*) Doors and proclaims melodiously (*μετὰ μέλους*)' [^2010].

Likewise indefinite in the Shiomgvime manuscript:

'he enters into the church through the royal doors; having signed a cross with the censer, he proclaims in a loud voice...' [^2011].

Hence arises the ambiguity also of the present Typikon regarding the place where the exclamation is pronounced. The present redaction of this passage is found in manuscripts of the 16th century [^2012] and in the Old Believer Typikon [^2013].—Nor did the second part of this section remain without change, regarding the actions of the priest from the exclamation **"Lord, bless!"** (*Господи, благослови*) unto the exclamation **"Glory to the Holy..."** (*Слава Святей*). The simplest practice here is given by the Shiomgvime manuscript:

'afterwards he censes the hegumen or his place, enters into the altar, and takes his stand before the ciborium; having depicted a cross twice with the censer, on the third time he proclaims: **"Glory to the Holy..."**' [^2014].

The concluding censing here is performed only unto the hegumen, since the blessing for the exclamation (**"Lord, bless!"**) is asked of him. The Typikon of Patriarch Philotheus, instead of 'the hegumen,' has: 'again the holy icons' [^2015], and concerning the censing of the holy table simply: 'censing.' The Sevastyanov MS: 'the superior or his place, then the saint (*τὸν ἅγιον*),' that is, the icon; later Greek MSS (14th c.): 'the saint of the monastery' (*τὸν τῆς μονῆς ἅγιον*) [^2016]. Slavic MSS of the 14th–15th centuries in place of the latter: 'the festal icon.' Manuscripts of the 16th century and the Old Believer Typikon: 'he censes the hegumen at his place (Old Believer: or his place, if it happen that the hegumen be not there) and again the previously censed holy icons as before, and then the Saint whose temple it is'—thenceforth as in our practice [^2017].

*(Orig. p. 499)*

## "Glory to the Holy..."

As the initial priestly exclamation for the All-Night Vigil, there was chosen not the exclamation of Vespers, which occupies the first place in the Vigil, but that of Matins, as the more important service. This exclamation (in Greek: *Δόξα τῇ ἁγίᾳ καὶ ὁμοουσίῳ καὶ ζωοποιῷ καὶ ἀδιαιρέτῳ Τριάδι, πάντοτε, νῦν, καὶ ἀεί, καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων, ἀμήν*—**"Glory to the Holy, Consubstantial, Life-Creating, and Undivided Trinity, always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages, Amen"**), as was noted also above [^2018], glorifies God, although not from so general a point of view as the Vesperal exclamation (**"Blessed is our God"**), yet neither with such elevation and expressiveness as the Liturgical one (**"Blessed is the Kingdom"**); namely, it glorifies the Holy Trinity only in Her indivisibility and general action upon the world (not in the economy of grace, like the Liturgical exclamation),—according to the explanation of this exclamation in St. Symeon of Thessalonica: 'before every song theologizing concerning the Cause of all and Master, the God of all One in Trinity, and giving doxology unto Him and thus laying the beginning for the hymnody of Him Who is the beginning and end of all' [^2019].

The exclamation **"Glory to the Holy..."** must have arisen from the Evangelical verse **"Glory to God in the highest."** In the most ancient typika, Matins during the Forty Days' Fast (the worship of which in general bears upon itself many traces of primitive practice) does not have the exclamation **"Glory to the Holy..."**, but after the usual beginning with the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** begins with **"Glory to God in the highest"** [^2020] (as in general in antiquity services sometimes lacked anything corresponding unto the present initial exclamations [^2021]). In its original form, the exclamation perhaps resembled the formula in the present rite for the consecration of temples and antimenses (which has, in all likelihood, a Constantinopolitan Hagia Sophia origin and has been preserved very stably since the 11th–12th centuries): "Glory to Thee, Holy Trinity, our God, unto the ages of ages" [^2022].

The present exclamation has its simplest form in the most ancient manuscript of the Studite Typikon of the Moscow Typographical Library of the 11th century in the order of Paschal Matins (in other places of this Typikon it is not cited): "Glory to the Consubstantial Trinity, always, now and ever" [^2023]. In a manuscript of the same Typikon of the 12th century there is already added:

*(Orig. p. 500)*

"Holy" [^2024]. In a subsequent redaction of the exclamation, represented by a series of manuscripts of the 12th–13th centuries, unto these two adjectives there is joined "Life-creating" [^2025]. Close to this, yet perhaps more ancient than it, judging by the expressed hypothesis regarding the very original form of the exclamation, is the following: "Glory to the Holy, Consubstantial, and Life-creating Trinity, our God, always..." [^2026]. The present form, apparently, is first found in Greek typika of the 13th century [^2027]. Attempts were also made to expand the exclamation beyond its present form. Thus, the most ancient

=== LEAF p599 ===
Slavic manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon have after "Trinity" the addition: "To the Father and to the Son and to the Holy Spirit" [^2028]. In St. Symeon of Thessalonica (15th c.), unto the four present adjectives there is added yet a fifth: "All-powerful" (*παντοδυνάμῳ*) [^2029]. Before "now" (*nyně*) there is sometimes present, sometimes absent "and" (*i*) [^2030]. *Ἀδιαιρέτῳ* [*Adiairetō*] in Slavic is translated sometimes as "Undivided" (*Нераздельней*), sometimes as "Indivisible" (*Неразделимей*); the first, apparently, is more ancient [^2031]; the second translation is more frequent [^2032] and was adopted by the Old Believer Typikon.

# THE INTRODUCTORY PSALM

## The Singing of "O Come, Let Us Worship"

Since the so-called "Usual Beginning" of church services was performed recently, before the Ninth Hour, and by its sorrowful-prayerful character would not correspond unto the solemn beginning of the All-Night Vigil, it here takes the shortened form of the verse **"O come, let us worship"** (*Приидите поклонимся*), which receives a rounding addition: **"O come, let us worship and fall down before Him."** This verse, with the responsive **"Amen"** preceding it, follows immediately upon the initial exclamation of the Vigil. Moreover, in view of the solemnity of the moment this verse is sung (concerning singing it is not explicitly stated, but this follows from the singing of the introductory psalm that is joined directly unto it, and is so done in practice), and it is sung not in the usual, but in an exceptional manner, as are also other most important prayers of the services—by the superior alone or by the ecclesiarch, and moreover with a gradual strengthening of the voice upon each of the three verses (to express the gradually enveloping prayerful inspiration):

'the superior (*ὁ προεστώς*) or ecclesiarch begins: **"Amen. O come, let us wor-** *(Orig. p. 501)* **ship..."** in a low voice (*χαμηλῇ καὶ ἡσύχῳ φωνῇ*). Then the second, somewhat higher (*εἶθ' οὕτως ἀνώτερον πρὸς ὀλίγον*): **"O come, let us worship..."** Again the third time in a higher voice thus (*πάλιν τὸ γ' ἀνωτέρᾳ φωνῇ οὕτως*): **"O come, let us worship..."** Then alone (*μόνον*): **"O come, let us worship..."** And immediately he begins in a higher voice (*ἀνωτέρᾳ φωνῇ*) in Tone 8: **"Bless the Lord, O my soul"** slowly and with sweet singing, the rest of the brethren singing along (*ἀργὰ καὶ μετὰ μελῶν, συμψαλλόντων καὶ τῶν λοιπῶν ἀδελφῶν*).'

Tone 8 for the singing of the introductory psalm was chosen as the most calmly solemn: as such, it is used predominantly for singing the concluding stichera; among the feasts it dominates on Pentecost and the Exaltation; in it also the Akathist is sung as a hymn of victory. However, Tone 8 for this psalm has not its usual chant (melody), but a special one, as indicated by the note 'with sweet singing.'

The development of the verse **"O come, let us worship"** unto its present
