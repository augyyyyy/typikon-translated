### The Prologues

Among subsequent recensions of the Greek Menologion, one must note the verse (metrical) *Prologue of Christopher of Mytilene* of the 11th century, which is very close unto the Studite-Jerusalem Menologion; next, the *Prologue of a certain Peter* containing commemorations down unto the 10th century, in a manuscript probably of the 13th century (St. Petersburg Public Library, from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius). The latter has 70 commemorations more than the Menologion of Basil, lacks 70, and includes 140 new ones: several are transposed unto other days in accordance with the Jerusalem Typikon; all the Syrian ascetics from the *Historia Religiosa* (*Philotheos Historia*) of Blessed Theodoret are incorporated and in the same sequence; many of the Seventy Apostles are missing (by comparison with the Menologion of Basil) as is their Synaxis. Close unto it is the *Sirmondian* (named after its owner, Fr. Sirmond) or *Claromontane* (named after the Parisian college) Synaxarion, or Prologue, compiled in the 11th–12th centuries. According unto the Menologion of Basil or the latter two Prologues, the *Verse Prologue* in the 1295 manuscript of the Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 390 and others was compiled; it is closer than they are unto the Jerusalem Typikon, yet it still lacks the Three Hierarchs, George Chozebite and Emilian on January 8, Theoktistos on January 4, Leo of Catania on February 20, Basil the Decapolite on February 28, the Great-Martyr George on November 3; on other dates Cosmas of Maiuma, Andrew of Crete, Paul of Thebes, Venerable Malachi, the Apostle Archippus; and in a different sequence the saints of September 21, January 4, and February 27. A redaction of the Menologion of Basil is likewise represented by the most ancient Slavic *Prologues* in manuscripts of the 12th–14th centuries; here the daily saints are already arranged according unto the dignity of their choirs—prophets and apostles coming first [^1764].

### The Evergetis Menologion

Among subsequent recensions of the Greek Menologion, one must note that provided by the *Evergetis Typikon* (see above, p. 363). It wavers between the Studite and the Jerusalem (see below), not infrequently departing from both; of present-day commemorations, it lacks those specifically of Jerusalem: September 1, the Dedication (*Enkaínia*), *(Orig. p. 402)* October 11, Theophanes the Confessor; October 28, Stephen the Sabbaite; November 3, the Great-Martyr George; January 8, George Chozebite; May 7, the Apparition of the Cross; it lacks the following Constantinopolitan saints and feasts: August 30, the Three Patriarchs; June 14, Patriarch Methodius; July 19, Venerable Dius; December 30, Venerable Zoticus; October 17, Andrew of Crete; December 29, Marcellus; November 9, Matrona; April 4, Joseph; August 1, the Procession of the Cross; October 28, the Earthquake (*seismos*); it contains not a single Athonite saint; it lacks the Synaxis of the Seventy Apostles, Cassian on February 29, Benedict on March 14, Ezekiel on July 21; on other dates it assigns John of Damascus unto December 2, Jonah the Prophet and Jonah the Presbyter unto September 21, and the Apostle Philemon unto November 23.

### The Studite Menologion

*The Studite Menologion was the penultimate step toward the establishment of the present one.* Yet it exhibits considerable variety across its various witnesses—in the Menaia and Typika. The most ancient of the Greek Studite Menaia—those of Grottaferrata of the 11th–12th centuries—contain, for example, on November 16, besides the Apostle Matthew, the Martyr Barlaam; they lack Paul of Thebes on January 15;

=== LEAF p382 ===
on January 20, rather than the 26th, Xenophon; they lack Venerable Benedict on March 14; contrary unto the Studite Typikon, they contain on September 13 the office of the Dedication (*Enkaínia*). Still closer unto the Jerusalem Typikon are the Greek Studite Menaia from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius in the St. Petersburg Imperial Public Library of the 12th century; its most significant differences from the latter are only on January 4, Martyr Phostirios rather than the Seventy Apostles; on January 5, Paul of Thebes; it lacks the Dedication, Cassian, and Benedict; and it contains three Italo-Sicilian saints. The Menologion of the Greek Studite Menaia often diverges from that of the Western Studite Typika, which, with the exception of that of Grottaferrata, are closer than they unto the Jerusalem Typikon. In the Menologion of the *San Nicola di Casole Typikon* (see above, p. 402), which does not indicate commemorations for every day, the Dedication is found on September 13; Western commemorations occur; and local peculiarities appear, such as December 7, the Apodosis of St. Nicholas, and December 8, the forefeast of the Conception of St. Anna. The *Mili* (*San Salvatore di Messina / Mili*) Menologion wavers among the Studite, Evergetis, and Jerusalem types; it contains commemorations found in no other Menologia, including several ancient Western ones. The Menologion in the *Studite-Alexis Typikon* is still closer unto the Jerusalem. It is not identical across the different copies of the Typikon (see above, p. 361). In the most ancient witness of the Studite-Alexis Typikon of the Moscow Typography Library MS No. 285/142/1206 of the 11th–12th centuries, there is no Menologion section proper, but it is replaced by a Kondakarion, wherein, evidently, only those saints who possess kontakia are listed; 105 days in all; all commemorations fall on their present dates, yet of Russian and Slavic saints there is not a single one. The Menologion in the Studite-Alexis Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 330/380 of the 12th century is likewise incomplete: approximately 160 days; all commemorations are the present ones with the addition of several Studite fathers, but it lacks 12 commemorations found in the Kondakarion as well as such customary ones as the saints of December 21–24 (doubtless because of the forefeast), Theodore the General (*Stratelates*) on February 8, Mary of Egypt on April 1, Isaiah on May 9, Nicephorus of Constantinople on June 2, Florus and Laurus on August 18, and the Apostle Bartholomew on August 25; of Slavic and Russian saints it has only Boris and Gleb on July 24. More than these redactions, the Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 333/382 of the year 1398 bears the imprint of Constantinopolitan provenance: thus on October 20 it assigns saints found *(Orig. p. 403)* only in the Typikon of the Great Church; on June 5 and August 7, commemorations of deliverance from enemies; on August 14, the Dedication of the temple entirely according unto the order of the Great Church; it has commemorations for all days; there are 3–4 commemorations unknown unto any other Menologia: of Russian saints, Vladimir, Olga, Boris, Gleb, Venerable Theodosius, and Leontius of Rostov; it also has Methodius, Bishop of Moravia, on June 20, and the Martyr Wenceslaus (*Vyacheslav*) on October 28. Close unto it is the Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 383/905 of the 15th century; mostly with one commemoration per day; June 15, Venerable Isaiah; June 23, the restoration of the narthex of St. John the Forerunner, as also on August 14 of the Church of the Theotokos; 5–6 commemorations fall on unusual dates. Richer in commemorations than it is the Typikon of the Moscow Typography Library MS No. 1444/1236 of the 14th century; it likewise contains several commemorations on unusual dates; there are repetitions of commemorations side by side on two consecutive days, and even on the same day. The Menologion of the *Slavic Studite Menaia* in the Moscow Typography and Synodal Libraries of the 12th–14th centuries coincides in its dates with the Menaia, excepting Venerable Joannicius on November 3; Cyril and Methodius are present on February 14 and April 6, and

### The Jerusalem Menologion

*The Menologion in the witnesses of the Jerusalem Typikon—both Typika and Menaia—is more uniform than that of the Studite.* The most ancient *Greek* Typika of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum, Sevastyanov Collection MS No. 35/491 of the 13th century and the Moscow Synodal Library MSS Nos. 456 of the 13th century and 381 of the 13th–14th centuries, contain for the first time on January 8 George Chozebite; on October 14 (not the 12th, as at present) Cosmas of Maiuma; on November 3 the Dedication of the Church of the Great-Martyr George in Lydda; supernumerary commemorations compared with the present Typikon include: September 1, the Great Fire (in Constantinople in 468 AD); September 4, additionally Martyr Babylas of Nicomedia and 84 children (4th century), Hermione and Eutychia, sisters (in others—daughters) of Deacon Philip; July 4, Michael Choniates, Archbishop of Athens (13th century); while they lack the present-day: September 6, the Apostle Archippus; September 22, Jonah the Prophet and Jonah the Presbyter; October 15, Euthymius the New; November 29, Acacius of the Ladder; December 13, Martyr Lucia; May 31, the Apostle Hermas; June 12, Peter of Athos; on July 5 Athanasius of Athos was added by a later hand. The *Slavic* Jerusalem Typika repeat the Menologion of the Greek ones almost in its entirety, sometimes revealing traces of even greater antiquity; for instance, the Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 328/383 of the 14th century contains on September 6 Venerable Eudoxius, on December 23 Venerable Niphon, and on February 28 Patriarch Proterius. In varying quantities, different copies of the Typikon introduce specifically Russian and Slavic commemorations; thus in MS No. 328/383 of the 14th century there are the Protection (*Pokrov*), St. Nicholas on May 9, Venerable Theodosius, St. Vladimir, Boris and Gleb twice, Ignatius of Rostov, and St. Cyril on February 14; in MS No. 329/384 of the 14th century the days of the repose of Metropolitans Peter and Alexis of Moscow are also noted (by a later hand), but in MS No. 678/386 of the 15th century there are already fewer Russian commemorations: Venerable Theodosius, St. Vladimir, Boris and Gleb, Venerable Sergius, Metropolitan Peter, and Bishop Ignatius of Rostov (the last three appended later); on the contrary, MS No. 336/388 of the 16th century contains not only more Slavic and Russian *(Orig. p. 404)* saints than the present Typikon, but also more Greek commemorations than the Greek Typikon, for instance on November 3 and 14 Gregory Palamas, and on January 28 Isaac the Syrian. Manuscript Slavic *Menaia* of the Jerusalem type present even greater variations: the majority have Martyr Conon on March 4, and Venerable Gerasimus on March 5; in one manuscript Hypatius of Gangra is on November 16 rather than March 31; in 16th-century Menaia there appear offices for Stephen of Surozh on December 15, Venerable Parasceve on October 14 (from South Slavic Menaia of the 14th century), the Great-Martyr Parasceve on October 28, and Venerable Andrew on October 2. For the first time among Athonite saints, besides Athanasius and Peter, there appear in 14th–15th century Menaia Michael Maleinos on July 12 and Euthymius the New on October 15. In Slavic and Russian saints the Menaia are poorer than the Typika; offices for them were customarily written at the end of the book, and prior unto the 15th century they are scarcely present; the first to appear are the commemorations of Venerable Sava on January 14 and Arsenius of Serbia on October 28, and later those of the Bulgarians: John of Rila on October 19 and

Hilarion of Moglena on October 21; Cyril and Methodius are absent. Of Russian saints in 14th–15th century Menaia there are occasionally offices for the Protection (*Pokrov*), St. Vladimir, the Sign (*Znamenie*) on November 27, and Barlaam of Novgorod on November 6.

### The Roman Catholic Liturgical Year

*The present Roman Catholic calendar was the outcome of the above-described gradual abbreviation and adaptation of the Pseudo-Hieronymian martyrology.* It acquired its present form under Pope Gregory XIII, who entrusted its reform unto Cardinal Guglielmo Sirleto; the latter accomplished this by 1584 with the assistance of the renowned historian Baronius and the astronomer Lilius, adopting the Martyrology of Usuard as its foundation [^1765]. Although the calendar contains commemorations for every day, approximately 70 days of the year lack proper offices, for which reason the *Missale* and *Breviarium* display no commemorations in the calendar for those days. What it shares in common with our calendar: all the Twelve Great Feasts and those akin unto them: the Nativity of John the Forerunner, the Beheading, and the Apostles Peter and Paul on the identical dates (the Conception on December 8). Of the commemorations of saints shared in common with ours on the identical dates: Paul of Thebes, Antony the Great, Agnes, Agatha, Gregory the Great, Cyril of Jerusalem, the Great-Martyr George, Mark the Evangelist, Athanasius the Great, the Apostle Barnabas, the Maccabees, Laurence, Eustathius (Placidus), Luke the Evangelist, Gregory the Wonderworker of Neocaesarea, the Apostle Andrew, Sabbas, Nicholas, and Ambrose; on other dates: Timothy on January 24, Polycarp on January 26, Ignatius the God-Bearer on February 1, Cyril of Alexandria on February 9, the Twenty-Five [Forty] Martyrs on March 10, Joseph the Betrothed on March 19, John of Damascus on March 27, Leo the Great on April 11, Justin the Martyr on April 14, the Apostles Philip and James the Brother of the Lord on May 1, Gregory the Theologian on May 9, Basil the Great on June 14, the Apostle James the Son of Zebedee on July 25, Anna on July 26, Nazarius and Celsus on July 29, the Chains of St. Peter on April 1 [August 1], the Finding of the Relics of St. Stephen on August 3, Helena on August 18, *(Orig. p. 405)* the Apostle Bartholomew on August 24, Cyprian and Justina on September 26, Matthew the Evangelist on September 21, Cosmas and Damian on September 27, Chrysanthus and Daria on October 25, Pope Martin on November 12, Catherine on November 25, the Apostle Thomas on December 21, Stephen on December 26, John the Evangelist on December 27, the Holy Innocents on December 28 [printed: 2], and Pope Sylvester on December 31. Specifically Roman Catholic commemorations, aside from Western saints, include: (a) in honour of the Saviour: (1) the Feast of Corpus Christi (*Corpus Domini*) on Thursday (as the day of the institution of the Eucharist) after the octave (on the 8th day) of Pentecost, instituted in the 13th century to counter doubts concerning transubstantiation; (2) the Feast of the Holy Name of Jesus on the second Sunday after Epiphany, detached from the Feast of the Circumcision and authorized by the Pope only in the 18th century; (3) a series of commemorations of individual events and instruments of the Passion of Christ on certain days of Lent and its preparatory period, such as: the Prayer in Gethsemane, the Crown of Thorns, the Spear and Nails, the Holy Winding-Sheet (*Plashchanytsia* / *Sindone*), the Five Wounds, the Precious Blood of Jesus, or the Sacred Heart of Jesus (the latter on the first Sunday of July); (b) in

=== LEAF p385 ===
honour of the Mother of God: (1) 'Her Holy Name' on the 1st Sunday after the Nativity of the Theotokos (from the 16th century); (2) 'Her Espousals with Joseph' on January 23 (from the 16th century); (3) 'Her Visitation (*Visitatio*) unto Righteous Elizabeth' on July 2 (from the 13th century, cf. our feast on July 2); (4) [printed: omitted numeral 4] 'The Rosary', that is, beads, in honour of the prayer rule among Roman Catholics (which attained wide diffusion in the 12th–13th centuries), consisting in the frequent repetition of the *Ave Maria* (**'Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos'** / *Bogoroditse Devo, raduisia*) joined with the *Pater Noster* (*Our Father*) counted upon beads; authorized after the victory over the Turks on October 7, 1571 (cf. our feast on October 1); (5) 'The Seven Sorrows of the Blessed Virgin Mary' (these sorrows: the Circumcision of Christ, the Flight into Egypt, the Loss of the Child Jesus in the Temple, the Carrying of the Cross, the Crucifixion, the Deposition from the Cross, and the Burial) on Friday of the 6th week of Great Lent (universally from the 15th century, but the hymn for it, *Stabat Mater*, originates in the 13th century); (6) 'The Seven Joys of the Virgin Mary' (these joys: the Annunciation, the Visitation, the Nativity of Jesus, the Adoration of the Magi, the Resurrection, the Descent of the Holy Spirit, and Her Coronation by God the Father and the Son) on the 3rd Sunday of September, established in the 18th century as a parallel unto the preceding feast and assigned unto the day on which the military Order of St. Mary (Mercedarians), founded for the redemption of captives, celebrated the liberation of captives from the Saracens in 1198; (7) 'Our Lady of the Snows' on August 5, that is, the Dedication of the Basilica on the Esquiline Hill in Rome, erected in the 4th century by direction of a miraculous vision upon the site where snow had wonderfully fallen; (8) 'Our Lady of Mount Carmel' on July 16—the feast of the Carmelites, an Order founded upon that mount, from the 13th century; (9) 'The Translation of the Holy House of Mary unto Loreto' on December 10—by angels from Nazareth, where it had been exposed unto peril from the infidels; there are also 13 less significant feasts of the Mother of God that are not entered into liturgical calendars. Of the feasts of saints not identical with the Eastern Church, the most significant are: 'All Saints' on November 1 (instituted prior unto the 8th century); All Souls' Day on November 2 (subsequent unto the 8th century); the Apparition of the Archangel Michael upon Mount Gargano (in Apulia) on May 8; the Dedication of the Church in Castel Sant'Angelo in honour of a similar apparition of the Archangel Michael there on September 20 (from the 5th century); the 'Holy Guardian Angels' on the 1st Sunday of September (from the 17th century); the 'Chair' (that is, in honour of the episcopate of the Apostle Peter in Antioch) on February 22; *(Orig. p. 406)* 'His Chair in Rome' on January 18 (from the 5th century); the 'Conversion of St. Paul' on January 25 (from the 13th century); and the Dedication of famous basilicas, such as that of Saints Peter and Paul in Rome on November 18 [^1766].

### The Classification of Feasts

Only in the present period was the current division of feasts according unto their degree of solemnity definitively established. Already in the preceding period, feasts in honour of the Saviour and the Mother of God occupied the most honourable position among others, and even the current number of these feasts—twelve—was approximately adumbrated. Yet it may be stated that prior unto the 11th century, the number of the foremost feasts was not measured with such precision and symbolic intent as now, that is, it was not subsumed under the number twelve. The Grottaferrata Typikon (11th–12th centuries) enumerates 14 'Dominical

=== LEAF p386 ===
feasts (*despotikaì heortaí / δεσποτικαὶ ἑορταί*)': the Annunciation, the Nativity of Christ, the Circumcision, the Meeting (*Hypapante*), the Theophany (*Baptism*), the Raising of Lazarus, Palm Sunday, the Transfiguration, the Mystical Supper, the Crucifixion, the Resurrection, New Sunday (*Thomas Sunday*), the Ascension, and Pentecost [^1767]. Theodore Prodromus in the 12th century omits from this catalog the Mystical Supper and New Sunday, thereby arriving at 12 feasts; Nicephorus Callistus in the 14th century replaces the Circumcision with the Dormition, and thus likewise obtains the number twelve [^1768]. Consequently, Marian feasts—among which the Annunciation (= the Conception of Christ) was not included—were not entered into the rank of the Twelve Great Feasts. In the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, the Entry is merely named under November 21, but regarding its service no rubrical directions are provided, nor are Epistle and Gospel readings even indicated, which is done there for numerous saints. Yet all other Typika, beginning with the most ancient Studite witnesses, assign for all feasts of the Mother of God services of equal solemnity with Dominical feasts, with those exceptions that are likewise made in this regard by the present Typikon (the absence of antiphons at the Liturgy—which ancient Typika permitted even for certain Dominical feasts—and a shorter afterfeast) [^1769]. With almost the same solemnity as the Twelve Great Feasts, the ancient Studite Typika prescribed the celebration of only two feasts of the Forerunner—June 24 and August 29—both because of the elevated dignity of this saint and, doubtless, because of his close association with the Studion Monastery: these feasts had an afterfeast, the Oktoechos was cancelled for them (though not unconditionally: on Wednesday and Friday the 1st apostichon sticheron was from the Oktoechos); they had stepennas (*gradual psalms*, which in the Studite-Alexis Typikon served in place of the present Polyeleos), and the like; yet all other feasts (excepting, naturally, the commemoration of Theodore the Studite, which constituted a great feast), even June 29, were celebrated according unto *(Orig. p. 407)* these Typika with significantly less solemnity: the Oktoechos was sung alongside the festal office (at the aposticha, the 1st sticheron was from the Oktoechos), and there were no stepennas. The Jerusalem (and Evergetis) Typikon advanced further in this regard: it imparted the same festal structure of service, with the absolute exclusion of the Oktoechos, unto an entire series of feasts in honour of saints: not only June 29, but also September 26 and December 6; moreover, with each subsequent redaction of this Typikon, the compass of such feasts—which acquired the designation of *Polyeleos* feasts—expanded: gradually there were included here the commemorations of all the Apostles in the order of their veneration (the earliest being November 30; yet all commemorations of the Apostles were made Polyeleos feasts only in the printed editions of the Typikon, though upon the authority of certain ancient recensions of the Jerusalem Typikon, for instance in Nikon of the Black Mountain), as well as certain martyrs and venerable monastics. Of the remaining saints, the more venerated were distinguished in both types of Typika—Studite and Jerusalem—by joining unto the aposticha stichera a sticheron unto the saint, and by an increased number of troparia at the canon (in the Studite the troparia of the canon were sung twice, in the Jerusalem the canon

=== LEAF p387 ===
was at 6), occasionally by special Praises (*Ainoi*) stichera at Matins and Epistle and Gospel readings at the Liturgy; while in the Studite Typika they were marked beyond this by exemption from the Hours and from manual labor until midday (sometimes **'Blessed is the man'** at Vespers, for example on September 26 and November 8 according unto the Studite-Alexis Typikon). A service of this type is prescribed by the Studite Typika for all the Apostles (excepting John the Evangelist) and for the majority of present-day Polyeleos and certain six-troparion (*shesterik*) saints; for these same saints the Jerusalem Typikon prescribes the like, with the aforementioned exceptions (Polyeleos saints). The Jerusalem Typikon, by virtue of its practice of the Great Doxology, was able to detach several commemorations from this compass of middle feasts into the rank of 'Doxology' feasts, that is, with the Great Doxology but without the Polyeleos: in the most ancient copies of the Jerusalem Typikon, September 23 and November 30 appear as such feasts. With respect unto the remaining saints, in both types of Typika (and in the Evergetis) some of them (those more venerated) possessed a troparion; for such was sung at Matins **'God is the Lord'** (*Theos Kyrios*); while others possessed none, and then there was sung at Matins *Alleluia* with the Triadica; the number of this latter class of saints was reduced in each subsequent recension of the Jerusalem Typikon (remaining almost constant in the Studite), until (already in the printed editions of the Typikon) *Alleluia* remained exclusively for the multi-day fasts. Thus, in both types of Typika there existed from of old three principal ranks of feasts: great, middle, and minor; their composition and ceremonial varied, yet the fundamental distinction among them has remained unto this day such as Nikon of the Black Mountain indicated:

> Great feasts 'have assemblies of the people according unto the rule (a service obligatory for all), and prophecies at Vespers, and readings (festal homilies) at Matins, and **"Let every breath"** and the Gospel... at minor feasts in church prostrations do not take place during the fast (public fasting?), but privately (in the cells) both take place (prostrations and fasting); at middle feasts in church prostrations in no wise take place, but privately as each one chooses; while at great feasts prostrations do not take place, neither fasting, neither in *(Orig. p. 408)* church nor in the cells' [^1770].

Nikon provides in a table these three ranks of feasts from various Typika (the Studite, the Jerusalem, and the Holy Mountain / Athonite), designating great feasts with a cross in a circle, middle feasts with a cross, and minor feasts with three dots. Yet since full consensus did not exist among the Typika in the distribution of feasts across these ranks, Nikon provides another table of them with certain variations from the first; moreover, different manuscripts of Nikon's work do not always agree in assigning a given feast unto a specific rank. In any event, this represents the earliest known attempt at a complete classification of feasts, and a comparison thereof with the present-day

=== LEAF p388 ===
Typikon demonstrates what development the church year has undergone in this regard since the 10th–11th centuries. Great feasts in Nikon, besides the Twelve Great Feasts and the movable feasts, are: January 1, or the entire Twelve Days (*Dodekahemeron*), June 24 and 29, August 29; in certain manuscripts of Nikon also November 13, April 23, and May 8. Middle feasts: September 26; October 6, 9, 18, 23, 26; November 8, 13, 14, 16, and 30; December 20; January 7, 20, and 25; April 25 and 30 [printed: 39]; May 8 and 10; June 11 and 19; July 8, 20, and 27; August 9; in certain manuscripts also September 1, 6, 9, 13, 16, 20, 23; November 6, 12, 25; December 4, 5, 9, 21; January 16, 17, 18, and 27; February 3 and 24; March 9; May 21; June 8 and 30; July 2, 13, and 25; August 1 and 31; according unto another table also December 26 (in certain manuscripts even a great feast), December 27, and May 25. ### The Roman Catholic Table of Feasts

*In the Roman Catholic Church, feasts are divided according unto their degree of solemnity into six ranks.* Feasts of the first four ranks, since each encompasses approximately two days (some have an eve or vigil, others continue through certain offices of the following day), are termed doubles (*duplicia*). Unto *duplicia primae classis* (first class doubles) belong feasts that possess an eight-day afterfeast (*octava*), which is especially solemn not on its final day—the Apodosis, as with us—but upon the Sunday falling within that eight-day period. Among the feasts with an eight-day octave are the Nativity of Christ, Epiphany, Easter (*Pascha*), Ascension, Pentecost, Corpus Christi, the Assumption (*Dormition*), the Immaculate Conception, the Nativity of John the Baptist, the Apostles Peter and Paul, All Saints on November 1, and the Dedication of a Church; unto this same first class are likewise assigned the following feasts that lack an octave: Joseph the Betrothed on March 19, the patronal *(Orig. p. 409)* saint and protector (*patronus*) of the country or locality (the locally venerated saint); secondary feast (*secundaria*, in distinction from those enumerated, which are *primaria*): the Sacred Heart of Jesus. Unto the second rank (*duplicia II classis*) belong: Circumcision, the Most Holy Trinity (on the 1st Sunday after Pentecost), Purification (*Meeting / Hypapante*), Visitation (July 2), Dedication of the Basilica of St. Michael the Archangel (September 29), commemorations of the Apostles, the Evangelists, Stephen, the Holy Innocents, and Laurence (the last three possessing an octave), Anna, Joachim (March 20); secondary: the Holy Name and Most Precious Blood of Jesus, the Finding of the Holy Cross, the Rosary, and the Patronage of St. Joseph. Unto the third class, *duplicia majora* (greater doubles), belong: the Transfiguration, the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, the Presentation (*Entry*), the Beheading, and others, besides

=== LEAF p389 ===
those enumerated above, the feasts of the Mother of God, the Forerunner, the Apostles, and the foremost saints: Benedict, Dominic, Francis. Unto the fourth rank (*alia duplicia*, other doubles) belong feasts of other foremost saints. Unto the fifth and sixth ranks, *semiduplicia* (semidoubles) and *simplicia* (simple), belong the remaining days. Sundays (*Dominicae*) are divided into three classes: I: 1st of Advent, 1st of Lent, Passion Sunday (5th of Lent), Palm Sunday, Easter, Low Sunday (*in Albis*), Pentecost, Trinity Sunday; II: 2nd, 3rd, and 4th of Advent, 3rd and 4th of Lent; III: the remainder. This classification is essential for determining which office is to be celebrated upon the confluence of movable feasts with immovable ones: thus upon the concurrence of feasts of the first three classes with a Sunday of the 1st class, the feast is transferred (*translatio*); upon the concurrence of other feasts with Sundays, for the most part only a commemoration of the former is made at the prayer (*commemoratio*) or a short reading (*capitulum*) is assigned for them [^1771].

### The Nativity Fast

In the present period, the *Nativity Fast* received universal recognition in the East, having arisen and spread, as we have seen, earlier in the West in the 6th century (see above, p. 327). In the treatise *On the Three Quadragesimas*, attributed unto Anastasius of Sinai, Patriarch of Antioch (561–600 AD), but having made its appearance scarcely prior unto the 9th century (as is evident from the fact that mention is made therein of the Dormition Fast, which was unknown prior unto the 9th century), it is stated that the obligatory nature of the Nativity Fast was still contested, that it was regarded as a monastic rather than an apostolic ordinance, that its duration was shortened unto 19, 12, 6, or 4 days, and that during its course it was deemed permissible to partake of everything except meat, save for a single day; the author defends the apostolic origin and universal obligation of the fast by appealing unto the tradition concerning the Apostle Philip, according unto which tradition it was revealed unto the Apostle, because before his death he had prayed for heavenly punishment upon his persecutors, that for 40 days he would not be admitted into Paradise, for which reason Philip entreated the Apostles to fast on his behalf during those days [^1772]. *(Orig. p. 410)* In the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* (9th century) detailed instructions are already given concerning food during the Quadragesima of St. Philip. The Council of Constantinople under Patriarch Luke Chrysoberges and Emperor Manuel Comnenus (1143–1180 AD) requires this fast to begin on November 14; Balsamon (12th century), in his commentary upon the canons of this Council, regards the fast in its full scope as obligatory for monks, but for laity considers a reduction thereof unto 7 days permissible [^1773]. According unto the testimony of Codinus (14th century), at the Constantinopolitan court the Nativity Fast was observed for 40 days, and on the day of the Nativity of Christ, upon the occasion of the dispensation from the fast, the protopapas (the first archpriest) came unto the palace to bless the imperial table [^1774].

### The Dormition Fast

In the 9th century we encounter the earliest mentions also of the *Dormition Fast*. The *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* do not yet mention it, even in manuscripts of the 14th century. Only in a 16th-century manuscript of the second of these Typika, in the margin opposite the chapter on fasts, is there an annotation: 'noteworthy (*semeiosai / σημείωσαι*), that nothing is said concerning the Fast of the Dormition' [^1775]. There is likewise no mention of it in the Evergetis Typikon (11th century) or that of the Pantokrator Monastery of Constantinople of 1136. Among Typika, the first indication of the Dormition Fast is encountered in the Typikon of the San Nicola di Casole Monastery (12th century). Here under August 1 there is the following observation:

> 'Of Nicholas, Most Holy Patriarch of Constantinople, New Rome (Nicholas I Mystikos, 895–925 with interruption, or Nicholas II Chrysoberges, 983–996 AD), concerning the Quadragesima of the Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos: We have another fast, called that of the Most Holy Theotokos, commencing from the 1st day of the month of August, which is mentioned by the Holy Council of Union (*henoseos / ἑνώσεως*—in another manuscript: "and by the Seventh Council at Nicaea"). Yet on the first day of August we do not fast because of the feast of the Holy Maccabees, but from the 2nd day of August [we begin] the established fast, and the office of the Transfiguration, that is, for 8 days, we sing entirely in full' (that is, despite the fast, the service of the Transfiguration is sung in church with its forefeast for 8 days).

Yet the same Typikon in its 'canon' on food does not name the Dormition Fast [^1776]. This Typikon furnishes us with the guiding thread for tracing the origin of the Dormition Fast. According unto it, the earliest mention of the fast must be sought in the acts of the so-called 'Council of Union' (*Synodus Unionis*) held in 920 AD. This Council, regarding the question of the admissibility of a fourth marriage, decreed that even trigamists are subject unto special ecclesiastical penance: they must 'receive communion three times a year: at Pascha, on the feast of the Dormition of the Mother of God, and at the Nativity of our Lord Jesus Christ, forasmuch as fasts precede these feasts' [^1777].
