=== LEAF p811 ===
In the well-known description of Sinai Matins of the 7th century there are indicated at it the "Odes of Moses" (that is, the Biblical Odes, just as all psalms are called "of David") and, undoubtedly, all 9 current ones (ibid., p. 295).

In the Greek Psalter of the Imperial Public Library from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius of 862, after the kathismata, alongside the most important songs and prayers, the Biblical Odes are placed; 1–7 are designated *ôdai* [Gr. ᾠδαί], the 8th *hymnos* [Gr. ὕμνος], 9, 10, and 11 (the Theotokos, Zacharias, and Simeon the God-receiver)—*euchai* [Gr. εὐχαί] [^2515].

Among non-Chalcedonian traditions, Coptic Matins has no Biblical Odes; Nestorian has only the 2 Odes of Moses; the Abyssinian (Ethiopian) has the Odes of Moses, the 3 Holy Children, and Simeon the God-receiver [^2515].

Armenian Matins (properly speaking, the Midnight Office) has the Odes of the 3 Holy Children, the Theotokos, and Zacharias; among the Armenians there is also one ode each at the Third Hour, Vespers, and the "Service before Sleep" (after Compline), the latter having portions of the Odes of Habakkuk, the 3 Holy Children, and Simeon the God-receiver. Among the Maronites: Matins has 7 odes, Vespers 4, the Hours 3 or 2, Compline 2. Among Western rites, the Roman rite already under St. Benedict had at the concluding, most important part of Matins (Lauds) in turn (beginning from Sunday): the Odes of the 3 Holy Children, Isaiah (from ch. 12), Hezekiah, Hannah, Moses 1, Habakkuk, Moses 2 (the Theotokos—at Vespers); the Ambrosian rite—Zacharias or Moses 2, Moses 1, the Three Holy Children (only on Sundays and feasts); the Mozarabic rite—one ode in turn, but differently from the Roman (Introductory Chapter, pp. 299–303, 315–318).

### Manner of Execution (*Sposob ispolneniya*)

The Biblical Odes in the kanon are used in three scopes: in their entirety in Great Lent (though not all daily), in an abbreviated form on weekdays, and in an even more abbreviated form on Sundays, feasts, forefeasts, afterfeasts, and on Saturdays. In the first and second cases, the Biblical Ode is first chanted by verses alternately by the two choirs without refrains until there remains a sufficient number of verses unto which the heirmos and troparia of the kanons are to be appended as refrains (usually 12 such verses are needed, or 14 with "Glory" and "Both now"; sometimes in Lent only 2); in the last case, the entire ode is chanted with the troparia of the kanon appended as refrains. To judge the degree of abbreviation of the odes for weekday and festal use, one must compare the number *(Orig. p. 670)* of verses of each of them in its complete form, in which the ode is used in Great Lent, with the number of verses in its abbreviated forms. At the same time, it must be noted that the division of the odes into verses for liturgical use for the most part does not correspond unto their division into verses in the Bible: long

...biblical verses are broken into two or more parts.

Ode 1 in the Bible: 19 vv.; in Lent: 19; on weekdays: 12; festal: 10.  
Ode 2 in the Bible: 43 vv.; in Lent: 65.  
Ode 3 in the Bible: 11 vv.; in Lent: 16; on weekdays: 12; festal: 10.  
Ode 4 in the Bible: 19 vv.; in Lent: 30; on weekdays: 12; festal: 10.  
Ode 5 in the Bible: 11 vv.; in Lent: 15; on weekdays: 12; festal: 10.  
Ode 6 in the Bible: 8 vv.; in Lent: 11; on weekdays: 10; festal: 10.  
Ode 7 in the Bible: 31 vv.; in Lent: 34; on weekdays: 12; festal: 10.

Ode 9 of the Theotokos in the Bible: 10 vv.; in divine worship: 8.

The abbreviation is carried out in such a way that from the ode those verses are omitted that are less poetic and whose absence does not disrupt the flow of discourse nor disturb the principal thought; for example, from Ode 1 there are omitted—for weekday use: "His chosen horsemen, captain-commanders, He drowned in the Red Sea"—a thought already contained in the preceding verse: "His host hath He cast into the sea"; or the verse: "Thy right hand, O Lord, is glorified in strength..."—a general thought; for festal use there are omitted the verses: "The waters grew thick as a wall... the enemy said: I will pursue, I will overtake..."—on account of the detailed description of the event. From Ode 5 only 1 1/2 verses are omitted; from Ode 6—1 verse. The first verse is never omitted, nor likewise the last, by reason of which the ode preserves also its external structure.

The weekday execution of the Biblical Odes bears the title **"Let us sing unto the Lord"** (*Gospodevi poim*), and the festal **"We sing unto the Lord"** (*Poim Gospodevi*) (see Chapter 18 of the Typikon). The odes in the indicated three scopes are placed in the Heirmologion; in the Followed Psalter, however, only in the Lenten scope. Unto the Heirmologion the chapter of the Typikon under examination likewise refers: "and after the Amen (unto the exclamation: Through the mercy and compassions) we begin the heirmos of the Sunday kanon... ### Manner of Execution in Antiquity (*Sposob ispolneniya v drevnosti*)

In the ancient Western Church also, the Biblical Odes were chanted with a more solemn melody than the psalms [^2516]. One may think that originally the Biblical Odes were chanted with a short refrain unto each verse, and such a manner of chanting them was not abandoned even when kanons appeared: prior unto the heirmos or troparia the entire ode was chanted with this refrain, and the troparia were chanted either without refrains, or with special refrains not taken from the ode, or, finally, with the repetition of the concluding verses of the ode as refrains. A trace of such

Traces of ancient responsorial refrains unto the Biblical Odes are preserved in certain Greek and Slavic manuscripts (of the Moscow Synodal Library), which also indicate "antiphons" unto the psalms, identical with those in the "Kanon of Antiphons of the Psalms of the Great Church of God" of the 6th-century Monophysite pseudo-patriarch Anthimus of Constantinople. ### Refrains (*Pripevy*)

Almost universal practice, at least in Russia, replaces the verses of the Biblical Odes, with the exception of Great Lent, with special refrains unto the kanon; thus, unto the Sunday kanon there is used the refrain: **"Glory, O Lord, to Thy holy resurrection"** (*Slava, Gospodi, svyatomu voskreseniyu Tvoemu*); unto the Cross-Resurrection kanon: **"Glory, O Lord, to Thy Precious Cross and resurrection"** (*Slava, Gospodi, Krestu Tvoemu chestnomu i voskreseniyu*); unto the Theotokos kanon and the Theotokia in the first two kanons: **"Most Holy Theotokos, save us"** (*Presvyataya Bogoroditse, spasi nas*); unto the kanons of the saints from the Menaion: "Holy [rank and name], pray to God for us" (*Svyaty... moli Boga o nas*).

Notwithstanding the wide dissemination of such a practice, it is difficult to find any justification for it. Is any refrain possible better than such verses, for example, of the Biblical Odes: **"The dead shall rise and they that are in the tombs shall awake, and they that are on the earth shall be glad"** (Isa. 26:19), or: **"And let my life ascend out of corruption unto Thee, O Lord my God"** (Jonah 2:7), or, finally, all the verses of the 8th Ode with the concluding refrain in each: **"Praise the Lord and exalt Him unto all ages"** (Dan. 3:57ff.)?

Moreover, non-biblical refrains divide the kanon into very unequal parts, owing unto the fact that in a single kanon there occur two or three identical refrains (and the third is a Theotokion). Finally, it is not always possible to compose a rhythmic refrain; in general, since the liturgical books scarcely appoint refrains (they appoint them occasionally only for the kanons of Compline), there is opened here a wide field for the arbitrariness of each reader and for uncalled-for

=== LEAF p815 ===
...creativity. But in view of the fact that the practice of refrains is exceedingly ancient and widespread, and there is little hope for an early triumph in this domain of the requirements of the Typikon, we shall indicate here the refrains sanctified by the liturgical *(Orig. p. 673)* books themselves and provide several directives concerning their composition. (Where the verses of the Biblical Odes are appended unto the kanons, these directives will be suitable for Molebens).

Besides the cited refrains of the Sunday and Theotokos kanons (the second is indicated by the Octoechos for Compline, see Chapter 1, Sunday), the liturgical books provide also the following refrains: **"Christ is risen from the dead"** (*Khristos voskrese iz mertvykh*); **"Glory to Thee, our God, glory to Thee"** (*Slava Tebe, Bozhe nash, slava Tebe*) (Holy Week, see Triodion and Typikon, Compline of Palm Sunday); **"O Most Holy Trinity, our God, glory to Thee"** (*Presvyataya Troitse, Bozhe nash, slava Tebe*) (Sunday Midnight Office, see Octoechos Tone 1, Sunday); **"Have mercy on me, O God, have mercy on me"** (*Pomiluy mya, Bozhe, pomiluy mya*) (see Triodion and Typikon, Compline of Monday of the 1st week of Lent); **"Venerable fathers, pray to God for us"** (*Prepodobnii ottsy, molite Boga o nas*) (see Triodion and Typikon, Cheesefare Saturday); **"Venerable father Andrew, pray to God for us"** (*Prepodobne otche Andree, moli Boga o nas*); **"Venerable mother Mary, pray to God for us"** (*Prepodobnaya mati Marie, moli Boga o nas*) (Triodion, Compline of Wednesday of the 1st week of Lent); **"Holy Hieromartyr Euthymius, pray to God for us"** (*Svyashchennomucheniche Evfimie, moli Boga o nas*) (December 26).

These models demonstrate that a refrain ought to consist of 2 strophes of approximately equal length, as for example:

*Venerable father Andrew,*  
*Pray to God for us.*

By such a rule one ought to be guided in the composition of refrains. Consequently, in the case of two or more saints it is no longer necessary to name them. In like manner one ought not to encumber the first strophe with the addition unto the saint's name of his epithets, not only such as are indifferent unto his glory, as "Tyro" (= recruit), "warrior", "gardener", but even honorary ones: "the Great", "the Theologian", "Chrysostom", "Wonderworker". Nor are multiple designations of the saint's sanctity required—that is, alongside "venerable" to place also "holy", for which reason one does not say "holy hieromartyr N", but simply "hieromartyr".

On this foundation the following refrains may be permissible for the diverse choirs of saints: "Holy Archangels and Angels, pray to God for us"; "Holy Apostle N, pray to God for us"; "Holy Apostles, pray..."; "Holy Prophet N, pray..."; "Holy Prophets, pray..."; "Holy Martyr N, pray..."; "Holy Virgin-Martyr N, pray..."; "Holy Martyrs, pray..."; "Holy Hieromartyr N, pray..."; "Holy Venerable-Martyr N, pray..."; "Holy Hierarch father N, pray..."; "Holy Fathers, pray..." (unto several hierarchs); "Holy Venerable father N, pray..."; "Holy Venerable mother N

### Antiquity of the Practice (*Davnost' praktiki*)

When the custom of replacing the verses of the Biblical Odes in the kanon with other refrains arose may be judged from the fact that in 16th-century monuments these refrains are already indicated. Thus, for example, in certain 16th-century Typika for the Nativity kanon there is indicated the refrain **"Glory, O Lord, to Thy Nativity"**, for the Theophany—**"...to Thy Theophany"** [^2526]. In a certain 17th-century Typikon there are sections: "On the refrains in the Octoechos on weekdays, how to speak them in the kanon" and "Rubric on the refrains in the kanons unto the saints" [^2527].

It is not surprising that among the Old Believers also they are accepted. In the *Short Practical Typikon* of the Old Believer Bishop Arseny, refrains are indicated with the following variations from those recommended above: "Glory, O Lord, to Thy resurrection"; "Glory, O Lord, to Thy Cross and resurrection"; "Most Holy Lady Theotokos, save us"; "Glory, O Lord, to Thy Precious Cross" (Exaltation and 3rd Sunday of Lent); "Glory, O Lord, to Thy Ascension, Transfiguration, Nativity, Theophany, Meeting" (in afterfeasts this or "Glory to Thee, our God, glory to Thee"); unto the Guardian Angel: "Holy Angel of Christ (or + my Guardian), save me, thy sinful servant"; "Holy and great John, Forerunner of the Lord, pray..." # THE HEIRMOS AND TROPARIA (*Irmos i tropari*)

More than the Biblical Odes, the significant portion of the kanon in terms of volume is constituted by the hymns of later composition—the heirmos and troparia.

The heirmos (*heirmos*, Gr. εἱρμός, link, chain), as its very name indicates, its position at the head of the troparia, and its customary content, serves as a link between the Biblical Odes, which have no relation unto the commemorated event, and the troparia, dedicated exclusively unto the latter. The heirmos accomplishes this by discovering an analogy between the event sung in the Biblical Ode and that being celebrated, paraphrasing the ode in accordance with this—that is, it sings the particular commemoration in adaptation unto the content of the Biblical Ode, as far as possible in its very words.

At the same time it serves as a model both in melody and in rhythmic structure for the troparia: in the troparion there must be as many *(Orig. p. 675)* strophes and of the same length and as many musical lines as in the heirmos.

As regards the quantity of heirmos and troparia, by the very idea of the heirmos there ought to be one for each ode of a single kanon; the quantity of troparia, however, is determined by the number of verses in the Biblical Ode that may serve as refrains unto the troparion. Since these odes are of diverse lengths, as the norm for the quantity of troparia there were taken the shortest odes—those of Jonah and Zacharias, each yielding 13 verses; on account of the oddness of this number, inconvenient in antiphonal chanting, as the normative number for the troparia, including the heirmos in their number, there was taken the number 14, since the number of verses in each ode is augmented by the two parts of the minor doxology obligatory at the end of each ode, as also at the end of each psalm (see above, p. 661): "Glory" and "Both now".

This normal quantity of troparia with the heirmos is maintained on the majority of days of the year—on all ordinary weekdays, on Sundays, and on the majority of feasts. Only on the greatest feasts is this quantity increased unto 16, and under special circumstances reduced unto 12, 10, and even 6 and 4 (the latter two cases not at Matins).

But very rarely does this entire series of troparia constitute a single kanon: as a consequence of the confluence on almost every day of the year of several commemorations, this number is divided among several kanons, in which case there falls unto the share of each 8, 6, 4, 3, or 2 troparia (sometimes with the heirmos). Thus, the entire kanon at Matins may be for 16, 14, 12, and 10 (only on Great Thursday), and the kanons entering into its composition individually for 8, 6, 4, 3, and 2. A peculiarity in

=== LEAF p818 ===
...the structure of the kanon is also that Ode 2 is usually omitted in it, except on Tuesday of Great Lent and certain Saturdays connected with Great Lent; this is done on account of the profound sorrowfulness and wrathfulness of this ode, and also, perhaps, because of mankind's perpetual unfavorable view of the number 2.

### History (*Istoriya*)

The earliest writer of kanons, and consequently the founder of this genre of church hymnody, might be recognized as St. Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem (7th c.), and the original form of the kanon might be considered the triodion (Introductory Chapter, p. 331), had not the view been expressed at present, with considerable foundation, that the triodia attributed unto St. Sophronius belong unto St. Joseph the Hymnographer (9th c.) [^2530].

However, refrains in the form of troparia unto the Biblical Odes are already indicated by the well-known description of the Sinai Matins of the 6th–7th centuries (Introductory Chapter, p. 295); it is remarkable that in this description the refrains unto Ode 8 stand out from the rest, although they bear the identical name of "troparia"; it is possible that the refrains unto the remaining odes had the form of the above-indicated (p. 671) short and uniform verses for each ode (for Ode 1 **"Let us sing unto the Lord, for He is gloriously glorified"**, for Ode 2 **"Glory to Thee, O God"**, for Ode 3 **"Holy art Thou, O Lord, and my spirit praiseth Thee"**, etc.), and only for Ode 8 were in honor of the commemorated event (cf. the significance that *(Orig. p. 676)* the Ode of the 3 Holy Children has at sung Asmatic Matins—Introductory Chapter, p. 346); if so, then the original form of the kanon was the single-ode kanon (*monoodion*).

The *monoodion* gradually grew into a two-ode kanon (*diodion*) through the appending of Ode 9 unto Ode 8: a certain 9th-century Georgian Menaion on the majority of days of the year has *diodia* of Odes 8 and 9, providing full kanons only on the greatest feasts and *triodia* for the Triodion period [^2531].

In addition unto Odes 8 and 9, they gradually began to append troparia unto the daily ode of the day: on Monday unto Ode 1, etc.; thus arose the *triodion*, which, like the *diodion*, was for a time, it must be, the exclusive form of the kanon; a trace of such practice is the present *triodia* at Compline in the forefeasts of the Nativity of Christ, Theophany, and especially the Transfiguration. A peculiarity of this period in the development of the kanon was the small number of troparia in each ode of the kanon (the aforementioned Georgian manuscript has 2–3 of them) and the absence of heirmos.

The first to begin writing large and full kanons was, it must be, St. Andrew of Crete. As his Great Kanon demonstrates, he, apparently, strived to make the quantity of troparia for

=== LEAF p819 ===
...each Biblical Ode correspond unto its length. He also, it must be, introduced the heirmos; it is remarkable that his heirmos are very close unto the Biblical Odes, often being a paraphrase of them. Ode 2 at that time was not excluded from the kanon (as is shown by the Great Kanon, where it is preserved for all four days, as also by his triodia for Great Saturday), nor likewise under John of Damascus [^2532].

The later exclusion of Ode 2 from the kanon was caused, perhaps, by the circumstance that when the heirmos diverged from the Biblical Odes, the poets found it difficult to give the heirmos of Ode 2 a celebratory or festive character, or did not consider it proper to introduce into a joyful feast the sorrowful and menacing motifs of this ode. Moreover, the acrostic—a frequent literary device in Greek kanons (e.g. from the acrostic *Klēmentos* [Gr. Κλήμεντος] there resulted the meaningless *Kēmentos* [Gr. Κήμεντος]) [^2533]—demonstrates that already St. Cosmas wrote no troparia for Ode 2; perhaps unto the latter belongs the initiative of its exclusion from the kanon.

In view of the fact that the odes from antiquity were used, like the psalms, with the addition at the end of "Glory" and "Both now", for a long time in the kanon a Trinity strophe (*Triadikon*) and a Theotokion (*Theotokion*) were considered obligatory; so in all the kanons of Andrew of Crete; in the time of John of Damascus only the Theotokion is already considered obligatory, and his kanons even of the great Despotic feasts originally possessed them; they were omitted by later editors. Cosmas here also introduced an innovation, beginning frequently to write kanons without Theotokia.

A great difference is observed between Andrew of Crete, on the one hand, and John of Damascus and Cosmas, on the other, in the volume of their kanons: in the latter they are many times shorter, and for great commemorations are limited unto two or three troparia. This was, it must be, in order to afford space for the compositions of other creators or for the purpose of antiphonal repetition.

In the 9th century, when the Triodion through the activity of St. Theodore the Studite and his brother Joseph, and the Octoechos and Menaion through St. Theophanes and Joseph the Hymnographer, were brought unto their present form—through the composition of kanons for the vast majority of ordinary days—there was fully developed also the present customary type of kanon with 4–6 troparia, which volume became established *(Orig. p. 677)* in view of the perpetual necessity of combining several kanons on a single day.

Significant importance was not yet attached unto the quantity of troparia in the kanon in the 9th century, as is shown by the indifference toward this in the *Hypotyposis*. But already the *Diatyposis*, and especially complete copies of the Typika—Studite, Evergetis, and Jerusalem, consequently from the 10th–11th centuries—perhaps in view of the extraordinary increase in this genre of liturgical literature and the necessity here of some regulation, began to measure the importance of a feast by the number of troparia in the kanon and the doubling of the heirmos and troparia, whereupon almost immediately in this regard there were established the present norms for the quantity of troparia in the kanon (see Introductory Chapter, p. 367).

# THE KANON OF SUNDAY MATINS (*Kanon voskresnoy utreni*)

The kanon of Sunday Matins, in regard unto its heirmos and troparia, consists of 4 parts and is usually composed of 4 kanons: the Sunday Resurrection kanon, the Cross-Resurrection kanon, the Theotokos kanon, and the daily Menaion kanon, of which only the first is with an heirmos.

In this manner, unto the celebrated event only half of the kanon is dedicated, since the Theotokos kanon glorifies the Mother of God without relation unto the Resurrection (in contrast, for example, unto certain Theotokia at "God is the Lord").

The reason for such duality in the composition of the Sunday Matins kanon is that on a simple Sunday, recurring so frequently, the soul of the worshiper cannot be so filled with the celebrated event as on an annual feast in honor of an event (such a view of the character of the celebration of the Lord's Day was expressed already in the 3rd century by Origen—see Introductory Chapter, p. 112). With these intentions even the second kanon in honor of the Resurrection is dedicated not unto this event exclusively, but together unto the Crucifixion of the Savior, whereby joy concerning the Resurrection is tempered.

In the following—Theotokos—kanon the diverting of the mind from the celebrated event, necessary for its refreshment, goes still further: the content of this kanon has with the celebrated event, the Resurrection, only that connection that there is glorified the foremost participant therein (in like manner as a separate kanon unto the Theotokos is appointed at Matins of Wednesday and Friday).

Finally, the 4th kanon leaves the Resurrection altogether and occupies itself with the other commemoration of that day.

Being sung directly only by the first of the 4 kanons, the event of the Resurrection does not occupy that kanon entirely either: of its four strophes (3 troparia and the heirmos) only the two middle ones speak of the Resurrection; likewise of the 3 strophes of the Cross-Resurrection kanon.

The most prominent part of the kanon is its heirmos, which opens the kanon. The heirmos of the Sunday kanon likewise has no relation unto the celebrated event. But it must be observed that the heirmos of other feasts rarely have such a relation; thus, for example, of the heirmos of the Nativity of Christ only half (of the first kanon) directly glorify the event; of the 8 heirmos of the Meeting only 2 do so; of the 16 heirmos of the Transfiguration—1; as regards the kanons unto saints, only the heirmos unto Gregory the Theologian on January 25 glorify him directly.
