Refrains unto **"Lord, I have cried"** are mentioned already in the 6th–7th century in the well-known narrative concerning the visit of Sophronius and John Moschus unto Nilus of Sinai (Introductory Chapter, p. 295), yet they are here called troparia. The word receives entirely its present application for the first time in the complete manuscripts of the Studite and Jerusalem Typika. — In their original form stichera were shorter than at present and in volume, presumably, equaled the verses of the psalms. According unto a Georgian manuscript reflecting the practice of the 6th–8th centuries, the sticheron unto St. Stephen was: **"Thou hast clothed thyself in holiness, O Stephen, archdeacon and protomartyr, companion of the Angels"** [^2171]. Such an appearance stichera still have in Chanted Vespers according unto Symeon of Thessalonica (Introductory Chapter, p. 342). At **"Lord, I have cried"** stichera not infrequently began with **"An evening sacrifice"** [^2172]: cf. the 1st Resurrection sticheron of Tone 1 and Tone 8 at **"Lord, I have cried"**.

## The Censing at "Lord, I Have Cried"

Both the content of the psalms of **"Lord, I have cried"** and the special solemnity of their execution demand that they be accompanied by censing. The censing at **"Lord, I have cried"** is performed by "the priest or the deacon"—the former, of course, when Vespers is served without a deacon. At Vespers outside the Vigil this is the first and only censing at this service. Of the other psalms, only the 118th is accompanied by censing.

The censing at **"Lord, I have cried"** the 13th-century Georgian manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon directs to be performed by the priest (although the litanies are pronounced by the deacon), yet all Greek and Slavic manuscripts direct it as in the present Typikon [^2173]. The *Diataxis* of Patriarch Philotheus (14th c.) assigns it only unto the deacon, who receives a blessing from the priest with the words: "Bless, master, the censer"; the priest: **"Blessed is our God always, now and ever, and unto ages of ages"** [^2174] (an echo of the ancient beginning of Vespers with **"Lord, I have cried"**?).

## The Number of Stichera

On Sunday the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** number 10, "for the sake of the perfection of this number," according unto St. Symeon of Thessalonica [^2175]. Besides Sunday, the same number of stichera and for the same reason is appointed at the Ascension and at the Vespers of the Sundays of Great Lent themselves (except Palm Sunday); in other instances 10 stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** occur on account of a concurrence of commemorations and an abundance of hymnographic material—thus on September 1 and at the Liturgies of the Presanctified Gifts. On the Twelve Great Feasts, excluding those always linked with Sunday—Palm Sunday and Pentecost—the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** are appointed for 8, including also at Pascha; so likewise for great saints; for lesser saints 6, and for small commemorations 3. In this manner, in the number of stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** Sunday surpasses the greatest feasts, notwithstanding that the Typikon in the number of these stichera clearly measures according unto the importance of the feast. If here a simple inconsistency does not obtain, then thereby, perhaps, the thought is instilled that Sunday is after all superior unto all other feasts in the event commemorated (before Pascha Vespers is still semi-lenten); moreover, a large number of stichera on Sundays was required by the almost invariable combination with them of the commemoration of a saint or another festal observance; with the normal number of stichera for commemorations of the latter kind being 3, there remain only 7 Resurrection stichera, one fewer than for great feasts.

The Western Studite Typika and the Evergetis Typikon had 8 stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** on Sunday: 4 Resurrection and 4 of the saint [^2176]; the Studite-Alexian Typikon had 9 [^2177], inasmuch as it tripled the 3 Resurrection stichera (if there was no commemoration of a great saint); the same occurs according unto the *Hypotyposis*, § 18, yet the passage is not read in all manuscripts; the Jerusalem Typikon from of old has 10 [^2178].

## The Composition of Stichera on Sunday

The customary composition of stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** is: 3 Resurrection stichera, 4 other Resurrection stichera called "Eastern" (*vostochny* / *anatolia*), and 3 from the Menaion. The first 3 Resurrection stichera, constituting in this manner a distinct group of stichera, are by an unknown author. To attribute them unto St. John of Damascus (8th c.), as is commonly done, is hardly possible in view of the fact that certain Western Studite Typika (the Typikon of San Nicola di Casole and others of the same recension), while ascribing the alphabetical stichera at the Aposticha unto the name of St. John of Damascus, the present

stichera designate as "ancient" (*archaia* [Gr. ἀρχαῖα]) [^2179]. They are simpler in content than the Damascene stichera and do not contain his favorite thoughts. — Concerning the 4 "Eastern" *(Orig. p. 546)* stichera in each Sunday service at **"Lord, I have cried"**, one cannot say with certainty what their designation signifies and unto whom they belong. The Greek word *anatolika* [ἀνατολικά] can hardly be translated, as is done in Slavic books in the margins, as "Anatolian" [^2180] (this in Greek would be *Anatoliaka* [Ἀνατολιακά]); moreover, the name of the author in the titling of stichera is always expressed by the genitive case, and not by an adjective; therefore, although there was a hymnographer Anatolius unto whom belongs a series of stichera for certain feasts and saints, presumably the Abbot of Studios (9th c.), yet neither unto him [^2181], nor still less unto Patriarch Anatolius of Constantinople (5th c.) [^2182], in whose day such a genre of church hymnody did not yet exist, can the "Eastern" stichera be attributed; these are evidently anonymous stichera, designated thus presumably because they entered for the first time into the service in the eastern Jerusalem Typika and were unknown unto the most ancient Studite ones: they are mentioned, and immediately in the present quantity, for the first time by the Jerusalem Typika beginning with its most ancient manuscripts [^2183]. Of Western Typika only the Typika of the Italo-Sicilian recension contain them, whereas neither the Studite-Alexian nor the Evergetis Typikon contains them [^2184]. In comparison with the first, "ancient" Resurrection stichera, the "Eastern" ones are somewhat longer and more detailed in content. — After the 7 Resurrection stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** there are appointed 3 stichera from the Menaion. This is, so to speak, the normal composition of stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** of Sunday Vespers, yet at the same time perhaps the rarest. On the majority of Sundays of the year, besides the commemorations of one saint there is yet another commemoration: of another saint, of a feast, a forefeast, an afterfeast, or an Apodosis; even with the commemoration of a single saint this rubric is inapplicable if that commemoration is more solemn than usual, that is, if the saint is great, with a polyeleos, with great doxology, or of six verses; finally, on the Sundays of the Triodion there are no stichera from the Menaion at all. All these cases are provided for by the Typikon in subsequent chapters, and rubrics are given for them. There is not provided for, either here or subsequently, only that case which is perhaps the most frequent—namely, the coincidence with Sunday of the commemoration of two saints ("small"), of whom each possesses a distinct service. For this case one is obliged to be guided either by the rubrics of Chapter 2 of the Typikon, "Concerning a Saint Having a Vigil on Sunday" [^2185], inasmuch as a saint with a Vigil has a double quantity of stichera and troparia at the canon in comparison with the ordinary, or one may be guided by the rubric for September 1 if it fall on Sunday [^2186], wherein *(Orig. p. 547)* there are likewise 2 commemorations, although

not of two small saints, and both commemorations are more solemn, or by the rubric for September 7 if it fall on Sunday, when there are likewise 2 commemorations, yet not both of saints. According unto all these rubrics alike, at **"Lord, I have cried"** it is necessary to sing 3 Resurrection stichera, 1 Eastern sticheron, 3 unto the one saint, and 3 unto the other. This case is directly provided for and such a rubric is given for it in the preface ("Brief Declaration" [*Izyavlenie vkratse*]) unto the General Menaion, a book unknown in the Greek Church, of very late origin (16th c.), with the indicated preface being of yet later date.

Typika of the Studite type having 8 stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** of Sunday Vespers, among which there were no "Eastern" ones, appointed the Resurrection stichera for 4 and unto the saint 4, with doubling of the former; those having Eastern stichera appointed: 3 Resurrection, 2 Eastern, and 3 unto the saint. The Studite-Alexian Typikon, having 9 stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, did not allocate a place for stichera of the Menaion, excluding great saints, and tripled the 3 Resurrection stichera [^2187]. The Jerusalem Typika, beginning with the most ancient manuscripts, are unalterable on this point. That they in no manuscripts provide for two saints on Sunday testifies either that this point of the Typikon hath been preserved without change from the time when for each day of the month there existed no more than one service unto a saint, or that when there were two saints on Sunday in former times the service of one was omitted. It is remarkable that even the Chapters of Mark (*Markovy glavy*) of all recensions, while providing for all manner of concurrences with Sunday, do not speak of such a concurrence, whereas they speak, for example, of the concurrence with Sunday of a forefeast or afterfeast with two saints, directing then the omitting of the service of the second saint; they speak also of how to sing the troparia if on Sunday there be two saints with distinct troparia [^2188]; yet concerning the stichera and canon for this same case they contain no rubrics.

## The Dogmatika

The most solemn stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** are sung with the refrain **"Glory to the Father..."**, wherein unto the first half of the refrain is joined the sticheron unto the saint if a sticheron with such a refrain is appointed for him (a *doxastikon* / *slavnik*); for the most part such a sticheron is appointed not unto the ordinary melody of the tone, but unto a special melody, and is called an *idiomelon* (*samoglasen*). Unto the second half of this refrain, unto **"Both now..."**, or unto the entire refrain if at **"Glory"** there is no sticheron unto the saint, is joined a hymn in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos, with Whose glorification each series of hymns in the Orthodox Church customarily concludes. The Theotokion concluding the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** of Sunday Vespers is always sung in the tone of the Sunday, notwithstanding the possible—and customarily avoided by the Typikon—discrepancy of such a tone with the preceding stichera, and in contrast unto the Aposticha stichera, which have a Theotokion always according unto the tone of the last sticheron, as well as in contrast unto the Praises stichera, which always have on Sunday one and the same Theotokion, consequently in one and the same tone (the 2nd). This is in view of the exceptional sublimity of these Theotokia, belonging unto St. John of Damascus, which by their entire aggregate present the complete doctrine concerning the most important of dogmas—the dogma of the Incarnation—and on that account are called "dogmas" or "Dogmatika". One can perceive a connection among all 8 Dogmatika according unto their content. "In the Dogmatikon of Tone 1 the unfolding of the dogma of the Incarnation begins with an indication of the universal glory of the Most Holy Virgin; thereby as it were is recalled the promise concerning the seed of the woman given unto the foreparents in Paradise and fulfilled in the Most Holy Virgin. In the Dogmatikon of Tone 2 is shown the relation of Old Testament types unto New Testament events. In the Dogmatikon of Tone 3 is shown the very birth according unto the flesh of the Lord

=== LEAF p656 ===
our Jesus Christ, and the manner of this birth. Inasmuch as it is necessary to know the purpose of the Incarnation of the Son of God, this is unfolded in the Dogmatikon of Tone 4. Further, it is incomprehensible unto us how the Mother of God, being a Virgin, gave birth unto Christ and how after childbirth She remained a Virgin: this is brought somewhat nearer unto our understanding through the content of the Dogmatikon of Tone 5 (through certain Old Testament types). Inasmuch as we do not yet know in what manner in the person of Jesus Christ two natures are united, the Divine and the human, this is spoken of in the Dogmatikon of Tone 6, namely, that the two natures are unconfusedly and indivisibly united in the one Person of the God-Man. Nevertheless, despite all explanations, the Incarnation of God the Word is a mystery understood more by faith than by the inquiring intellect; for this reason in the Dogmatikon of Tone 7 it is stated that it was accomplished in a supernatural manner. In the Dogmatikon of Tone 8 the dogma concerning the Incarnation of God the Word is set forth in a positive and concise manner" [^2189].

*(Orig. p. 549)*

## The Distribution of Stichera Between the Choirs

Concerning the actual manner of singing the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** the Typikon says nothing, yet it is understood of itself that they are sung by choirs alternately (*po likam*), and that before each sticheron the same choir sings also its refrain [^2190]. If the chanting is with a canonarch, then it is likewise understood of itself that before the stichera of the Menaion the canonarch announces their tone and *prosomoion*; if the tone of the Resurrection stichera coincides with that of the Menaion, he proclaims: "The same tone, *prosomoion* such-and-such" [^2191]. The last verse of the psalms, **"For His mercy is confirmed"**, with the last sticheron the Typikon directs both choirs to sing together—this in view of their timely convergence for the united singing of the Theotokion, during which the Entrance takes place and which therefore must be sung more solemnly than the other stichera. "At the final verse the choirs converge, signifying thereby both their mutual harmony and the union of the whole world through the Savior" [^2192]. At this same verse the priest is directed, having bowed unto the superior, to proceed (understood: from his *stasiad* / stall) into the sanctuary and vest for the Entrance (the deacon vests earlier—for the last Little Litany and the censing).

The above-mentioned 13th-century Georgian manuscript directs at **"For [His mercy] is confirmed"** that the priest with the deacon enter into the sanctuary and vest, and the choirs converge at **"Glory... Both now..."** and the Theotokion. The same is directed in Greek manuscripts, yet concerning the union of the choirs they state in general that at the Entrance they are already united. Slavic manuscripts speak of the vesting of the priest alone and of the union of the choirs at the 10th

# THE ENTRANCE

## Its Meaning

The most important and sacred rite of Vespers is the Entrance (*eisodos* [Gr. εἴσοδος]). The very name of this rite fully indicates its essence. The sacred ministers now enter into the sanctuary and approach the Holy Table, evidently in order that their intercession for the faithful may be more powerful. Hitherto they entered into the sanctuary for brief periods through the side doors for necessary sacred actions; now they enter into it solemnly in order to remain there for prayer. Such *(Orig. p. 550)* a significance first and foremost the Entrance possesses likewise at the Liturgy, upon the foundation of which Vespers arose, at times replacing it: as there the priest enters into the sanctuary for the offering of the unbloody sacrifice, so here he enters for the offering of the rational sacrifice. From the Entrance the service acquires a more prayerful than laudatory character; from it begin the blessings of the priest; the service becomes more abundant in grace and bolder before God. "The Entrance is performed," says St. Symeon of Thessalonica, "for the imparting from the sanctuary of the grace of God and for the offering up of our prayer" [^2194]. The Entrance has also another, more mystical signification, linked, however, with that already indicated. It signifies "that the Only-begotten Son of God, having descended unto us from the heavenly spheres, ascended again and raised us unto heaven" [^2195]. Inasmuch as this was accomplished preeminently through the Incarnation of the Son of God, the Entrance signifies His Incarnation, and the Holy Doors [Royal Doors], like the impassable gate of the Prophet Ezekiel, the Most Holy Virgin (for which reason they are provided with an icon of the Annunciation) [^2196]. In this manner, the Entrance at Vespers expresses by action that which the Theotokion sung during it expresses by words. Action is more powerful than word, and the prophets, when their predictions mounted unto special intensity, replaced words by actions (the so-called "symbolic actions"). Therefore the mystery of the Incarnation does not content itself for its expression in divine worship with the words of a hymn, but seeks a more effectual expression, to a certain degree as it were being accomplished again through this action (as occurs with the death upon the Cross of the Savior in the Eucharist). Such an important significance the Entrance has at Vespers. It is in truth the holiest moment thereof.

## The Order of the Entrance

=== LEAF p659 ===
From this meaning and significance of the Entrance are explained all the particulars of its setting, which the Typikon in Chapter 2 indicates briefly, referring, evidently, unto the *Sluzhebnik* for the detailed order. According unto the Typikon, the priest during the singing of the verse **"For His mercy is confirmed upon us"**, that is, the last [verse] at **"Lord, I have cried"**, having bowed unto the superior, enters into the sanctuary and vests in the phelonion. Upon the pronouncing of **"Glory"**, the holy doors [Royal Doors] are opened, and the Entrance is made in the following order: in front are carried two candlesticks with candles—as a sign, of course, of the light of the teaching of Christ (two—either for greater solemnity in comparison with the initial censing, when the priest is preceded by a single candle, or, perhaps, as an indication of the two natures in Christ, as in the hierarch's dikirion); after them goes the deacon with the censer, by the incense whereof, according unto St. Symeon of Thessalonica, "is depicted the sweet-smelling soul and life *(Orig. p. 551)* of the Savior" [^2197]; and then goes the priest "plain" (*prost* / *haplous*), that is, with lowered hands—as a token of reverence (which is then required also of all by the exclamation: **"Wisdom! Stand upright!"** [*Sophia, orthoi!* / *Premudrost', prosti!*]), with lowered phelonion (meaning the ancient phelonion with the front portion reaching unto the feet)—of course, as a token of reverence in imitation of the Seraphim, who covered their feet with their wings [^2198]. The *Sluzhebnik* supplements these rubrics with the observation that at the Entrance "they proceed from the right side behind the Holy Table" and go forth through the north door; the latter as a token of the self-emptying (*kenosis* / humiliation) of the Son of God at the Incarnation.

Having gone forth from the sanctuary, the priest (further according unto the Typikon) takes his stand facing the holy doors [Royal Doors], while the deacon stands unto the right of him, somewhat obliquely; the deacon, having inclined slightly, holding his orarion with three fingers of his right hand, says quietly, yet so that the priest can hear: **"Let us pray unto the Lord."** The priest recites quietly the prayer **"Evening and morning and at midday"**, named in the *Sluzhebnik* "the Prayer of the Entrance". This prayer, in which there is an expression from the most important liturgical hymn **"We hymn Thee"**: "we praise, we bless, we give thanks, and we pray unto Thee" (significantly!) [^2199], repeats the supplications of the psalms at **"Lord, I have cried"** (as it were offering these supplications also from the priest), namely entreating God that our prayer may be directed unto Him as incense, and that our heart may not incline unto evil words and thoughts; it entreats further for deliverance from all that hunt after our souls; the prayer concludes with the doxology (termed "the exclamation") of the Great Litany: **"For unto Thee are due all glory, honor, and worship..."**, whereby it is expressed that a new independent part of Vespers begins, or that the latter properly begins from here, whereas up unto now there was preparation. The further rites of the Entrance the Typikon describes

=== LEAF p660 ===
in the following manner. The deacon, having stood upright (*vosklonivsya*), says unto the priest, pointing toward the east (a region so rich in grace for a Christian) with the orarion, which he holds with three fingers of his right hand: **"Bless, master, the holy Entrance."** The priest blesses toward the east, saying: **"Blessed is the Entrance of Thy holy ones, O Lord, (the conclusion in the *Sluzhebnik* at the Liturgy:) always, now and ever, and unto ages of ages. Amen."** Thereby is expressed the assurance that in the Entrance there participate both the holy Angels, for which the priest directly prays in the prayer of the Liturgical Entrance, and the spirits of the righteous, inasmuch as it as it were repeats the Entrance of the Savior into heaven. Thereupon the deacon steps aside toward the superior, censes the icon at his place and the superior himself (asking thereby also of him a blessing upon the Entrance), and returns unto his former place, awaiting the end of the sticheron ("the verse"); upon the conclusion thereof he "enters into the midst", that is, approaches the center of the holy doors [Royal Doors] and, having signed a cross with the censer [^2200], as a token that by the Cross the doors of heaven are opened unto us, proclaims: **"Wisdom! Stand upright!"** (*Sophia, orthoi!* / *Premudrost', prosti!*), inviting thereby unto reverent standing in view of the sacred importance of the present moment, that is, of the Entrance, as is *(Orig. p. 552)* done also before the most important moments of the Liturgy—at the beginning of the Liturgy of the Faithful, before the "Canon" [Anaphora] of the Liturgy, and likewise before the dismissal of Vespers and Matins. (Unjustly is this proclamation considered preparatory unto the hymn **"O Joyful Light"** [*Svete tikhiy* / *Phōs hilaron*] or unto the prokeimenon: neither the one nor the other has place after the Entrance at the Liturgy, when the same exclamation is uttered; at weekday Vespers, when there is no Entrance, the exclamation is appointed neither in the Typikon nor in the *Sluzhebnik*).

After the proclamation, continues the Typikon, during the singing of **"O Joyful Light"**, the candle-bearers carrying the candles at the Entrance approach with them unto the holy doors [Royal Doors], the deacon enters into the sanctuary and censes the Holy Table (it is not said "round about"), while the priest, having bowed before the holy doors [Royal Doors] and kissed them, enters through them, after which the holy doors [Royal Doors] are closed. Upon entering into the sanctuary the priest, according unto the *Sluzhebnik*, having turned unto the west, stands with folded hands awaiting the conclusion of the prokeimenon, thereupon bows and departs unto his place—it is presumed, presumably, as is done in practice, that the priest stands during the singing of the prokeimenon at the High Place. By practice it is accepted that the priest at the Entrance kisses not the holy doors themselves, which are open, but their doorposts, or rather—the small icons placed upon the latter (the kissing of the doors themselves is preserved for hierarchical services). In exactly the same manner, by practice it is accepted to close the holy doors not immediately after the Entrance, but after the singing of the prokeimenon [^2201].

## Ancient Typika and the Greek Typikon Concerning the Entrance

In ancient manuscript copies of the Jerusalem Typikon the Vesperal Entrance is described more briefly than in the present Typikon, which
