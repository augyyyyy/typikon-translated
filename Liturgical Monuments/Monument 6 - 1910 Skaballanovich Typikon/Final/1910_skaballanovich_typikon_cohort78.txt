=== LEAF p771 ===
...by the church typikon: they are time and again appointed as model hymns (*prosomoia*, *podobny*) unto sessional hymns, even on the greatest feasts: for example, the sessional hymns on the Nativity of Christ after the 2nd Kathisma, Theophany after the 1st, the Annunciation after the 3rd have as their model melody **"Awed by the beauty of thy virginity"** (*Krasote devstva*); on Theophany after the 2nd Kathisma, and likewise on the Nativity of Christ and the Annunciation after the Polyeleos—**"Joseph was astonished"** (*Udivisya Iosif*). Of the high merit of the sessional Theotokia bears witness also the fact that certain of them have been taken into other services, even of the greatest feasts; thus, the Theotokion of Tone 3 after the 2nd Kathisma, **"Incomprehensible and Indescribable"** (*Nepostizhimago i neopysannago*), serves as the sessional hymn of Matins of the Nativity of Christ after the 2nd Kathisma; the sessional Theotokion of Tone 2 after the 2nd Kathisma, **"All-blessed art thou"** (*Preblagoslovena esi*), serves constantly as the Theotokion for the Praise Stichera of Sunday Matins. And the Theotokion of Tone 8 after the 2nd Kathisma—the concluding kathisma unto all—**"All of creation rejoiceth in thee"** (*O Tebe raduetsya*), serves as the Megalynarion (*Zadostoynik*) of the Liturgy of St. Basil the Great, for which reason 'we sing it not sitting, but standing, and with fear and reverence.' On account of the high merit of the Theotokia, they are almost never omitted at the Sunday service, even upon the concurrence with a Twelve Great Feast of the Mother of God, although, as we have seen, they speak almost nothing of the Resurrection, but are of general content.

The sessional Theotokia are replaced only by troparion Theotokia—namely, as is stated concerning this in Chapter 52 of the Typikon, if the troparia at **"God is the Lord"** are sung with a Theotokion not of the current tone, then this Theotokion is sung after the 1st Kathisma, while the regular one is omitted (since the Theotokia after the 1st Kathisma are superior [**"Joseph was astonished,"** **"Awed by the beauty of thy virginity,"** **"Gideon prefigured"**], it is better to transfer them unto the 2nd Kathisma and omit those appointed there, excepting, of course, the Theotokion **"All of creation rejoiceth in thee"**) [^2436].

*(Orig. p. 639)*

### Sunday Sessional Hymns According to Ancient Typika (*Voskresnye sedal'ny po drevnim ustavam*)

Sessional hymns are already indicated by the well-known description of the Sinaitic Matins (Introductory Chapter, p. 295) under the name *kathismata anapausima* (Gr. καθίσματα ἀναпаύσιμα), which in Slavonic is inaccurately translated as 'Sunday kathismata' (*kafizm voskresnykh*); it should rather have been translated: 'restful sessional hymns' (for resting, see below concerning the readings).

In the *Hypotyposis* they are called *troparia tou kathismatos* (Gr. τροπάρια τοῦ καθίσματος) [^2437].

In the Holy Sepulchre *Order of Passion and Paschal Week*, the designation 'sessional hymn' (*kathisma*), besides its present usage, is applied unto all troparia: after **"Now lettest Thou..."** (*Nyne otpushchaeshi*), and at **"God is the Lord"** [^2438].

The Evergetis Typikon after the 1st Kathisma had a 'Sunday sessional hymn'

=== LEAF p772 ===
...and after the second—a 'hypakoe' (*ypakoi*), because it did not have the Blameless (*Amomoi*).

The Studite typika speak indistinctly concerning sessional hymns.

Of the MSS of the Jerusalem Typikon: the Georgian MS speaks of one sessional hymn with a Theotokion after each kathisma; the ancient Greek MSS: 'we say the Sunday sessional hymn of the tone, the first, and if there be more sessional hymns, we say the verse: **"Arise, O Lord, help us..."**, and we likewise sing the other; but if not, **Glory... Both now...** and the Theotokion'; later MSS and the printed Greek add: 'and again the psalm verse: **"I will love Thee, O Lord,"** and again the Sunday sessional hymn and the Theotokion'; the Slavic MSS are like the present one, but later ones do not indicate the verses. The Old Believer Typikon gives the 1st verse as in the Greek. In the Western divine service an analogy unto the sessional hymns is constituted by the responsories, although they follow after the readings (Introductory Chapter, p. 307); they correspond for the most part unto the content of the reading, yet at times are common unto different readings; the Sunday ones do not differ in character from the weekday ones.

### Readings After the Kathismata (*Chteniya po kafizmam*)

After the sessional hymn follows the reading prepared by it. The readings of Matins are of an entirely different kind from the reading at Great Vespers: the latter is taken, on Sunday at least, from Holy Scripture; the former—from patristic writings. The rule for these readings is set forth in Chapter 10 of the Typikon. According unto it, the readings serve as commentaries of St. John Chrysostom or Blessed Theophylact on the New Testament *(Orig. p. 640)* (the commentaries are read on that Evangelist from whom the Gospel at the Divine Liturgy is taken), from Symeon Metaphrastes, and the *Margarites* (*Pearl*). The length of the reading is not defined, but if one divides all the books named therein among the readings of the entire year, there will fall upon a single reading about 10 octavo pages. Neither in the present place of the Typikon nor in Chapter 10 is it indicated from which book the reading is taken at which place of Matins; it is only noted concerning the reading after the 1st Kathisma that it takes place 'in the Commentary Gospel' (*Tolkovoe Evangelie*) and concerning the reading after Ode 6 of the Canon that the *Prolog* is read then. Nor is the order of the reading indicated here; it is given in Chapter 35 for the trapeza: the reader pronounces the 'preface,' that is, the name of the author and the title of the book, while the superior or the priest on duty exclaims: 'By the prayers of him whose reading this is' (that is, 'By the prayers of Saint *N*, O Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us'). In ancient manuscripts of liturgical readings, after the title of each reading there is the addition: 'Bless, father,' unto which the exclamation of the priest serves as the response.

### Their History (*Istoriya ikh*)

=== LEAF p773 ===
Already Canon 17 of the Council of Laodicea prescribes: 'Psalms should not be joined together in a continuous sequence in the services, but a reading should take place in the interval after each psalm.' Balsamon says that this is prescribed for rest. The readings at Matins in the well-known description of the 7th-century Sinaitic Vigil are from the Catholic Epistles, taken in course. The *Hypotyposis*, the *Diatyposis*, and the Studite typika, while mentioning readings at the same places of Matins as today, do not indicate the content of the Sunday readings. Among non-Orthodox traditions, readings are especially developed in the Armenian divine service, where they are called 'exhortations' (*uvehshchanie*); of such 'exhortations' there are even 2 at the Hours, 5 at the Midnight Office (= the 1st part of Matins), and up to 3 at Matins in the strict sense (Introductory Chapter, pp. 299–301). In ancient Western rules readings were likewise very highly developed, especially in the Rule of St. Benedict (ibid., pp. 310ff.), where it is noted concerning them: 'at vigils books of both the Old and New Testaments are read, and commentaries upon them composed by the most renowned orthodox teachers and ecumenical fathers.' At the present Roman Catholic Sunday Matins there are 9 readings: the first three from the Old or New Testament (Epistles), the second three from the Fathers, the 7th the Gospel, and the 8th and 9th its homiletic exposition; in length each reading is less than half a duodecimo page; before the reading a blessing is implored: 'Command, O Lord, a blessing (*Iube Domine benedicere*)'; the blessing before each reading is varied, with the response Amen [^2439].

*(Orig. p. 641)*

# THE BLAMELESS OR THE POLYELEOS (*Neporochny ili Polieley*)

### The Blameless (*Neporochny*)

The second part of Matins draws near its conclusion, and therewith, following the general rule of our services, solemnity must increase. Now there arrives the most sublime section of this part and, according unto the customary view, the most solemn moment of the entire Matins and of the whole Vigil—the singing of the Polyeleos or the Blameless (*Neporochny*), with the Gradual Antiphons (*anabathmoi*), concluded by the reading of the Gospel. Yet concerning the Polyeleos, which at the ordinary Sunday Vigil is appointed to be sung comparatively rarely—for a little over 3 months in the course of the year—the Typikon makes no mention whatever in the present place. After the 2nd Kathisma and its reading, it says here, 'we sing the Blameless.' Taking thus the place of the festal Polyeleos at the ordinary Sunday Vigil, the Blameless (*Amōmos*, Gr. Ἄμωμος) is fully suited for such a substitution. Thus is named Psalm 118 [LXX; Masoretic 119], **"Blessed are the blameless in the way"** (*Blazheni neporochnii*), after its second word, which expresses the theme of the entire psalm, and therefore in antiquity was prefixed unto it during singing as an antiphon or melodic model (as even now in the Order of Burial). Representing an ecstatic hymn unto the Law of God, the psalm depicts the ardent love of the righteous man for this Law, or, more precisely, toward God for His so perfect and beneficent gift unto mankind. This greatest of psalms, containing 176 verses, in the Hebrew text consists of 22 strophes beginning in alphabetical order with all the letters of the alphabet; in almost every strophe, consisting of 8 verses, the name Jehovah [YHWH] is used once (at least 22 times in all), and the Law is called by various names: 'law,' 'commandment,' 'statutes' (properly 'ordinances'), 'testimonies' (that is, revelations), 'judgments' (that is, decrees), 'precepts' (in Slavonic likewise 'commandments'), 'ways,' 'words'—all this, of course, with the epithet 'Thy,' that is, 'God's'; by these are designated various aspects of the Law of God. The righteous man finds no words to depict his devotion unto this Law and his zeal for it: for him it is dearer than all treasures and good things, higher than all earthly wisdom; he meditates upon it day and night, prays for heavenly illumination to comprehend it, is ready to suffer for it, and well-nigh paid for it with his life. With such a content, the psalm is applicable, first of all, unto the Savior, unto the entire work of His life, for which there is given here the most touching depiction that can possibly be conceived; and secondly, the psalm is applicable unto every Christian, as sketching a complete ideal for him. The psalm

=== LEAF p775 ===
...breathes a remarkable warmth, acting with irresistible charm upon a heart in any degree capable of religious feelings, and surpassing in this respect the other psalms. All this prompted the church order to make Psalm 118 [LXX; Masoretic 119], which constitutes the 17th Kathisma, the funeral hymn over the Savior, introducing it into Holy Saturday Matins, which it fills more than halfway—just as it is sung over every Christian in the Order of Burial. Adjoining the kathismata as the last of them, and moreover adapted, in contrast unto the first two kathismata, unto the commemorations of the day, the psalm *(Orig. p. 642)* at this place of Matins well prepares the subsequent joy of the Resurrection. In general, the relationship of the Typikon unto this psalm is exceptional, answering fully unto its high merits: the kathisma which it alone constitutes is not considered regular; when the Psalter is sung twice a week, as in Great Lent, the 17th Kathisma is excluded from the ordinary alternation, from which even the 1st Kathisma is not excluded; of all the kathismata, it alone is not omitted at the end of Passion Week, when kathismata are entirely absent; furthermore, it serves as the opening psalm of the Midnight Office (weekday)—a service celebrated at the most contrite hour of the night.

### Method of Execution (*Sposob ispolneniya*)

In execution at church services, Psalm 118 [LXX; Masoretic 119] is divided, like other kathismata, into three parts, or 'Glories' (at the burial service sometimes into two), named after their opening words: **"Blessed are the blameless"** (*Blazheni neporochnii*), **"Thy hands have made me"** (*Rutse Tvoi sotvoriste mya*), and **"Look upon me"** (*Prizri na mya*), which gradually decrease in length: the 1st Glory, verses 1–72, depicts preeminently the firmness of the righteous man in the Law; the 2nd, verses 73–131, asks for consolation in persecutions; the 3rd, verses 132–176 [printed 177], asks for victory in conflict.

The Blameless is appointed to be sung in Tone 5—of course, in that melody of this tone wherewith are sung the 'Sunday troparia' immediately adjoining the psalm—**"The assembly of the Angels"** (*Angel'skiy sobor*). The melody of Tone 5 represents a union of tender contemplation, reaching at times unto quiet sorrow, with joy—yet a joy not so much exultant as deep and serene, and therefore most suited unto the event of the Resurrection, which cost the Savior so much sorrow and the shadow of death. Therefore in this tone (only in another, special melody of it) is sung the troparion and the most important stichera of Pascha (**"Sacred Pascha"**). The Typikon does not indicate more particularly how to sing this psalm, perhaps because it is self-evident that it must be sung, like

=== LEAF p776 ===
...other psalms, alternately by the two choirs by verses, and, of course, with the inserted 'Glories,' excepting the concluding 'Glory,' which is placed already after the troparia **"The assembly of the Angels."** Such a manner of singing considerably shortens the execution (under skillful singing, when not only are there no pauses between the singing of both choirs, but one choir begins without awaiting the complete finish of singing by the other). In view of the considerable length of the psalm and the duration of the Sunday Vigil, no refrains are appointed for the psalm such as it has after each verse, for example, at the burial service. In place of such refrains, there is added at the end of the psalm a series of troparia with the subjoining unto them of the most characteristic verse in the psalm, expressing its primary thought: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord, teach me Thy statutes"** (*Blagosloven esi, Gospodi, nauchi mya opravdaniem Tvoim*). By this addition the thought is instilled that, properly, the entire psalm ought to be sung with the addition unto each verse of a Sunday troparion (as on Holy Saturday), and if this is not done, it is solely from lack of time.

*(Orig. p. 643)*

### History of the Blameless (*Istoriya Neporochnykh*)

Psalm 118 [LXX; Masoretic 119] under the name 'Blameless' (*Amōmos*, Gr. Ἄμωμος) occupied a very prominent place at Sunday Matins according unto the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, both in its most ancient form, in the 10th–11th centuries (see Introductory Chapter, p. 339), and in the so-called Chanted Office (*asmatikos akolouthia*): it constituted the central and larger part of this Matins; it was sung here in three staseis (*statii*) with the refrain on the 1st and 3rd of **"Alleluia,"** and on the 2nd—**"Give me understanding, O Lord,"** with censing and a solemn entrance into the temple at the conclusion of the psalm (ibid., pp. 345–346).

The Studite typika did not have this kathisma at Sunday Matins, restricting themselves unto the 2nd and 3rd Kathismata [^2440]. They had it on Saturday, wherein it was for the most part sung without Alleluia (see below, 'Concerning the Saturday Service'), consequently, as with us on Sunday [^2441].

The Jerusalem Typikon concerning the Sunday Blameless: the Georgian MS: 'after the 2nd Kathisma and its reading, **"O give thanks unto the Lord"** with 3 Alleluias (Psalm 135 [LXX; Masoretic 136], that is, the Polyeleos, see below), the Sunday sessional hymn, the reading, and the Blameless, during which the priest censes'; the most ancient Greek MSS: 'we sing the Blameless in Tone 5, at which the priest also censes' [^2442]; later Greek: 'then the Blameless'; Slavic MSS (14th–15th centuries): 'we recite the verses of **Blessed are the blameless** with refrains in three staseis; but after the third stasis, not having said **Glory... Both now...**, he says the troparion of Tone 5 **The assembly of the Angels**'; later MSS: 'we sing

=== LEAF p777 ===
...the Blameless in Tone 5'; the Old Believer Typikon: 'we sing the Blameless in Tone 5' [^2443].

### The Polyeleos (*Polieley*)

According unto Chapter 17 of the Typikon, on Sundays from the Apodosis of the Exaltation, that is, from September 22, until the Forefeast of the Nativity of Christ, December 20, and from the Apodosis of Theophany, January 14, until Cheesefare Sunday, instead of the Blameless there is sung the *Polyeleos*, that is, Psalm 134 [LXX; Masoretic 135], **"Praise ye the name of the Lord"** (*Khvalite imya Gospodne*), and Psalm 135 [LXX; Masoretic 136], **"O give thanks unto the Lord"** (*Ispovedaytesya Gospodevi*). The grounds for such periods are:
1) the Polyeleos, occupying more time than the Blameless, is sung preeminently on autumn and winter Sundays, when the nights are longer and, consequently, the Vigil must be started earlier and ended later;
2) the Polyeleos is canceled from December 20 unto January 14 owing unto the concurrence in this period of great feasts having a Polyeleos more solemn than the Sunday one, whereby the impression of the latter would be weakened;
3) in Great Lent the Polyeleos is canceled on account of the mournful character of this period.

Psalms 134 and 135 [LXX; Masoretic 135 and 136] are homogeneous in content. They invite Israel unto the glorification of God the Creator (134:6; 135:5–9) and Provider (134:7; 135:25) for the election of Israel as the people of God and for all the mercies shown *(Orig. p. 644)* unto him throughout the whole of his history, especially for the deliverance from the Egyptian bondage (134:8–10; 135:10–16) and the settlement in the Promised Land (134:10–12; 135:17–20); these mercies obligate Israel to be faithful unto God and to shun idolatry (134:15–18). The homogeneity of the psalms reaches in places unto literal identity in expressions (134:10–11 and 135:17–20). Both psalms breathe the most ecstatic joy, pouring forth in an irrepressible stream of gratitude; but the second psalm, developing the thoughts of the first, is more ecstatic and powerful than it (**"praise"** and **"give thanks"**; 134:5: **"our Lord is above all gods"** and 135:2–3: **"unto the God of gods,"** **"unto the Lord of lords"**; 134:3: **"for the Lord is good,"** 135:1: **"for His mercy endureth unto the ages"**); special solemnity is imparted unto the second psalm by the accompaniment of each verse thereof with the refrain **"For His mercy endureth unto the ages,"** which is repeated, consequently, 26 times and sounds with particular force in verses 17–20, where it divides into 4 parts a single grammatical sentence (**"Unto Him that smote great kings, for His mercy endureth unto the ages; and slew mighty kings, for His mercy endureth unto the ages; Sehon, king of the Amorites, for His mercy endureth unto the ages; and Og, king...**

=== LEAF p778 ===
...**of Bashan, for His mercy endureth unto the ages"**). — According unto Symeon of Thessalonica, the Polyeleos psalms are 'a triumphal song proclaiming the marvelous works of God, especially the passage of our souls out of the Egypt of sin and delusion unto faith in Christ, our deliverance from Pharaoh and the Egyptians, that is, the devil and demons, the deliverance of the Church from Diocletian, Maximian, and the Apostate, and other most ungodly rulers, as from Amalek, Sehon, Og, and those like unto them—and after trials and labors, settlement in the Promised Land and the heavenly inheritance through Jesus' [^2444].

The order of singing the Polyeleos psalms is not indicated in the Typikon and other liturgical books. Musical tradition prescribes to sing them with the refrain of Alleluia after each verse: in some melodies this Alleluia is sung thrice, in others after the first psalm once and after the second thrice, wherein after the first half of the verse it is sung once, and after the second twice, or vice versa. The melody for the Polyeleos psalms is not tonal, even if it be idiomelic (*samoglasny*) (as, for example, for the Prooemiac Psalm or **"Blessed is the man"**), but entirely independent, distinguished by special solemnity, exultation, and majestic slowness (the Polyeleos psalms are more than twice as short as the Blameless).

### Its History (*Istoriya ego*)

The Polyeleos, though it is unknown in what sense, is indicated by the 7th-century Jerusalem Canonarion in the Georgian version at the Paschal Vigil 'in Tone 8 with Alleluia,' as well as by later recensions of the Constantinopolitan-Hagia Sophia Typikon, for example, on the Exaltation (Introductory Chapter, p. 340). In a monument with a clearly expressed character of this Typikon—in the Kondakar of the Annunciation Monastery (wherein there is the so-called *asmatikon*—a species of chant of unknown purpose, but applicable only unto the Chanted Office)—Psalm 135 *(Orig. p. 645)* is set to musical notation in different tones: Tone 1 from verse 1 through 4, Tone 2—from verse 5, Tone 3—from verse 9, Tone 4—from verse 13, Tone 5—from verse 17, Tone 6—from verse 21, Tone 7—from verse 23, Tone 8—from verse 25 [^2445].

In the Studite-Alexian Typikon Psalms 134–136 [LXX; Masoretic 135–137], although sung only in course among the other psalms of the 19th Kathisma wherein they are included—consequently, always on Friday at Matins—nevertheless had a particularly solemn execution, a special melody of Tone 4 or 6, a refrain of Alleluia differing in quantity for each of these psalms, and were called the Polyeleos (Introductory Chapter, p. 365). Thus, according unto this typikon, the Polyeleos occurred every Friday and never otherwise. Presumably, Matins of the most ancient type, when on Sunday it had no kathismata, likewise had no Polyeleos; therefore it was an adjunct only of the Sunday Matins

=== LEAF p779 ===
...of the Forty Days [Great Lent]; and today the complete Polyeleos—with Psalm 136 [LXX; Masoretic 137]—occurs only before the Forty Days. Western Studite typika appoint Psalm 135 or both as festal psalms at Matins alongside others, sometimes with special refrains, akin to our megalynaria (*velichaniya*) (Introductory Chapter, p. 366). The Jerusalem typika in ancient Greek MSS make of these psalms a usage analogous unto the Studite-Alexian (ibid., p. 376), but speak of the Polyeleos only on feasts (as does also the Evergetis Typikon) and during the period of 3 kathismata (from September 20 unto Great Lent) [^2446].

According unto Arseny Sukhanov, in the East 'he who says the Psalter, that same one at the analogion at the choir stall says **Lord, have mercy** thrice, **Glory... Both now...**, then the two psalms which are at the Polyeleos, at the end **Glory... Both now...**, and they do not sing those psalms' [^2447].

In the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra the Polyeleos psalms are sung in the following composition: **Praise ye the name of the Lord, alleluia; praise the Lord, O ye servants, alleluia** (3). **For the Lord is great, and our Lord is above all gods, alleluia** (3) (and so forth). **O Lord, Thy name endureth unto the ages, and Thy memorial from generation to generation. Blessed be the Lord out of Zion... O give thanks unto the Lord, for He is good, alleluia** (2). **For His mercy endureth unto the ages, alleluia** (and so further 'alleluia'). **With a strong hand and a high arm. And He hath redeemed us from our enemies. O give thanks unto the God of heaven.**

### Censing at the Blameless and Polyeleos (*Kazhdenie na Neporochnykh i polielee*)

Both the Blameless and the Polyeleos that replaces it are accompanied by censing, which in divine worship marks the most solemn and sacred moments. Symbolizing, as always, the power and God-pleasingness of prayer, which truly reaches during the singing of these psalms the highest degree of intensity; symbolizing likewise the grace of the Holy Spirit attracted by such prayer; contributing unto the elevation of the spiritual mood by the pleasant fragrance of incense, censing in the present case has further a relationship unto the commemoration associated with this part of Matins: it recalls that sweet-smelling myrrh which 'with tears of compassion' the Myrrh-bearing Women blended at this hour of the night of the Resurrection. Concerning censing at the Blameless, in the continuous order of the Typikon it is only noted that 'at them the priest censes.' This brief indication in *(Orig. p. 646)* the rubric to the chapter of the Typikon under consideration (the 2nd 'Notice' [*Zri*]) is supplemented:

'the priest with the deacon only (not concelebrating, as at the megalynarion, see below) vest in the sacred vestments (from the preceding discourse in this rubric it is clear that the priest vests in the phelonion) and the deacon takes

=== LEAF p780 ===
...a candlestick with a burning candle (usually in place of this a single candle, but a large one), while the priest takes the censer, and having censed round about the holy table and the whole altar, they go forth through the north doors, and he censes the Royal Doors and on both sides the holy icons and on the analogion, then the superior and the choirs and the standing people, as is customary, and they enter again through the doors of the south side.'

Consequently, the Royal Doors are not opened during this censing, in accordance likewise with Chapter 23 of the Typikon, according unto which the Holy Doors in the first half of Matins are opened only for the reading of the Gospel, which is still far off (the hypakoe and the reading) and the preparation for which (the bell ringing) begins only with the Gradual Antiphons (*anabathmoi*). In like manner is the censing performed, according unto the aforementioned rubric of the Typikon, 'and on other days, [when] a megalynarion is sung unto anyone apart from a vigil,' that is, at the Polyeleos to a saint without a vigil. But at the Polyeleos of a feast or of a saint with a vigil, this censing is performed already with greater solemnity:

'an analogion is set (a reading table with a slanted top, by virtue whereof the icon placed upon it is visible unto all) in the midst of the temple, and upon it is placed the festal icon of the Day of the Lord, or of the Mother of God, or of the Saint (male or female); and the superior (the sole place at the Vigil where he takes direct part in the sacred ministration) vests in the sanctuary (altar), and all the concelebrants in phelonia according unto custom, and having gone forth from the altar through the opened Royal Doors, they take their stand on both sides round about the analogion.' The saint, whose icon is surrounded by the ministers of the altar, stands as though visible in the temple in the midst of the worshipers, responding thereby unto their desire (expressed in a hymn unto St. John the Baptist: 'Come and stand with us') [^2448].

If the icon depicts an event, then it is, as it were, reenacted among the faithful, while the clergy, as the most worthy, are present at its very accomplishment. Thus the glorification of the saint or event in this place of the Vigil reaches the highest degree of immediacy, when all the faithful, headed by the clergy, surround the very author of the celebration and converse with him face to face, 'as though one were speaking unto his friend.' 'And the superior distributes candles unto the concelebrants and unto the holy people standing in the temple.' The candle in the hand of the worshiper signifies the spiritual burning of the whole being in
