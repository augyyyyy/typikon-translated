# VOLUME II: EXPLANATORY EXPOSITION OF THE TYPIKON — PART I: ORDER OF LITTLE VESPERS (Typikon Chapter 1)

## EXPLANATORY EXPOSITION OF THE TYPIKON

## The Title of the Book

### The Meaning of the Word "Typikon"

In every book, its title is of the utmost importance. A good book title immediately reveals to the reader what new insight he will find within it, and even guides him toward the resolution of the question to which the book is devoted. The book now called the *Typikon* previously bore other titles—for example, the designation still in common use today: the *Ustav* [Rule]. Its present title developed over centuries, and this fact alone demonstrates that it must be deliberate and deeply significant. Being an adjective derived from *typos* (τύπος)—feature, form, model, pattern, norm—*typikos* (τυπικός) signifies "fashioned according to a model"[^1835]; yet it is not identical with *antitypos* (ἀντίτυπος), an imprint or copy. Therefore, in English the word *typikos* corresponds somewhat to the word "exemplary," if the latter is used in the sense of "most fully conforming to its archetype." *Typikon* (Τυπικόν), with which *biblion* (βιβλίον, book) is understood, can signify a "book of models"—models, understandably, of divine worship. Only in the Orthodox Church does the book setting forth the order of divine service bear such an original title. It is untranslatable into the Slavonic language, for which reason it was retained without translation. The word *ustav* [rule/statute] would be an inaccurate rendering of the Greek *typikon* (*ustav* corresponds rather to the Greek *diataxis* [διάταξις]).
*(Orig. p. 447)*

### The Meaning of the Title

Such a title artfully defines the character not only of the book to which it is assigned, but also of the divine worship itself with which this book is concerned. In relation to this divine worship, a book bearing such a title seeks not so much to enact statutory legislation for its minutest details—thereby eliminating all freedom on the part of the officiants—as it seeks to depict a lofty ideal of divine worship, an ideal whose beauty would constantly evoke an involuntary striving toward its realization, even if that realization, in full measure, might not always be possible, just as the realization of every ideal and the following of every lofty model is not always possible. Such, essentially, is the entire law of Christ: not fully realizable in all its heavenly height, yet by His divine majesty inspiring an irresistible yearning in humanity toward its fulfillment, and through this imparting life unto the world.

### The History of the Title

It is not surprising that so profound a title for our book had a long and intricate history. Along the path of its development, the Typikon exchanged the following designations. The one whom tradition considers the author of the rule presently in force among us, the so-called Jerusalem Typikon—St. Sabbas the Sanctified (5th c.)—named his concise rule himself, or those who recorded it named it, with three words at once: *Typos kai paradosis kai nomos* (Τύπος καί παράδοσις και νόμος: model, tradition, and law). To the representative of another form of the church rule, which for a long time enjoyed far wider dissemination than the first—St. Theodore the Studite (9th c.)—tradition attributes a rule bearing the name *Hypotyposis* (Ὑποτύπωσις: outline, sketch). To St. Athanasius the Athonite (10th c.), who transferred this second rule to the Holy Mountain of Athos where it was to undergo a new redaction, is attributed the composition of a *Diatyposis* (Διατύπωσις: "re-formation," "representation")[^1836]. The most ancient of the complete typika that have come down to us, the rule of the Church of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (9th–10th c.), has survived without its title leaves, but in all probability bore the title *Synaxarion* (Συναξάριον), a collection of assemblies—that is, an indicator of the days on which solemn liturgical assemblies take place[^1837]. Competing with this rule in antiquity and completeness, the Sinai manuscript of the rule is entitled *Kanonarion* (Κανονάριον), a collection of canons [rules]. In the 11th century, the term *typikon* (τυπικόν) already appears applied to the church rule, although still accompanied by other explanatory titles. The rule of the Evergetis Monastery in Constantinople in a 12th-century manuscript is inscribed *Synaxarion ētoi Typikon* (Συναξάριον ἤτοι Τυπικόν). The rule in a manuscript of the Turin University Library is entitled *Typikon ētoi diataxis tēs ekklēsiastikēs katastaseōs kai akolouthias* (Τυπικόν ἤτοι διάταξις τῆς ἐκκλησιαστικῆς καταστάσεως καί ἀκολουθίας: *Typikon, that is to say, Order of the Ecclesiastical Constitution and Office*). In the 13th century, in manuscript titles *(Orig. p. 448)* the single word *typikon* (τυπικόν) already appears without explanatory additions. Thus is entitled, for example, a 13th-century manuscript of the Vallicelliana Library in Rome[^1838], and, seemingly, as far as can be deciphered, a manuscript in the Sevastyanov collection of the Moscow Rumyantsev Museum (No. 491/35). In this manner, in all attempts to title our book, preference was most frequently given to words derived from *typos*. Ancient Slavonic translations hesitate in rendering the Greek *typikon* between *ustav* and *tipik*. The majority of manuscripts translate it as *Ustav*; thus the manuscripts of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 (14th c.), No. 329/384 (14th c.), and others. But manuscript No. 27/1458 of the Sevastyanov collection of the Rumyantsev Museum (1372) reads *Tipik*; likewise No. 330/485 of the Moscow Synodal Library of the late 14th century. Printed editions prior to 1641 were entitled *Ustav* or *Tserkovne Oko* (lit. 'Eye of the Church'); in the edition of 1682 this title was corrected to *Typikon*. Among the Old Believers: *Ustav*, and in the afterword: "that is to say, *Tserkovne Oko*" (*siye rech' Tserkovne Oko*).

### The Full Inscription of the Typikon

The full title of the Typikon at present on the flyleaf reads: "The Typikon, that is, the Rule" (*Tipikon siyest' ustav*). On leaf 1: "The Typikon, that is to say, the depiction of the ecclesiastical order in Jerusalem of the holy Lavra of our venerable and God-bearing father Sabbas. The same order also obtains in the other honorable monasteries in Jerusalem, and likewise in the other holy churches of God" (*Tipikon sirech' izobrazhenie tserkovnago posledovaniya vo Iyerusalime svyatyya lavry prepodobnago i bogonosnago ottsa nashego Savvy. Tozhde posledovanie byvayet i v prochikh vo Iyerusalime chestnykh obitelekh; podobie i v prochikh svyatykh Bozhiikh tserkvakh*). The title, therefore, links the origin of the present rule with the Lavra of St. Sabbas the Sanctified, situated near Jerusalem. The history of the liturgical rule does not permit us, as we have seen, to determine with precision what in the present Typikon must be attributed to St. Sabbas and the customs of his monastery. Indisputably, such an origin can be assigned only to the custom of the Sunday All-Night Vigil, with the order of which the present Typikon begins. It is possible that originally the present inscription of the Typikon was applied primarily to the "office" (*posledovanie*) of the Sunday Vigil.

The title in nearly this form is repeated in all complete manuscripts of the rule, known at present only from the 13th century onward. But in some of them the final sentence—"The same order also obtains..."—is absent (for example, in a 16th-century Greek manuscript from the library of the Lavra of St. Sabbas itself[^1839]). Without this sentence, the title must have had its original form. The next oldest form of the title must be when, in place of this sentence, there stands the addition: [in the Lavra of St. Sabbas] "and in the majority of the holy monasteries found here" (thus in a Greek manuscript of the Vatican Library of 1346, No. 320/331). In the majority of manuscripts, only the concluding addition is lacking: "and likewise in the other holy churches of God"—evidently the latest addition of all (thus in the manuscripts of the Moscow Synodal Library, Nos. 328/383 and 329/384, Rumyantsev Museum Sevastyanov collection No. 27/1458, and others).
*(Orig. p. 449)*

# CHAPTER 1 OF THE TYPIKON: "THE ORDER OF LITTLE VESPERS"
## (LITTLE VESPERS AND ITS ACCOMPANYING SERVICES ON SUNDAY)
*(Orig. p. 450)*

## THE CONTENTS AND DIVISION OF CHAPTER 1 OF THE TYPIKON

The Typikon begins with the exposition of the Sunday service, because it was formed earlier than the weekday service and served as the foundation and model for the latter. Such a beginning for the Typikon is all the more natural since in the majority of churches divine services are celebrated comparatively rarely on weekdays.

Such a beginning for the Typikon, like its title, was likewise chosen not without historical hesitation. The most ancient monuments of the church rule of the 9th–10th centuries—the *Hypotyposis* attributed to St. Theodore the Studite, the *Diatyposis* bearing the name of St. Athanasius the Athonite, and the *Kanonarion* of the Sinai Library—begin with the Paschal service. The Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th century and others begin with the Menologion. Typika of the Studite type begin with the preparatory Sundays before the Great Fast. Typika of the Jerusalem type were the first to begin the Typikon with the evening service, which is explained by the significance they attached to the celebration of the Lord's Day [Sunday].

The exposition of the Sunday service in the Typikon begins with Saturday evening in view of the fact that the liturgical day among us Christians, as among the Jews, begins in the evening[^1840], because the course of time, according to the teaching of Holy Scripture, began in the evening. Moreover, it is all the more natural to begin the celebration of the Resurrection in the evening because the very Resurrection of Christ took place on "the evening of the Sabbath"[^1841]—that is, during the Saturday night. Naturally, the church rule appoints that this night, beginning from the evening, be spent in prayers.

The sequence of such prayers, or Sunday services, opens with the Ninth Hour and Little Vespers. Chapter 1 of the Typikon sets forth the order of these two services, subjoining to them also the order of the evening meal [trapeza]. Prior to examining these three parts into which Chapter 1 of the Typikon naturally divides, we prefix a preliminary remark on the bell-chime [blagovest] and on the customary beginning of church services, since both are mentioned here in the Typikon for the first time.

## THE BELL-PEAL [BLAGOVEST]

### The Meaning of the Bell-Chime

Much attention in general is devoted to the bell-chime [*blagovest*] in the Typikon, and rightly so. It not only announces the time of divine service, but also prepares Christians for it: the grace-filled effect that it exerts upon the soul is universally recognized. For those absent from the divine services, it in a certain manner even *(Orig. p. 451)* substitutes for the latter. It is, properly speaking, already divine worship itself, celebrated by the sounds of music. Old Testament worship consisted predominantly of music; and behold, as though in commemoration of this, and in token of its indissoluble bond with that worship, the more spiritual and exalted New Testament worship possesses in the bell-peal its own sacred music—of all forms of which it has chosen, in this manner, the most simple, austere, and unartificial.

### The History of the Bell-Chime

Like the entire order of church services, the bell-chime had a long history, and assumed its present form—that of bell-ringing—at a relatively late date, although no later than when the church services themselves assumed their current form. Naturally, under a simpler structure of divine worship, the method of summoning to it was also simpler. The Old Testament Church, however, possessed, one might say, a more musical method of summoning to divine service than ours (which is natural given the entire character of its worship)—namely, by means of silver trumpets[^1842]. Among ancient Christians during the period of persecutions, bishops summoned the faithful to divine service by sending messengers to their homes. St. Ignatius the God-bearer advises in a letter to St. Polycarp of Smyrna: "Let assemblies be held more frequently; summon all to them by name (*ex onomatos*)"[^1843]. In the spurious epistle of Ignatius the God-bearer to the deacon Heron, among the duties of a deacon is placed also the summoning of the faithful to divine service, each one individually[^1844]. Tertullian calls liturgical assemblies *convocationes*[^1845]. In the Church of Jerusalem in the 4th century, the archdeacon during divine service announced the place and time of the subsequent service with such formulas as: "Let us all be ready at such-and-such an hour in such-and-such a place" (for example, "in the Martyrium," "on the Mount of Olives")[^1846]. In the primitive coenobia of Egypt, according to the words of St. John Cassian, they summoned the brethren to divine service by knocking upon the door of their cells: "When sitting in their cells and diligently engaged in labor and meditation, they hear the sound of the knocker (*sonitum pulsantis*) upon their door and the doors of other cells, which summons them to prayer or to some common task, each one immediately hastens to depart from his cell"[^1847]. In accordance with this, in the *Life of St. Pachomius* the summons to divine service and to the refectory in his monastery is designated by the word "to strike" (*krouesthai* / κρούεσθαι)[^1848], which is why in the 5th century "striking"—*krousma* (κρούσма), *(Orig. p. 452)* *krouma* (κροῦμα)—becomes the technical term for the summons to divine worship[^1849] (as also in the present Typikon).

In the *Lausiac History* of Palladius (5th–6th c.) the instrument for this knocking is also indicated; here, concerning the monk Adulphes (4th c.), it is recorded: "Having fulfilled the customary canon of prayer, he immediately struck with an awakening mallet (*tō exypniastikō sphoriō* / τῷ ἐξυπνιαστικῷ σφορίῳ) upon all the cells, gathering [the brethren] into the churches for the morning doxology"[^1850]. Thus, a mallet for knocking upon the cell door is the most ancient instrument for summoning to divine service. But from the 6th century onward we find mentions also of the semantron [*bilo*]. In the *Life of St. Theodosius the Great* († 529), composed by his contemporary Theodore, Bishop of Petra, concerning the summons to the Hours and the Liturgy it is recorded: "they struck the wood (*ekrouon to xylon* / ἔκρουον τὸ ξύλον)"[^1851]; the same appears in *The Spiritual Meadow* of John Moschos[^1852]. Besides *xylon* (ξύλον)—"wood"—the semantron was called *krousma* (κρούσμα, striking), *semantron* (σύμαντρον, a hand-semantron), *sēmantēr* (συμαντήρ), and *sēmantērion* (συμαντήριон, the latter being, presumably, the mallet for striking the semantron), and *sidēron* (σίδηρον)—an iron semantron. Iron semantra are mentioned for the first time only in the complete typika of the 11th–12th centuries; in some of them and in Balsamon (12th c.) there is mention also of bronze semantra[^1853]. At present, semantra are used predominantly on Athos, Sinai, and in Palestine, and in general in large monasteries (including Russian ones) alongside bells. A wooden semantron is called *sēmantron* (σήμαντρον), and an iron one *sidēron* (σίδηρον); distinctions are also made between the "great" semantron and the "little semantron" [*bil'tse*]; the largest semantra (festal ones) are called "heavy" (*tyazhkaya* / βαρέας)[^1854]. In addition to material and size, semantra are distinguished also by the manner of their use: there are hand-held semantra and suspended semantra. In shape, the semantron is an elongated straight or curved board, sometimes with perforations at the ends. The hand-held semantron is held with the hand or placed by its center upon the left shoulder; the suspended semantron is hung from chains or a rope passed through the center of the board. They strike the semantron with a mallet of the same material, and depending on whether the blow is struck nearer to or farther from the center, sounds of different pitch are produced, forming an entire gamut of tones (especially on a *(Orig. p. 453)* metal semantron), thanks to which the striking of the semantron acquires an entirely musical character[^1855].

Bells appeared originally in the West and are first mentioned by Gregory of Tours († 594) under the name *signa*[^1856].

Dageus, an Irish bishop († 586), being a master of copper and iron smithing, made 300 bells (*campanos*)[^1857]. In the East, bells are mentioned for the first time in the 9th century: according to the Venetian chronicle of John the Deacon, Doge Ursus sent to Constantinople, at the request of Emperor Basil the Macedonian (876–879), 12 bells for a church constructed by the latter[^1858]; Byzantine historians recounting the construction of this church, however, do not mention the bells. Transferred from the West around this time, bells were evidently admitted to liturgical use in the East not without hesitation and not entirely willingly. Although from the 11th and 12th centuries there are several accounts of the use of *kōdōnes* (κώδωνες—bells, presumably of small size[^1859], whence through corruption of the word arose *kandiya*) in the churches of Constantinople and Thessalonica[^1860], yet Balsamon *(Orig. p. 454)* in the 12th–13th century remarks concerning semantra: "Among the Latins there exists another custom of summoning the people into the churches, for they employ a single sign—I mean the campan (*kampanon*), which they call thus from the field (*kampos* / κάμπον), because, according to their words, just as an open field presents no obstacles to one who wishes to travel, so also the high strike of a bronze *kōdōn* resounds everywhere"[^1861]. (Balsamon's etymology for *kampanon* is not uncontroversial; others derive this word from Campania, which supplied the finest bronze for bells). And in the early 15th century, Symeon of Thessalonica speaks only of semantra.

Introduced and disseminated so late in the East, bells were banned by the Turks (permitted in Constantinople only in 1856). In Rus', chronicles mention bells from the 11th century (first in 1066), but seemingly as a great and costly rarity: bells serve as war booty, carried off by princes who conquered Kyiv and Novgorod. In the 14th century (under the year 1394), mention is made for the first time of a bell-tower—in Pskov—and of bell-founders in Moscow (under 1342 and 1346, and in Tver under 1403)[^1862].

### The Mode of the Bell-Chime for Little Vespers

The church rule recognizes the following modes of the bell-chime (and of ringing in general during divine services): festal, Sunday, weekday of three degrees of solemnity, Lenten, and funeral. Of these modes of bell-ringing (concerning which more detailed treatment will be given under the corresponding rubrics of the Typikon)[^1863], for Little Vespers there is appointed the weekday ringing of the lowest solemnity—on the small campan (bell). Such a bell-chime is appointed also for Compline, the Midnight Office, and the Hours, equally on weekdays and on feasts. The bell-chime conforms in solemnity not only with the day, but also with the service. Because all the specified services, with the exception of Little Vespers and the Midnight Office, are identical in structure on feasts and weekdays, the bell-chime for them is appointed to be always identical and of the lowest solemnity (according to the most ancient typika of the Studite type, on feasts these services—especially the Hours—were omitted altogether). Little Vespers and the Midnight Office, however, are subsumed in the Typikon (as we shall see) under the same type as the Hours.

### The Time of the Bell-Chime

As regards the time of the bell-chime for Little Vespers, it is indicated by the present Typikon indeterminately: "before the setting of the sun," whereas for other services, especially on the most important days of the Triodion and Menologion cycles, this time is indicated more precisely—by the hours. Here an indeterminate indication is given in view of the difference between the length of the day in summer and in winter.
*(Orig. p. 455)*
In ancient Greek and Slavonic manuscripts of the Typikon (14th–16th c.) there is added to this rubric: "that is, about the tenth hour." This addition was excluded by the Nikonian correctors (it was retained in the Old Believer Typikon), because the assignment of fixed hours for church services is inconvenient in view of the unequal length of the day in winter and in summer: the tenth hour (3–4 p.m.) in summer would fall in the heat of midday. The most widely accepted time for Little Vespers, as for Daily Vespers, is in Russia 3–4 p.m. in winter and 5 p.m. in summer. On Athos, the bell-chime for Little Vespers begins about 3 p.m.[^1864]

### The Order of the Bell-Chime

The bell-chime for Little Vespers, as for every service, the Typikon appoints to be performed by the paraecclesiarch (assistant ecclesiarch) or candle-lighter (*kandilaptēs* / κανδηλάπτης), who receives a blessing for the bell-chime from the superior (the abbot). Every task in a monastery is performed with the blessing of the superior, all the more so such a sacred action as the bell-chime—the beginning of divine service. By the blessing is expressed also the superior's consent to the beginning of the service. Oversight of the timeliness of the bell-chime, however, is laid first of all upon the paraecclesiarch—a thought conveyed by the characteristic expression: "he comes to the superior, signifying by his coming the time of the striking."

According to the *Life of St. Sabbas*, written approximately 20 years after his repose, the canonarch summoned the brethren to the services "at the hour of striking" (*hora krousmatos* / ὥρα κρούσματος) each time "with the assent (*para gnōmēn* / παρὰ γνώμην) of the superior."[^1865] According to the Greek manuscript of the Rule from the library of the Monastery of St. Sabbas, MS 1458, the ecclesiarch tolls the bells for the service and prepares everything necessary for it. According to the earliest Slavic manuscripts of the Rule, this is performed by the "kandilaptis, that is, the candle-lighter";[^1866] likewise in the current Old Believer Typikon. The phrase "signifying by his approach the time for striking" is absent from the earliest manuscripts.

# THE USUAL BEGINNING

## Its Significance

The Typikon naturally attaches special significance to both the beginning and the end of church services and therefore surrounds them with special solemnity, seeking in both to present in compendium the entire content of divine worship, as it were encapsulating the whole faith and hope of Christians. Therefore the usual beginning, like the customary conclusion (the dismissal / *apolysis*), of church services is composed of the most *(Orig. p. 456)* substantive prayers. Most especially, the "usual beginning" directly constitutes, as it were, a complete and self-contained service.

## The Opening Blessing of the Priest

The usual beginning of church services consists of two parts quite unequal in scope: the opening exclamation (blessing) of the priest and the opening prayers of the reader. The priest's exclamation is thus, in the proper sense, the "beginning" of the service, its primordial starting point. The Typikon assigns it extraordinary significance. Without this blessing, the service cannot begin. Consequently, despite the brevity of this exclamation, it may be regarded as a component fully coequal with all the remaining extensive content of the usual beginning. The opening exclamation, generally speaking, contains the glorification of God. Through this content, the opening blessing well expresses the entire character of Orthodox worship, wherein the glorification of God predominates not only over petition, but also over thanksgiving and instruction; by virtue of this, Orthodox worship is devoid of that utilitarian-practical character typical of Roman Catholic worship—with its predominance of the petitionary element—and of Protestant worship—with its predominance of homiletical content. Thereby Orthodox worship most closely approximates heavenly worship, the angelic doxology of God. (And the character of worship stands, of course, in intimate connection with the general comprehension of Christianity across its three confessions). An exclamation of such content immediately elevates our thought to that glory and praise which all His creatures offer to God, and of which the service we are undertaking is intended to form a certain part. This initial glorification of God, or more precisely—merely the confession of that Divine glory which exists even without us—is not identical before different services; rather, in its solemnity it corresponds to the degree of solemnity of the service itself. Most sublime and expressive is the opening exclamation of the Divine Liturgy, which glorifies distinctly all the Persons of the Holy Trinity and in Their most grace-filled and mysterious operation (in the establishment of the Kingdom of God); in the opening exclamation of Matins the Holy Trinity is glorified only in Her indivisibility and general operation upon the world (as life-creating); whereas the opening exclamation of the remaining services is limited to the glorification of God in general. Yet even in this last form, the opening exclamation of church services constitutes, in the view of the Typikon, such a sacred mystery that the unconsecrated lips of a layman cannot touch it. This is, of course, because within it the Name of God is pronounced for the first time in the service with extraordinary solemnity—as the constitutive fundamental concept and content of the entire service—the Name which the ancient Hebrew considered unlawful to utter, replacing it with descriptive divine designations or (like the Samaritans) with the word *shema* ("the Name"), or else accompanying it with the phrase: "He is blessed forever."[^1867]
*(Orig. p. 457)*

## History of the Opening Blessing

**"Blessed is God"** (*Blagosloven Bog* / *Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεός*) is a frequent Old Testament expression and the most common glorification of God in both the worship and everyday life of the ancient Hebrew.[^1868] But concerning how ancient the use of this expression is as the opening blessing of the Hours, Vespers, and other services, nothing can be asserted with certainty, since to this day no complete manuscript Horologia of public divine worship earlier than the 13th century have been discovered (cell Horologia have been preserved in 8th–9th century manuscripts), and even these Horologia do not deem it necessary (given the very purpose of the Horologion) to indicate the priest's exclamation. The Hieratikons of the 9th–12th centuries (Euchologia) restrict themselves to the text of the priest's prayers at Vespers and Matins. It is seemingly in 13th-century manuscripts that the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** (*Blagosloven Bog nash* / *Εὐλογητὸς ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν*) is first clearly indicated; thus, for example, it is indicated in the 1292 Typikon of the Sicilian Monastery of Mili according to Vatican Library MS 1877 as the exclamation for the Hours and Matins (in the order for the first week of the Fast), whereas for Vespers the exclamation is **"Blessed is the Kingdom"** (*Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo* / *Εὐλογημένη ἡ βασιλεία*).[^1869] In the 13th-century Typikon of the Georgian Monastery of Shio-Mgvime, the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** is appointed for weekday Matins,[^1870] and in the Typikon of the Sevastianov Collection of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum MS 491/35 for Vespers (fol. 153v).

Naturally, in the earliest period the opening exclamation for divine services varied, like the liturgical orders themselves, across different local Churches. Yet it is remarkable that already the *Testament of Our Lord Jesus Christ* (*Testamentum Domini Nostri Jesu Christi*), a Syriac document of the 3rd century, indicates an exclamation by the bishop before Matins identical in substance to our current one: "Glory to the Lord."[^1871] The 6th-century Sinai ascetics began the service with "Glory to the Father..." (that is, with the Minor Doxology).[^1872] The Greek Horologion according to the order of the Lavra of St. Sabbas in Sinai Library MS 863 of the 8th–9th centuries likewise begins services with "Glory to the Father..." (in these latter two cases, the beginning may be indicated solely for cell services). The Coptic Horologion begins the offices with "In the Name of the Father..." The exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** (*Blagosloven Bog nash*) was presumably formulated in imitation of the exclamation at the Liturgies of St. Basil the Great and St. John Chrysostom: **"Blessed is the Kingdom"** (*Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo*). But regarding this latter exclamation, one likewise cannot state how ancient it is. The earliest manuscripts of these Liturgies already contain it in its current form—the Barberini manuscript of the 8th century and Imperial Public Library MS 226 (from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius Uspensky) of the 8th–9th centuries. Yet in the time of Chrysostom, "the presiding celebrant, entering the church (that is, before the service), said 'Peace to all.'"[^1873] And the earliest so-called "apostolic" liturgies—of the Apostle James, the Evangelist Mark, and others—possess nothing *(Orig. p. 458)* corresponding to the current solemn exclamation of the presiding celebrant before the service.[^1874]
Among our contemporary services, the rites for the consecration of a church and of an antimension—which represent very lengthy and complex services—begin without an exclamation (starting with "Let us pray to the Lord" and a prayer), but in their midst contain a series of exclamations that now serve, or previously served, as opening blessings: **"Blessed is our God"**, repeatedly "Glory to our God unto ages of ages," "Glory to Thee, Holy Trinity, our God, always, now and ever, and unto ages of ages," and "Blessed be the glory of the Lord from His place."[^1875] An allusion to the liturgical use of this last exclamation is made in the words of Abba Silouan (4th c.) cited by Nikon of the Black Mountain (11th c.): disapproving of the chanting of newly composed troparia, the elder remarks that in the heavens "one choir chants Alleluia unceasingly; another choir: Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord of Sabaoth; another choir: Blessed be the glory of the Lord from His place and habitation."[^1876] Inasmuch as the services of the daily cycle generally imitated the Divine Liturgy, in those Churches where the Liturgy lacked anything corresponding to the present opening exclamation, the daily offices lacked it as well. It was presumably the Cathedral / Sung Office (*asmatike akolouthia*) that first introduced solemn exclamations at the beginning of Matins, Vespers, and the Hours following the model of the Constantinopolitan Liturgy, wherein originally such an exclamation was presumably literally borrowed from the Divine Liturgy; in the earliest monument of the Sung Office (12th c.), not only Matins and Vespers, but even the Hours possess the exclamation: **"Blessed is the Kingdom"** (*Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo*).[^1877] Monastic offices, adopt*(Orig. p. 459)*ing from the Sung Office the custom of a solemn exclamation at the beginning, presumably replaced the eucharistic liturgical exclamation with less solemn ones, introducing among them various gradations of solemnity.

## Structure of the Usual Beginning

The opening exclamation constitutes, as noted, although the chief, nonetheless the smaller and exclusively sacerdotal portion of the "usual beginning," which in the remainder of its content represents something independent. This remainder is subdivided, in turn, into several sections. First of all, one must distinguish from the usual beginning the first two prayers: **"Glory to Thee, our God"** (*Slava Tebe, Bozhe nash*) and **"O Heavenly King"** (*Tsaryu Nebesny*), as introductory prayers not constituting an indispensable portion of the usual beginning (it lacks them during fasts and at services for the departed). The first of these prayers constitutes, as it were, the opening exclamation of the reader following that of the priest (a variation of the priestly exclamation); the second (**"O Heavenly King"**) implores the abiding presence with us of the Holy Spirit, the true Author and Perfecter of every prayer pleasing unto God,[^1878] wherein, corresponding to the opening exclamation and the entire character of Orthodox worship, this petition is clothed in doxological form. With the exception of these supplementary and later (as we shall see) parts of the usual beginning, it represents a harmonious and intricate whole. Specifically, it resolves into three supplications: (1) the Trisagion (**"Holy God"**), (2) the Prayer to the All-Holy Trinity (**"All-Holy Trinity"**), and (3) the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"**), each concluding with a doxology: the first two with "Glory to the Father...", and the last with "For Thine is the kingdom..." The supplications increase in volume with each successive section: the threefold "have mercy on us" in the Trisagion gives way to a sevenfold petition in the second supplication (4 petitions in the prayer "All-Holy Trinity" and a threefold "Lord, have mercy" after this prayer) and a twelvefold "Lord, have mercy" in the third supplication. Correspondingly, the simple concluding doxology of the first two sections (the minor doxology) is augmented in the third into a sacerdotal doxology ("For Thine is the kingdom..."). Once again (like the opening exclamation and all Orthodox worship), this entire complex prayer—gradually ascending in its final section to the Lord's Prayer—despite all its spiritual tension, bears a joyful and laudatory character, beginning with the seraphic doxology, invoking the Persons of the Holy Trinity by diverse laudatory names across all supplications addressed to Them, and concluding with doxology (the final "Glory to the Father...").
The transition from the supplications of the usual beginning to the introductory psalms of each service is provided by the verse from Psalm 94:6 [LXX 94:6 / MT 95:6]: "Come, let us worship and fall down before Him," repeated like a prokeimenon three times with subtle textual modifications that masterfully intensify its theological emphasis: "... to the King, our God," "fall down before Christ (the King...)," and "(fall down before) Christ Himself (... and our God)."
*(Orig. p. 460)*

## Abridgment of the Usual Beginning

However, the usual beginning does not always assume this form. This represents its most complete form, which it receives three times during the course of the day before the three cycles of daily offices: before the cycle of morning services, daytime services, and evening services; since these cycles are opened respectively: the first by the Midnight Office, the second by the Third Hour, and the last by the Ninth Hour, it is only before these offices that the usual beginning is appointed in its full scope. Before the other services it is abbreviated to **"Come, let us worship"** alone. The most solemn services—the Divine Liturgy, the Typika (*Izobrazitelnye*) which replaces it, and the Mystery of Crowning (Marriage)—possess no usual beginning whatever. Conversely, during fasts, for the most important services that follow others—namely, Matins, and Vespers with the Ninth Hour (if these latter two do not follow immediately after the Sixth Hour)—as well as for services for the departed, the usual beginning omits only its introductory portion (**"Glory to Thee, our God"** and **"O Heavenly King"**), in view of its joyous tone (moreover, these prayers were introduced into the "usual beginning" later than the others, and without them it assumes its ancient form).

## History of the Usual Beginning

Like all ecclesiastical orders, the usual beginning evolved gradually. Its earliest composition was presumably restricted to the Lord's Prayer alone. In the 2nd century, secondary daily offices such as the Hours existed simply in the form of this single prayer.[^1879] When the structure of the services expanded, it was natural to place this prayer at the head of the service or make it its center. How early it became customary to begin church services with the Lord's Prayer is demonstrated by the fact that in this regard, the practice of the Western Church—generally so divergent from the Eastern—coincides with it:

every Roman Catholic service, except the Mass and Compline, begins with the Lord's Prayer recited secretly (*sub silentio*). The present "usual beginning" of Roman Catholic offices places it in the first position. Yet according to ancient breviaries—for example, a Mozarabic one—it was preceded by: "Lord, have mercy; Christ, have mercy; Lord, have mercy" (*Kyrie eleison, Christe eleison, Kyrie eleison*),[^1880] an exclamation preceding the Lord's Prayer in a somewhat modified and expanded form in our own "usual beginning." According to the Greek Typikon of the Sicilian Monastery of Mili (Vatican Library MS 1877) of 1292, the usual beginning of Matins and Vespers, at least in Great Lent, consists solely of the **"Our Father."**[^1881] In addition to the Lord's Prayer, the usual beginning in the Roman Catholic Church includes the "Hail Mary" (*Ave Maria*) and the Creed (the latter not always: only before Matins and Prime). With this one may compare the counsel of Abba Silouan (4th c.) recorded by Nikon of the Black Mountain: "When Thou risest from sleep, first of all glorify God with thy lips, and *(Orig. p. 461)* do not begin thy rule immediately; rather, stepping outside thy cell, recite the confession of faith and the Our Father, and then entering, begin thy rule."[^1882] Is not our present prayer **"All-Holy Trinity"** (*Presvyataya Troitse*) a vestige of the former presence of the Creed in the usual beginning?
No less ancient a component of the usual beginning than the Lord's Prayer and the "Lord, have mercy" is the Minor Doxology. Among the 6th-century Sinai ascetics, the usual beginning of the office was **"Glory to the Father"** (*Slava Ottsu*).[^1883] The gloss "that is, the Trisagion," which certain manuscripts of Nikon's *Pandects* append to these words where the Sinai liturgical practice is described—for example, a 16th-century manuscript of the St. Nicholas Edinoverie Monastery—merely testifies to how strange the absence of the Trisagion from the usual beginning must have appeared to later copyists of Nikon. Meanwhile, the usual beginning lacked the Trisagion not only in the 6th century, but even in the 8th–9th centuries, as demonstrated by the Greek Horologion according to the order of the Lavra of St. Sabbas in Sinai Library MS 863, wherein services begin with the Minor Doxology and the **"Our Father."**[^1884] Still later than the Trisagion was the prayer **"All-Holy Trinity"** introduced into the usual beginning; it is, however, already mentioned by the 11th-century Studite Typikon, Moscow Synodal Library MS 330/380 (fol. 12). Latest of all was the prayer **"O Heavenly King"** (*Tsaryu Nebesny*) introduced into the usual beginning; it appears in this position for the first time in 14th-century typika and horologia. Later still was introduced the prayer **"Glory to Thee, our God"** (*Slava Tebe, Bozhe nash*); although it is present in certain 14th-century Horologia (for example, Moscow Synodal Library MS 48/151/1238), it is absent from 16th-century manuscripts and from the printed Horologia of the Krakow edition of 1491. In the modern Greek *Hōrologion*, this prayer is indicated only in the usual beginning of the Midnight Office; for the Third and Ninth Hours, the prayer **"O Heavenly King"** is designated as the first prayer.

## History of the Individual Prayers of the Usual Beginning

Although in its present form it is of comparatively late origin, the usual beginning consists, nonetheless, of prayers of profound antiquity. The most ancient prayer within it must be acknowledged as **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Gospodi pomiluy* / *Κύριε ἐλέησον*).

### "Lord, Have Mercy"

"Have mercy" is the most ubiquitous prayerful exclamation in the Psalms; the formula **"Lord, have mercy"** occurs in the Prophets Isaiah and Baruch.[^1885] Concerning its liturgical use in the Christian Church, the earliest witness is provided by the *Apostolic Constitutions* (4th–5th c.), where it serves, as with *(Orig. p. 462)* us, as the response to the petitions of the liturgical litany (*ektenia*).[^1886] Into the daily offices this formula was borrowed from the litany and, consequently, serves as a substitute for it.

### "Our Father"

The next most ancient prayer in the usual beginning is the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"** / *Otche nash* / *Πάτερ ἡμῶν*). However, its concluding doxology did not issue from the lips of the Savior in the form in which it is used among us today, but rather, according to the most widely received text in the Evangelist Matthew, in the following form: "For Thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory forever, Amen";[^1887] according to the Evangelist Luke, the Lord's Prayer possesses no doxology whatever.[^1888] In view of this, and on account of the variance in the transmission of this doxology across different documents (for instance, in the *Teaching of the Twelve Apostles* [Didache][^1889] it is transmitted in an even briefer form than in Matthew: "For Thine is the power and the glory forever"), scholars today consider that this doxology found its way into the text of the Gospel of Matthew from liturgical practice.[^1890] (In the Roman Catholic Church it is used without a doxology).

### The Minor Doxology

The next most ancient component of the usual beginning is the doxology, the so-called Minor Doxology: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, now and ever, and unto ages of ages. Amen"** (*Slava Ottsu i Synu i Svyatomu Dukhu, nyne i prisno i vo veki vekov. Amin'* / *Δόξα τῷ Πατρὶ καὶ τῷ Υἱῷ καὶ τῷ ἁγίῳ Πνεύματι, νῦν καὶ ἀεὶ καὶ εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας τῶν αἰώνων, ἀμήν*), which enjoys the most pervasive use throughout Orthodox divine services. It arose, presumably, from the concluding doxologies of liturgical prayers: traces of these doxologies can be found repeatedly in the New Testament Epistles and in the Apocalypse. The simplest formula of such doxologies is: "To Thee" or "To Whom be glory (*doxa* / δόξα) forever."[^1891] This formula began to be expanded in all three of its clauses, wherein the expansion of the first term ("To Him") and of the last ("forever") gave rise to the present Minor Doxology, while the elaboration of the second term ("glory")—for example, into expressions such as "glory and honor," "glory and dominion"—gave birth to our contemporary litanic exclamations (concerning which more will be said hereafter). The Person ("To Him") to Whom the doxology is offered in New Testament literature is in most instances God the Father alone,[^1892] or God the Father through Jesus Christ, for example: "Unto Him be glory in the Church by Christ Jesus..."[^1893] or "To the only wise God our Savior, through Jesus Christ our Lord, be glory and majesty, dominion and power..."[^1894]
*(Orig. p. 463)*
Occasionally glory is ascribed to Christ alone.[^1895] The third term of the primitive brief doxology—"forever"—likewise receives amplification in apostolic literature: "unto ages of ages,"[^1896] or "throughout all generations of the age of ages,"[^1897] or: "before all ages, and now, and unto all ages."[^1898] In this sense one may say with Blessed Theodoret that the Minor Doxology was "handed down to the Church by eyewitnesses and ministers of the Word."[^1899] But the first example of a complete doxology explicitly naming all the Persons of the Holy Trinity is found in the final prayer of the Martyr Polycarp: "through Whom [Jesus Christ] to Thee, with Him and with the Holy Spirit, be glory..."[^1900] In like manner, the martyrial acts of St. Ignatius the God-bearer conclude with the words: "he finished his course in Christ Jesus our Lord, through Whom and with Whom be glory and might to the Father with the Holy Spirit forever, Amen." In similar fashion conclude the acts of other 2nd-century martyrs.
Moreover, in the *Life of St. Eugenia*, who suffered under Emperor Commodus (180–192 AD), there occurs a doxology almost in its current form; it is recounted therein that Angels escorting the martyr to heaven sang a hymn in which one could discern the words: "glory and honor to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, both now and ever, and unto ages of ages, Amen."[^1901] According to St. Irenaeus of Lyons (2nd c.), the Gnostics perceived in the words "unto ages of ages" (*eis tous aiōnas* / εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας...), uttered "by us in our thanksgivings," a designation of the aeons.[^1902] Tertullian (3rd c.) remarks that a Christian attending the public spectacles is forced "with those very lips wherewith he utters Amen in church (*in sanctum*), to give acclamation to a gladiator, shouting 'forever' to someone other than to Christ God."[^1903] In these passages of Irenaeus and Tertullian scholars discern a reference to the doxology.[^1904] Origen states that "prayer must always conclude with the doxology of God through Christ in the Holy Spirit."[^1905] In the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* (3rd c.), the formula of the doxology is identical to that in Polycarp, but alongside it our contemporary formula also occurs, occasionally both combined together, for instance: "through Whom [Jesus Christ] to Thee [the Father] be glory, and power, and honor to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit in the Holy Church, now and ever, and unto ages of ages";[^1906] or: "Glory to Thee, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, unto ages of ages."[^1907] Nevertheless, even in the 4th century the names of the Persons of the *(Orig. p. 464)* Holy Trinity in the doxology were predominantly not placed in the identical grammatical case. Thus, the prayers in the Euchologion attributed to Serapion, Bishop of Thmuis (4th c.), for the most part have the conclusion: "through Whom [Jesus Christ] to Thee [the Father] be glory and dominion in the Holy Spirit, now and unto all ages of ages."[^1908] Basil the Great cites the doxology in the form: "Glory to the Father and to the Son with the Holy Spirit."[^1909] The Arian heresy, exploiting for its own designs one of the ancient doxological formulas—"Glory to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit"—compelled the Church definitively to adopt the formula: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit"**, as that which most clearly expresses the consubstantiality (*homoousion*) of the Holy Trinity. According to certain reports, this formula was ratified at the First Ecumenical Council.[^1910] According to the testimony of Blessed Theodoret, Leontius, Patriarch of Antioch, in order to conceal his Arianism, pronounced the words "to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit" in the doxology in an undertone, but "and now and ever..." loudly and distinctly.[^1911] St. Flavian, Patriarch of Antioch, together with a multitude of monks, was the first of all to chant with distinct clarity: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit."**[^1912] In the treatise *On Virginity*, attributed to St. Athanasius the Great, the thanksgiving prayer after a meal concludes: "Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit." One of the Gallican Councils (of Vaison or Bazas), held several years after the Council of Nicaea, decreed: "Inasmuch as not only in the Apostolic See, but also throughout the entire East and in all Africa and Italy, on account of the machinations of heretics blaspheming against the Son—alleging that He was not always with the Father, but began from a certain time—in all conclusions of prayers (*clausulis*), after 'Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit,' there is said: 'As it was in the beginning, is now, and ever, and unto ages of ages,' therefore we decree that in all our churches it shall likewise be recited in this manner."[^1913] The Minor Doxology possesses this very form today in the Catholic Church: *"Gloria Patri et Filio et Spiritui Sancto. Sicut erat in principio, et nunc, et semper et in saecula saeculorum"*,[^1914] consequently differing from our own by a different arrangement of words concerning the Holy Spirit and by the addition: "as it was in the beginning" (which corresponds in our exclamations to: "always" alongside "now and ever"). Traces of primitive forms of doxology (in which the names of the Holy Trinity were *(Orig. p. 465)* not placed in identical grammatical cases) have been preserved among us in certain exclamations ("with Whom Thou art blessed, together with the All-Holy... Spirit"), and in the Roman Catholic Church in the conclusions of hymns (*"Gloria Deo Patri cum Filio, Sancto simul Paraclito"*); the preposition "through" (*dia* / *per*) with the Name of the Son, despite New Testament prototypes, is avoided by both Churches in view of Arian abuses thereof.

### The Trisagion

The prayer **"Holy God..."** (*Svyaty Bozhe...* / *Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός...*) represents an amplification of the Seraphic hymn in the vision of the Prophet Isaiah [Isa. 6:3]. According to the tradition related in the epistles cited by Zonaras (12th c.)[^1915] of Patriarch Acacius of Constantinople (471–479) and other contemporary bishops to the Chalcedonian presbyter Peter the Fuller (who sought to introduce into this prayer the addition "Who wast crucified for us"), it was heard from the Angels by a boy lifted up into the air during a penitential moleben on the occasion of an earthquake in Constantinople in 438–439 (under Emperor Theodosius the Younger, Pulcheria, and Patriarch Proclus) 20 days prior to Pascha around the 3rd hour of the day, and by imperial edict was immediately introduced into liturgical use. The Monophysite addition "Who wast crucified for us" introduced by the aforementioned Fuller, notwithstanding the extensive polemic against it, was accepted for a time even in Constantinople, for which the city, according to tradition, suffered many calamities (during a litany with the singing of such a Trisagion, an ash rain fell upon the city). This addition is retained today in the Armenian and Coptic [Egyptian] Churches, wherein in its justification it is argued that the entire hymn may be referred to the Son of God alone.[^1916] The Greek text of the prayer: *"Ἅγιος ὁ Θεός, ἅγιος Ἰσχυρός, ἅγιος ἀθάνατος, ἐλέησον ἡμᾶς"*—permits also the translation: "Holy God, Holy Mighty, Holy Immortal, have mercy on us" (such a translation would be closer to the original, which lacks the vocative case); then the prayer formula would consist of praise and petition, which also corresponds to that detail of the tradition that the boy heard from the Angels only the first part of the hymn, and the people added: "have mercy on us."

### "O Heavenly King" and "Most Holy Trinity"

Concerning the remaining prayers in the usual beginning, regarding the origin of the prayers **"Most Holy Trinity"** (*Presvyataya Troitse*), **"O Heavenly King"** (*Tsaryu Nebesny*), and **"Glory to Thee, our God, glory to Thee"** (*Slava Tebe Bozhe nash, slava Tebe*), nothing can be stated with certainty. The prayer **"O Heavenly King"** is rather a hymn than a prayer, and serves as a sticheron of Pentecost, from the service of which it was undoubtedly borrowed into the usual beginning, inasmuch as it appears therein later than in that festal office. Judging from the fact that this hymn is unknown to the 9th-century Typikon of Constantinople, it appeared *(Orig. p. 466)* no earlier than the flourishing of hymnographic activity in the 8th–9th centuries. According to the Horologion of the Sinai Library No. 865 of the 12th c. and the Ethiopian Horologion (14th c.), it is appointed as yet only as a troparion at the Third Hour (in the latter also at the Midnight Office). The prayer **"Most Holy Trinity"** the Coptic Horologion contains in the following simpler and therefore, perhaps, most ancient form: "Most Holy Trinity, have mercy on us (thrice). Lord, cleanse our sins; Lord, cleanse our iniquities; Lord, forgive our transgressions; Lord, visit the infirmities of Thy people and heal them, for Thy Name's sake." The Horologion places it not within the order of daily services, but within the order of evening prayers, in their midst, after **"Holy God"**.[^1917] The Ethiopian Horologion, however, places it in various services after the Trisagion, before the **"Our Father"**, as with us, but again in a different recension: "Most Holy Trinity, have compassion upon us; Most Holy Trinity, spare us; Most Holy Trinity, have mercy on us. Lord, forgive our sins; Lord, remit our iniquities and our transgressions; Lord, visit the infirm among the people and heal them, for the sake of Thy Holy Name."[^1918]

### "O come, let us worship"

**"O come, let us worship"** (*Priidite poklonimsya*) likewise did not immediately receive its present form. In the Typikon of the Monastery of St. Nicholas of Casole (in Calabria) of the 12th c. (according to a 12th–13th c. MS of Turin University), these verses have the following simpler form: "O come, let us worship and fall down before our King. O come, let us worship and fall down before Christ our God. O come, let us worship."[^1919] In the Typikon of the Georgian Monastery of Shio-Mgvime of the 13th c. (at the All-Night Vigil): "O come, let us worship and fall down before Christ our King and God"—twice, then the same without "King," and finally, only: "O come, let us worship" (a gradual abbreviation, just as today there is a gradual expansion).[^1920] In the Greek Typikon of the Moscow Rumyantsev Museum of the 13th c., our present first verse occurs twice, then our present third verse, and "O come, let us worship."[^1921] In the Greek Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 381 of the 14th c.—our present first verse, our present third verse without "Himself," and our present third and fourth verses at the All-Night Vigil (fol. 1). In the Slavic Typikon of the same library No. 328/383: "O come, let us worship" (perhaps unfinished) twice, and our present third verse (fol. 3). In the Ethiopian Horologion (14th c.) the form is as follows: "O come, let us worship, come let us pray to Christ our God. O come, let us worship, come let us pray to Christ our Savior," and again the second verse.[^1922]

*(Orig. p. 467)*

# THE NINTH HOUR

## Character of the Service

Sanctified by the death of the Savior and His descent into Hades, the ninth hour of the day opened unto us a new era of spiritual life; it was the pre-beginning of the Resurrection of Christ and of our own, and therefore with it the Church naturally begins the cycle of the daily services. But in view of the fact that at this hour there took place so sorrowful an event in the earthly life of the Savior as death, the liturgical commemoration of this hour cannot bear a festive character, and to this hour, as to the other sorrowful hours in the life of the Savior, the least festive type of service is assigned.

## The Order of the Hour in General

The Hours (*hōrai* / ὧραι, *horae*) are services specially instituted for the sanctification of specific hours noteworthy in some respect in the history of our salvation, in thanksgiving for the events that transpired in them; but since our salvation was accomplished through the sufferings of Christ, the individual moments of this great work were the most agonizing in the life of the Savior. Therefore the sentiment of thanksgiving for salvation cannot but be mingled with tears of compassion for Christ and sorrow over the sins that brought Him to the Cross. Hence the service of each of the Hours, of which the Orthodox Church, like the Roman Catholic, celebrates four—the First, Third, Sixth, and Ninth—bears a sorrowful, penitential character. This service for all the Hours possesses one and the same order (scheme), completely unlike the order of other services and, specifically, inferior to them in solemnity. The service of the Hour begins (if one excludes from it the usual beginning, as an accessory and contingent part) with Psalms, as do all services except the Liturgy. This is the most natural beginning for a church service, since in the Psalms the Christian Church has always possessed ready-made, specifically prayerful material, and moreover material not only of unsurpassed but of unattainable height, divinely inspired.[^1923] Moreover, it is natural for a Christian to begin prayer in the Old Testament spirit, in order to pass thence to New Testament prayer, experiencing thus in the small compass of one's soul that gradation of religious disposition which believing humanity experienced through the centuries. At each Hour three Psalms are appointed in honor of the Holy Trinity and in correspondence with the three hours of each "watch of the day," each of those divisions of the day which is sanctified by the service of the Hour. To the series of Psalms at the Hour, as at every service, there is joined *(Orig. p. 468)* as a conclusion to them a brief but rather complex *prayer formula*, half Old Testament and half Christian in content (as constituting a transition to purely Christian prayers), of a laudatory and prayerful character. It consists of the *Minor Doxology*, wherewith from ancient times in the Christian Church the singing of Psalms was concluded;[^1924] after this Christian glorification of God follows the glorification of God customary among the Psalmists, serving also as a refrain to certain Psalms: *“Alleluia”*, left untranslated for the purpose of reminding us of our connection with the Old Testament Church and in order to impart a special mysteriousness and ineffability to our doxology,[^1925] as well as on account of the impossibility of rendering this word into the Slavonic tongue by a single word.[^1926] Repeated thrice in honor of the Holy Trinity, Who has already been glorified also by the Minor Doxology, the *Alleluia* is concluded by joining to it the verse **"Glory to Thee, O God,"** breathing the artless simplicity of antiquity in the glorification of God. The thrice-repeated *Alleluia* with the single—as if in token of the consubstantiality of the Holy Trinity—**"Glory to Thee, O God"**[^1927]—the whole in its en*(Orig. p. 469)*tirety is repeated again thrice in honor of the Holy Trinity, and for greater concentration of attention.[^1928] After this altogether lengthy doxology there follows a comparatively brief petition (as throughout Orthodox worship generally praise predominates over petition): **"Lord, have mercy,"** likewise thrice. This prayer formula replaces at the Hours the litany (*ektenia*) that follows the preparatory Psalms at longer and more important services. After the preparatory Psalms at Lenten Hours (though not always and not at all of them) a *kathisma* is appointed—one, as at Vespers. Having glorified God through the lips of the Psalmist, the service of the Hours begins now to praise God with the Church's own Christian hymns and to pray to Him with its own prayers. But, in accordance with the modest solemnity of the Hour as a church service, there are assigned to it the simplest (and most ancient) of the various genres of hymnography, namely the troparion and kontakion (at Compline and Typika—even a kontakion alone). The *troparion* for Lenten Hours is adapted to the commemoration of the Hour, while for non-Lenten Hours it is adapted to the commemoration of the day (from the Menaion, Sunday Octoechos, or Triodion). Inasmuch as the troparion by its concept is not (like stichera and in contrast to the kontakion) an independent hymn, but only a refrain to a Psalm verse, at the Hours, as at the beginning of Matins (its primary and original place), *(Orig. p. 470)* it is sung not alone, but is framed with *verses*, to which it serves, as it were, as a refrain. These verses are selected likewise in accordance with the commemorations associated with the Hour, but with them only the Lenten troparion is sung (which is repeated thrice with two verses), since only the Lenten troparion is adapted to the commemorations of the Hour (for this reason in ancient monuments these troparia were termed prokeimena;[^1929] the current troparion of the First Hour, by reason of its brevity, completely possesses the appearance of a prokeimenon). On non-fasting days, however, in place of Psalm verses, the Minor Doxology serves as the refrains to the troparion; for this purpose the doxology is divided here (as always when it is divided) into two halves, each of which represents a complete thought: the first renders honor to the Holy Trinity, while the second directs our thought toward eternity; the first precedes the troparion, and the second precedes the Theotokion. (At Lenten Hours the doxology in its entirety precedes the singing of the troparion and concludes it). Originally, according to ancient Typika (as will be seen), the present Lenten troparia were always performed at the Hours (on the other hand, the Hours according to these Typika were not sung on Sundays, feasts, and even post-festal periods). The Theotokion of the Hour for Lenten and non-Lenten troparia is one and the same, and is adapted to the commemoration of the Hour. If the day has two commemorations, then at the Hours both troparia are read, with "Glory to the Father" inserted between them. If the day has three commemorations, then two troparia are still read at the Hours, but the second place is occupied in turn at the different Hours by the second and third troparia. With the Theotokion ends the first half of the Hour—the laudatory and psalmodic half; consequently, all insertions at the Hour—for example, paremias and instructional readings at Great Lenten Hours, special troparia, paremias, the Epistle, and the Gospel at the Royal Hours—are made after the Theotokion.

The second half of the Hour is predominantly prayerful. Its beginning, like the beginning of all services and of all their more or less significant parts, is psalmodic. But as a beginning here there is taken not a series of Psalms or an entire single Psalm, but only an excerpt of a Psalm. This excerpt bears no particular name in the Typikon, but one might designate it the *"verse of the Hour"*; at the First Hour this verse is **"Order my steps in Thy word..."** [Ps. 118:133], at the Third Hour—**"Blessed is the Lord God..."** [Ps. 67:20], and so forth. In its content the verse of the Hour represents the prayerful petition of the believer at a particular hour of the day, initiating with divinely inspired words the second, prayerful part of the Hour. Instead of the brief prayer of the first part of the Hour, consisting merely of the threefold **"Lord, have mercy,"** this part of the Hour possesses three (corresponding to the three Psalms of the first part) lengthy prayer texts, noticeably demarcated from one another: the Trisagion with the **"Our Father,"** the prayer **"Thou Who at all times,"** and the special prayer of the Hour. Between the first and second of these prayer texts, that is, after the **"Our Father,"** there are appointed at Lenten Hours once again *troparia*, adapted to the commemorations of the Hour: at the First Hour—one, and at the remaining Hours—three each, including the Theotokion, linked together by the two halves of the *Minor Doxology*, wherein the troparion of the First Hour and the Theotokion of the Sixth Hour change on Wednesday and Friday. These troparia are bor*(Orig. p. 471)*rowed from the services of the Triodion and Menaion cycles, and the majority of them serve also as sessional hymns (*kathismata*) of the Octoechos. On non-fasting days, however, in place of these troparia there is appointed the kontakion of the day (Sunday, Menaion, or Triodion, corresponding to the troparion of the first part of the Hour). The kontakion *introduces* into the purely prayerful content of the second part of the Hour a laudatory element and, together with the troparion, adapts the service of the Hour to the commemoration of the day (the troparion and kontakion, of all hymns, illuminate the celebrated event most concisely and fully). Just as in the first of the three prayer sections of this part of the Hour everything prior to the **"Our Father"** can be regarded as preparation for this prayer, so in the second prayer section the 40-fold **"Lord, have mercy"** is a preparation for the richly meaningful prayer **"Thou Who at all times,"** in which we penitently entreat Christ to accept the prayers of the Hour—and in general everything necessary for our body and soul. This prayer is attributed by a number of ancient Horologia, beginning with Sinai MS No. 865 of the 11th c., to St. Basil the Great. The prayer concludes, as it began, with **"Lord, have mercy"**—threefold, the Minor Doxology to the Holy Trinity, and in addition (in view of the end of the service), the hymn to the Most Holy Theotokos, the most concise and expressive: **"More honorable than the Cherubim."** Then, conscious of the insufficiency of our prayers, we entreat those of the priest with the words: **"In the Name of the Lord, Father, give the blessing."** In response to this the priest at the First and Ninth Hours prays: **"God be bountiful unto us, and bless us, and cause His face to shine upon us, and have mercy on us"** (an allusion to dawn and evening), and at the Third and Sixth Hours: **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers, O Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us,"** as if referring those entreating his prayers to more powerful intercessors. This exclamation serves at the same time as the dismissal for the Hour, which for the most part has no formal dismissal (in antiquity, as will be seen, the service of the Hour ended at this point). Inasmuch as the needs of the believer at different hours of the day vary, after the prayer common to all the Hours (**"Thou Who at all times"**) there follows also a special *prayer* proper to each Hour, which is the third prayer section of the second part of the Hour and together the *concluding prayer of the Hour*. After it, if with the Hour a series of services concludes (as is always the case with the First Hour), or if its service is sharply separated from the next (by being performed in the narthex), a *dismissal* is made, which for the Hour is naturally appointed as the small dismissal, differing from the great dismissal by the brevity both of the preparatory prayer formulas (the prayer to the Mother of God and Her glorification being omitted) and of the priestly dismissal formula (among the saints only the Most Holy Theotokos and the venerable [monastic] fathers are commemorated—the latter in view of the original assignment of the Hours exclusively for monasteries).[^1930] Unlike other services, the Hours (non-Lenten), as the least solemn service, consist of continuous reading without singing. According to the intention of the Typikon (which, however, is nowhere explicitly expressed), the troparia and kontakia of non-Lenten Hours should likewise be read, since their Theotokia are not adapted in tone (*ēchos*) to the troparion (to Lenten troparia they are adapted). Only in fasts, when the Hours, by virtue of their sorrowful tone, correspond more to the needs of the day *(Orig. p. 472)* and therefore are performed with greater solemnity, are the troparia at them sung (according to ancient Typika, on certain fasting days so-called sung Hours were performed, consisting entirely of singing).

## History of the Office of the Hour in General

The custom of sanctifying with prayer the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours passed to Christians from the Old Testament Church and was observed already by the Apostles.[^1931] In the 2nd century at these hours, presumably, they read the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"**), which was required to be recited thrice daily.[^1932] In the 3rd century, in addition to this prayer, at the sacred hours of the day they sang Psalms with the refrain of a single verse and *Alleluia*,[^1933] and specific commemorations were assigned to the Hours,[^1934] expressed, perhaps, only in corresponding reflections and discourses. In the 4th century in the Church of Jerusalem the service of the Hours possesses the identical composition with Matins and Vespers, namely, it consists of the singing of Psalms and antiphons until the arrival of the bishop, who, having arrived, entered the sanctuary, read a prayer, came out to the people, and blessed everyone individually.[^1935] From this latter order of the Hours there were subsequently formed the so-called sung Hours. Such Hours in the 9th–11th centuries in the same Church of Jerusalem consisted of the singing of a single Psalm, a troparion with Theotokia (the present ones), a prokeimenon, Old Testament readings, the verse of the Hour, and the Trisagion.[^1936] In the 13th century the sung Hours consisted of the singing of a single Psalm, the Great Litany, three antiphons, the Little Litany, a troparion with Theotokion, a reading, the Litany of Supplication, the Trisagion with **"Our Father,"** **"Lord, have mercy,"** the present blessing of the priest, and the prayer of the bowing of heads.[^1937] Both orders, to be sure, are appointed by those monuments wherein these orders are transmitted for Holy Week, wherein in the second monument for ordinary days Hours close to our present ones are appointed (in the first monument the Typikon is preserved only for Holy and Bright Weeks); but judging from Jerusalem practice of the 4th century, one may suppose that originally, where the so-called sung order of worship prevailed, the Hours in general approached in their structure Vespers and Matins, and already beginning with the 5th–6th centuries possessed an order close to the specimens presented, especially the first. But the sung order of the Hours did not enjoy universal dissemination, and alongside it there was formed another order of the Hours—the monastic order, which reduced, for example, in the 5th century among the Egyptian ascetics, according to the testimony of St. John Cassian, the entire service of the Hour to Psalms, interspersed with secret ("noetic" / mental) prayers, prostrations, and the lifting up of hands, wherein the number of Psalms was diverse in different places: either six at each Hour, or corresponding *(Orig. p. 473)* to the number of the Hour (at the Third—three, and so forth).[^1938] The Psalms were chanted by one of the brethren in an even melody without interruption, presumably in recitative, judging from the fact that John Cassian expresses it now as "were sung," now as "were recited." From the mutual influence of these two types—of which one imparted to the service of the Hour too great a solemnity, not distinguishing it from the other major services, while the other gave a very monotonous order to the service of the Hour—there arose the present type of the Hours, uniting elements of both. By the 8th–9th centuries we find this latter, present type already outlined in its details. Thus, according to the Greek Horologion of the Sinai Library (No. 863) of the 8th–9th c. according to the order of the Lavra of St. Sabbas, the Hours have the following composition. After the preparatory Psalms, whose number for different Hours varies—for the First: 8, the Third: 7, the Sixth: 6, the Ninth: 4—there follows *Alleluia* with two Psalm verses corresponding to the Hour (as today at Lenten Vespers and Matins); then the *troparion* of the Hour (the present one, excluding the First Hour), but before it and after it there is written in the Horologion its concluding phrase, which presumably served as its refrain (thus, for example, for the First Hour: "And direct. [O Christ,] True Light"). After this troparion there is appointed another—varying on different days of the week (for Sunday none is indicated, presumably because the Hours were not chanted then). After the troparia comes our present *verse of the Hour*, but divided into two parts, of which the first is repeated thrice (as today in Lent at the First Hour: **"Order my steps..."**). The service of the Hour concludes with the *Trisagion* (the **"Our Father"** is not mentioned). From this type of the Hours in this Horologion only the First Hour diverges by certain additions at the beginning and at the end.[^1939] Beginning with the 11th century, surviving Horologia provide a service of the Hours almost of the present structure, excluding the portion following the Trisagion. Thus, troparia after the Trisagion are not present in all Horologia up to the 15th century,[^1940] or have troparia not like the present ones, and diverse across different Horologia. Furthermore, the concluding (now preceding **"Thou Who at all times"**) **"Lord, have mercy"** is not appointed in all ancient Horologia 40 times. Thus, in one Horologion of the Moscow Typography Library of the 15th c., at the First Hour **"Lord, have mercy"** is appointed 30 times, at the Third and Sixth—40, and at the Ninth—12 times (at the end of the Typika and Matins likewise 12 times each).[^1941] In the Ethiopian Horologion at each Hour **"Lord, have mercy"** is appointed 41 times, while at the offices of the third hour of the night and at cockcrow 51 times each, at Vespers and Compline both 51 and 41, and at Midnight Office 51 and 51.[^1942] But in the Studite Typikon of the Moscow Typography Library of the 11th c. it is appointed 40 times, only at the Royal Hours 30 times.[^1943] According to the Typikon of the Roman Barberini Library of 1205, even at the Royal Hours it is 40 times.[^1944] Of the concluding exclamations of the priest: **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers..."** or **"God *(Orig. p. 474)* be bountiful unto us..."**—in the 13th century the first still possessed this form: "O Lord Jesus Christ our God, have mercy on us,"[^1945] while the second already possessed its present form,[^1946] but the first was rarely used; in the 14th century, however, both present formulas appear.[^1947] The prayer **"Thou Who at all times"** and the concluding prayer are completely lacking in certain Horologia (for example, one of the Moscow Typography Library of the 15th c.).[^1948] Other ancient Horologia indicate the prayer **"Thou Who at all times"** only for the First Hour among two or three others (for example, the Horologia of the Sinai Library No. 865 of the 12th c. and No. 870 of the 14th c.). Other ancient Horologia place the concluding prayers only in the Mesoria (inter-hours) (Sinai Library No. 868 of the 13th–14th c., Khludov No. 123 of the 14th c.), noting that if there is no Mesorion, then the prayers are said at the Hours (Khludov); others have very brief concluding prayers, like troparia (Vatopedi No. 350 of the 12th c.; for the Sixth Hour the prayer here is the present troparion: **"Thou Who at the sixth day and hour..."**); others have two prayers each, either both different from the present ones (Sinai No. 12 of the 13th c., Sophia 1152 of the 14th c.), or one of them the present one (Sinai No. 865 of the 12th c.); or still others indicate the present brief prayer of the Third Hour (**"O Master God..."**) also for the Sixth (Athos St. Athanasius Library No. 306 of the 13th–14th c.) and for the Ninth (Athos St. Panteleimon No. 7 of the 12th–13th c.);[^1949] yet Horologia no less ancient than these, such as Athos St. Andrew No. 12 of the 11th c. and No. 861 of the 13th c., already contain all the present prayers. As noted above, the ancient Typika (of the Studite type) designated the Hours only for weekdays, which is why in ancient Horologia nothing is said concerning the replacement of the troparia of the Hours by Menaion and Sunday troparia (otherwise termed dismissal troparia) and kontakia. The Hours were always sung with their own proper troparia. For the first time the Novgorod Sophia Horologion 1052 of the 14th c. speaks of such a replacement,[^1950] although all Typika of the Jerusalem type presuppose it for those days of the year that possessed their own troparion and kontakion (but of such days in the Menologia, the older they are, the fewer there were).[^1951]

*(Orig. p. 475)*
## The Order and Content of the Ninth Hour

The Ninth Hour is dedicated to the commemoration of the very death of the Saviour upon the Cross, since, according to the Evangelists Matthew and Mark, it was precisely around this hour (according to Matthew 'about the ninth hour', *περὶ δὲ τὴν ἐννάτην ὥραν*, according to Mark 'at the ninth hour', *τῇ ὥρᾳ τῇ ἐννάτῃ* [^1952]—not: *in* the ninth hour) that Christ made His penultimate cry upon the Cross: **"My God, My God, why hast Thou forsaken Me?"** (*Боже, Боже Мой, вскую Мя еси оставил*), which was *(Orig. p. 476)* immediately followed by the offering of vinegar to the Saviour to drink and (after an unspecified interval of time) by the final cry recorded only by the Evangelist Luke: **"Father, into Thy hands I commend My spirit"** (*Отче, в руце Твои предаю дух Мой*) [^1953], with which the Lord died. Beginning from this first moment, the *Instructional Notice* (*Uchitel'noe izvestie*, appended to the *Sluzhebnik*) exhorts the priest to commemorate at the Ninth Hour the final events of the life of Christ. But the office of the Ninth Hour dwells not so much upon this striking yet external aspect of the Saviour's final minutes, which is already embraced by the office of the Sixth Hour, as upon the inner and more essential aspect—the very act of Christ's death as the separation of soul from body and the descent of His soul into Hades (an aspect of the redemptive work of Christ unfolded with special depth by the Apostle Peter [^1954]), and subsequently upon the death of Christ as the definitive consummation of our salvation.

Among the Psalms of the Ninth Hour, the 83rd [LXX 83 / MT 84], **"How beloved are Thy dwellings, O Lord of hosts"** (*Коль возлюбленна селения Твоя*), which was presumably a song of the Jerusalem pilgrims [^1955] and portrays the beauty of God's habitation on Zion, is applicable to the soul's disposition of the Saviour upon the Cross, to the yearning of His soul from thence into the mansions of the Heavenly Father [^1956].

The following Psalm of the Ninth Hour, the 84th [LXX 84 / MT 85], **"Lord, Thou hast been favorable unto Thy land"** (*Благоволил еси Господи землю Твою*), which celebrates the blessing of God poured out upon the holy land following its repopulation by the returning captives (from Babylon), directs our thought from Christ dying for us unto the good things acquired by His death: preeminently, the forgiveness of sins and the cessation of God's wrath toward us. It is true that (according to the Psalm) although salvation has been accomplished, it is not yet fully realized (so long as salvation has not been appropriated by all mankind)—hence the prayer **"Turn us, O God of our salvation"** (literally from the Hebrew 'restore us'), and so forth. Nevertheless, the psalmist as it were overhears the divine decision concerning us, concerning peace with us, and in ecstasy contemplates how, instead of God's former enduring wrath upon us and His strict justice over the earth, now the earth is overshadowed only by God's saving mer*(Orig. p. 477)*cy and His truthful faithfulness (that is, to His former promises); these mercy and truth [faithfulness] are personified in the Psalm in the highly poetic portrait of two benevolent spirits meeting upon the earth and kissing one another [^1957]. The final Psalm of the Ninth Hour, the 85th [LXX 85 / MT 86], **"Bow down Thine ear, O Lord, and hear me"** (*Приклони Господи ухо Твое*), inscribed 'a prayer of David' (perhaps during the rebellion of Absalom, at any rate during some mortal peril), returns our thought from the work of Christ—from the redemption accomplished by Him upon the Cross, considered in the second Psalm of the Hour—unto His Person (with which the first Psalm was concerned), unto His condition upon the Cross; but in this condition the present Psalm directs attention unto another moment (in comparison with the first Psalm), namely, unto the state of Christ, of His soul, after death, that is, unto His descent into Hades. The Psalm continually speaks of the soul (in the Old Testament this word, however, for the most part signified 'life'), and unto the salvation of this Soul of the suffering Righteous One from enemies, from the lowest Hades itself, there is attached such significance that all nations shall worship God if God works upon His Servant a 'token' (*знамение*), a miracle of salvation (the Resurrection). The final words of the Psalm concerning the 'token' [v. 17: **"Work in me a token for good"**], in view of their special importance, are read twice (as is done elsewhere only at the introductory Psalms of Vespers and Matins—a remnant of the ancient chanting of Psalms with refrains).

In the subsequent hymns and prayers of the Ninth Hour, the sacred commemoration connected with this Hour is unfolded from other aspects. Thus, in the Lenten troparion of the Hour, **"Thou Who at the ninth hour"** (*Иже в девятый час*), prayer is offered [^1958] unto Christ Who died for us to mortify in us carnal 'mindings' (lusts); in the verses to the troparion (Ps. 118:169–171 [MT 119:169–171]) [^1959] the persecuted righteous one (Christ or the Christian) entreats the hearing of his prayer. The Theotokion **"Thou Who for our sake wast born"** (*Иже нас ради рождейся*), which is already of Sunday character, commemorating the Nativity, Death, and Resurrection of Christ, beseeches Him for lovingkindness and salvation through the intercessions of the Mother of God for a despairing people (*λαόν*, Slav. *люди*, *ἀπεγνωσμένον*). The verse of the Hour, borrowed from the Song of the Three Holy Youths [Dan. 3:34 LXX] [^1960], **"Deliver us not up utterly"** (*Не предаждь нас до конца*), prayerfully reminds God of His covenant with the chosen people (a covenant realized through the death of Christ). In the Lenten troparia after the Trisagion in Tone 8, **"When the thief beheld"** (*Видя разбойник*) and **"Between two thieves"** (*Посреде двою разбойнику*), and in their Theotokion **"The Lamb and Shepherd"** (*Агнца и пастыря*), the sacred commemorations of the Hour are augmented by moments of great importance not previously indicated in the office: *(Orig. p. 478)* the repentance of the thief and the suffering of the Mother of God beside the Cross. The prayer of the Hour, attributed (as is also the prayer of the Sixth Hour) to St. Basil the Great and addressed to Christ (as is elsewhere only the prayer of the First Hour; the prayer of the Third Hour is addressed to the Holy Trinity, and that of the Sixth to God the Father), entreats Him, by virtue of His sufferings for our sake, to cleanse our frequent and grievous sins, to spare, to save us, to deliver us from the devil, and to mortify our carnal mindings unto a righteous life, wherein mention is made also of eternal rest (evening) in the dwelling of all those who make merry with Christ, rejoicing with the gladness and joy (the Resurrection) of those who love Him.

## History of the Ninth Hour

Sanctified by prayer already by the Apostles [^1961], the Ninth Hour is subsequently indicated as an hour of prayer by a succession of 3rd-century writers and monuments, wherein as sacred commemorations for it there are specified the darkening of the sun at this hour during Christ's Crucifixion, the renewal of the covenant with the chosen people (cf. the present verse of the Hour), and the descent of Christ into Hades; moreover, in distinction from the other Hours, the comparative joyfulness of the commemorations associated with this Hour is noted [^1962]. Concerning the structure and content of the office of the Ninth Hour prior to the 8th century, no definite accounts have survived (Sylvia of Aquitaine [Egeria] in the 4th century speaks, as we have seen, only concerning the general order of the service of the Hour, as does also Canon 18 of the Council of Laodicea in the same century, according to which the service of the Ninth Hour must be the same as Vespers). In the Sinai Horologion No. 863 of the 8th–9th c., at the Ninth Hour all our present Psalms are indicated, and in addition to them the canticle: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers, and praised and glorified is Thy Name unto the ages"** [Dan. 3:26 LXX]; as verses to the *Alleluia* [^1963] there is appointed Psalm 22 [LXX 22 / MT 23], **"The Lord is my shepherd"** (*Господь пасет мя*), divided into two sections; the troparion is close to the present one: 'Thou Who, hanging upon the Tree at the ninth hour, didst yield Thy soul unto the Father', with a conclusion that served as a refrain: 'O Christ our God, glory [to Thee]'; the verse of the Hour is our present one, with the portion preceding 'for Abraham's sake' repeated thrice. In the Sinai Horologion No. 864 of the 9th c., clearly intended only for cell use, there are appended to the present Psalms at the Ninth Hour four others: 68, 112, 110, 120 [LXX 68, 112, 110, 120 / MT 69, 113, 111, 121], and following them stand an additional 12 Psalms, presumably of the nocturnal office; the troparion is different from the present one (*Ἐν γαστρὶ ὡς τὸν ἕν... / Έν γαστρί ώς τον έν άηνεγκάστης Τριάδος*); there is already also a Theotokion, but different from the present one: **"Thou Who didst contain the Word in Thy womb"** (*Иже во чреве Слово вместившая*). In subsequent Horologia appear both our present Psalms and our present troparion with its Theotokion. The troparia following the Trisagion in ancient Horologia appear from the very beginning almost in their present form; variation exists only regarding the second troparion: thus, the Athos St. Andrew Horologion No. 12 of the 11th c. and Sinai No. 867 of the 14th c., as well as the Greek Typika of the Moscow Rumyantsev Museum (Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35) and the Moscow Synodal Library No. 381, lack the second troparion. The concluding prayer, which in the earliest manuscripts is entirely absent, is in the Vatopedi Horologion No. 350 of the 11th c. *(Orig. p. 479)* brief and similar to a troparion: 'Thou Who hanging upon the Tree didst yield Thy soul', while in the Athos St. Panteleimon Horologion No. 71 of the 12th–13th c. and in the aforementioned Greek Typika—it is our present prayer of the Third Hour appointed also for both the Sixth and Ninth Hours; but in Sinai No. 865 of the 11th c. there appears already our present prayer and another special one. In the Athos St. Andrew Horologion No. 12 (11th c.), Sinai No. 866 (12th c.), and Vatopedi No. 352 (13th c.)—there is only our present prayer [^1964].

## The Typikon on the Ninth Hour Preceding Sunday

The celebration of the Ninth Hour on Saturday is presupposed by the Typikon to take place in the narthex, but it permits its celebration also in the temple; in the latter case the Ninth Hour, as merging more closely with Vespers, has no dismissal. Concerning the celebration of the Ninth Hour itself, the Typikon limits itself to the rubric: 'and the reader reads the Ninth Hour according to the custom' (the reading of the most important parts of Vespers and Matins is entrusted not to a reader, but to the superior or the ecclesiarch).

In the earliest of the currently known Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon, Moscow Rumyantsev Museum (Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35) of the 13th c., the description of Sunday services begins with Little Vespers. But the earliest Slavic manuscripts, for example, Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 of the 14th c., speak also of the Ninth Hour before Little Vespers: 'When the brethren are gathered in church, they chant the Ninth Hour' [^1965]. The present Old Believer Typikon (of the Edinoverie edition) speaks of the Ninth Hour like our own, only without the note for the case when the Ninth Hour is not celebrated in the narthex. The *Pomorian Answers* (*Pomorskie otvety*, an Old Believer polemical work of the early 18th c.) reproaches the Orthodox for celebrating the Ninth Hour together with Vespers, rather than before dinner prior to the Liturgy or immediately *(Orig. p. 480)* after it [^1966]. Such a celebration of the Ninth Hour was indeed prescribed by ancient Typika, but only for fasting days, so that the meal would occur as it were in the evening; such a direction is given, for example, by the earliest manuscript of the Studite Typikon [^1967].

Inasmuch as the Ninth Hour still belongs to the preceding liturgical day, the troparion and kontakion at it are read for the expiring day. This is evident from the direction of the Typikon concerning the Ninth Hour preceding St. Thomas Sunday: at it there are appointed the troparion **"Thou didst descend from on high"** (*С высоты снизшел еси*) and the kontakion **"Though Thou didst descend into the grave"** (*Аще и во гроб*) (and not **"When the tomb was sealed"** and **"With his curious right hand"**). By reason of this, there occurs within a single sequence of services at times a conjunction of the most diverse commemorations; thus, before Sunday Vespers of Meatfare Sunday there will be at the Ninth Hour a funeral troparion and kontakion, while before Sunday Vespers of the Sixth Sunday of Lent there will be the troparion **"That which was commanded secretly"** (*Повеленное тайно*), and the like.

# LITTLE VESPERS

## General Character of the Service

Vespers incorporated into the composition of the All-Night Vigil, and for that reason termed Great Vespers, is celebrated later than its customary time. It falls upon an hour designated rather for Compline than for Vespers. In order not to leave the customary hour of Vespers without its usual prayerful sanctification, Little Vespers is appointed for it. By omitting all litanies, as prayers that are most solemn and lengthen the service, the order of Little Vespers thereby, on the one hand, approaches the Hours and services homogeneous with them; on the other hand, through the singularity of its structure, through the predominance of changeable and festal portions of the service (stichera) over fixed ones, and given the comparative rarity of this service, Little Vespers receives a coloring no less festal than the Vigil itself.

## History

A rich development of festal ritual was required before the conception of such a service could arise. Certain manuscripts even of complete Jerusalem Typika of the 13th c., for example, the Georgian manuscript of the Shiomgvime Monastery, do not yet possess Little Vespers. But Greek Typika of the 13th c. (Moscow Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35) and 14th c. (Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 456 and 381) and Slavic Typika of the 14th c., such as Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 328/383, 329/384, and 332/385, already contain it in its present form. Consequently, the service arose in the 12th–13th centuries.

*(Orig. p. 481)*
## The Opening Exclamation and Psalm

Notwithstanding that Little Vespers is preceded by the Ninth Hour (which ordinarily is without a dismissal), it begins with the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** (*Благословен Бог наш*). In this respect Little Vespers is placed on a par with full Daily Vespers, which likewise has this exclamation notwithstanding that it is preceded by the Ninth Hour, whereas Great Lenten Vespers is joined to the Hours without an exclamation. The opening exclamation of Little Vespers, as of Daily and even Great Vespers, is pronounced before the Holy Doors (the exclamations of Matins and the Liturgy, in view of the greater solemnity of those services, are pronounced before the Holy Table). After the exclamation, there is immediately read **"Amen. O come, let us worship..."**, inasmuch as the 'customary beginning' took place before the Ninth Hour. The Introductory [^1968] Psalm at Little Vespers is appointed to be read not by the superior, as at full Vespers (and as the Six Psalms at Matins), but by the reader [^1969], yet with the requirement that the reading be more solemn and reverent than ordinary: 'in a quiet (slow) and gentle (contrite—*ἡσύχῳ καὶ ἠρεμαίᾳ* / *ήσύχω καί ήρεмаία*) voice'. Following the customary conclusion of the evening Psalm: **"Glory... both now... Alleluia, alleluia, alleluia, glory to Thee, O God"** (three times); in place of the Great Litany there is a threefold **"Lord, have mercy"** (corresponding to the petitions of the litany) and **"Glory... both now..."** (corresponding to its exclamation); consequently, the Introductory Psalm receives the conclusion usual for Psalms at the Hours.

## Stichera at "Lord, I Have Cried"

At **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Господи воззвах*) there are appointed only 4 stichera—a number that full Vespers never has (at it the smallest number of stichera is 6). An even number of stichera is appointed in view of their chanting by two choirs. Such a number of stichera (4) is also appropriate for Vespers because the stichera thereby begin with the verse: **"From the morning watch until night"** [Ps. 129:6 / MT 130:6], which is most suitable for the evening hour.

Four stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** are appointed in the Evergetis Typikon (12th c.), the Typikon of the Monastery of St. Nicholas of Casole (13th c., Turin Library), and others for an Alleluia Vespers [^1970], that is, for the most ferial and penitential service.

The stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** for Sunday Little Vespers are taken from Great Vespers; namely, the first three stichera serve as these (probably the most ancient). In order not to append to these stichera a heterogeneous sticheron (from the Anatolian stichera or the aposticha), the first sticheron is chanted twice to form the number of 4 (moreover, the repetition of stichera is a distinguishing mark of festal services).

*(Orig. p. 482)*
## The Little Dogmatika

As Theotokia at **"Lord, I have cried"** of Sunday Little Vespers there are appointed the so-called 'Little Dogmatika'. The Little Dogmatika, judging from their use only in such a comparatively late service as Little Vespers, must be of later origin than the Great Dogmatika, and therefore do not belong to St. John of Damascus. This is proven also by internal evidence—their theological content.

In content, the Little Dogmatika are somewhat more complex than the Great Dogmatika. Having, like the latter, as their primary subject the dogma of the incomprehensible union of the two natures in Christ, they dwell, for example, also upon the manner of the union of the two wills in Christ (the Dogmatikon of Tone 8). In them there is also greater refinement of expression and, in general, more theological detail. For example, among Old Testament types there are mentioned also the closed gate of Ezekiel's temple [Ezek. 44:2] (Tones 2 and 5); the Most Holy Virgin is termed the 'treasury of the union of the two natures'; 'Emmanuel... thus came forth from Her womb as He entered through hearing' (Tone 2). There is an adaptation to the commemoration of the Resurrection of Christ (Tones 5 and 7). There are more poetic apostrophes; for example: 'Today is a virginal festival, brethren; let creation leap for joy, let nature dance' (Tone 1); 'O most great mystery! Beholding wonders, I proclaim the Godhead' (Tone 2); 'O strange wonders and most glorious mysteries! Who will not marvel, giving ear unto this?' (Tone 5).

## The Prokeimenon

Following the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, Little Vespers has the hymn **"O Joyful Light"** (*Свете тихий* / *Phos hilaron*) and the prokeimenon of Great Vespers, **"The Lord is King"** (*Господь воцарися*, Ps. 92:1 / MT 93:1), which is sung not 4 1/2 times, but 2 1/2 times. After it follows **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Сподоби Господи*) and the aposticha.

## Aposticha

The first sticheron of the aposticha at Little Vespers is in common with Great Vespers—a Resurrection sticheron. The remaining 3 stichera are in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos. Such stichera were assigned to Little Vespers on the same ground on which in the entire Sunday service whole sections are dedicated to the Most Holy Theotokos, such as: the dismissal Theotokion of Vespers, the third canon at Matins, and the katavasia **"I shall open my mouth"** (*Отверзу уста моя*). Celebrating the Resurrection, we simultaneously perform the 'divine and all-honorable' festival of the Mother of God, and Sunday is dedicated, together with the Saviour, to the honoring of His Mother, just as other days of the week are dedicated to other saints. Occurring frequently, the celebration of Sunday cannot so completely absorb the prayerful attention of the believer as do rare feasts, such as the Twelve Great Feasts, the offices of which are therefore entirely occupied with the celebrated event (in those services there are therefore no Theotokia even at 'Both now'). Constituting the subject of independent glorification in the Sunday service alongside Her Son, the Mother of God in all the hymns dedicated to Her on Sunday is praised not in connection with the Resurrection (as, for example, in the Theotokia of the Paschal canon: **"Beholding Thy Son risen..."**), but from a gen*(Orig. p. 483)*eral point of view. So it is also in the present stichera. Specifically, the aposticha of Little Vespers now glorify the Mother of God, depicting a picture of the benefactions received by us through Her, now contain sorrowful, penitential supplications unto Her. At the same time, in the stichera of certain tones the first theme predominates, in others the second, but in the majority these themes alternate and are even combined within one and the same sticheron; the stichera of Tone 8 bear the most laudatory character (being composed of a series of 'Rejoice' acclamations); those of Tone 2 are the most sorrowful; at times (for example, Tone 5) the first sticheron is sorrowful, while the remainder are laudatory. The refrains to the stichera are those verses of Psalm 44 [LXX 44 / MT 45] that serve as refrains for the aposticha of the majority of feasts of the Mother of God (for example, the Nativity of the Theotokos): **"I shall remember Thy name..."** [v. 18 / MT 17]; **"Hear, O daughter, and see..."** [v. 11 / MT 10]; **"The rich among the people shall entreat Thy face..."** [v. 13 / MT 12]. As the sticheron at 'Glory... both now...', as in the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, there serves a 'Dogmatikon', consequently, a sticheron of more exalted content than those preceding it, and devoid of mournful themes.

## Troparion, Litany, and Dismissal

Following the aposticha at Little Vespers there immediately follows **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** (*Ныне отпущаеши* / *Nunc dimittis*), the Trisagion with the **"Our Father"**, the Sunday troparion with its Theotokion, and an abbreviated augmented litany (termed the 'Little Augmented Litany') consisting of 4 petitions: the initial (introductory), the petition for the Reigning House (without specifying the objects of petition), for the Holy Synod and the local Ruling Hierarch; while all the remaining petitions of the augmented litany are united in the concise: **"Again we pray for all the brethren and for all Christians"** (which is why the petition for the Holy Synod is without the addition: 'and for all our brethren in Christ'). This litany occupies at Little Vespers the same place as the augmented litany at Daily Vespers, that is, the very end of the service, as its most solemn part. An identical litany and in the same position is found in the initial, 'Royal' section of Matins.

From thence it was borrowed into Little Vespers, since this portion of Matins, as we shall see, is more ancient than Little Vespers. In turn, into that portion of Matins the litany was borrowed, probably, from the ancient *pannychis* (*παννυχίς*), an office that preceded Matins and for the most part consisted of: Psalm 50 [LXX 50 / MT 51], the canon, the Trisagion, and this litany, like the present service before the Paschal Matins, where there is likewise such a litany.

Little Vespers concludes with a Little Dismissal, like the Hours, Compline, and Midnight Office, and with the Polychronion (*Mnogolietie*, **"The Most Pious, Autocratic..."**), as does every Vespers.

## Alterations in the Order from the Concurrence of Commemorations

The rich content of Little Vespers becomes still more varied when there coincides on Sunday any Great Feast or even a Medium (Polyeleos) feast. In that case the festal aposticha from Great Vespers, at which they cannot be chanted on account of the Sunday stichera, are transferred unto Little Vespers. Thus, if a Great Feast of the Mother of God *(Orig. p. 484)* or the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*) coincides with Sunday, their aposticha can be heard only at Little Vespers.

In the case of such a concurrence (of a feast, or of a great or polyeleos saint) with Sunday, Little Vespers undergoes also a series of other alterations in its composition. These alterations concern the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, the aposticha, and the troparion. At Little Vespers—though not on Sunday—there may even be a paremia (if the Meeting of the Lord coincides with Cheesefare Saturday). All these deviations of Sunday Little Vespers from its usual (set forth above) composition will be discussed in the rubrics for feasts whose concurrence with Sunday induces such alterations.

# THE EVENING REFECTORY

## Liturgical Character of the Refectory

According to the commandment of the Apostle: **"Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God"** [1 Cor. 10:31] [^1971], the Orthodox Church regards even the partaking of food as divine worship, in essence not differing from other church services. Therefore, among the rank of the latter, she possesses a special 'order' (*chin*), or church office of the refectory, which is why meals in monasteries are served in a church (a special refectory temple, *trapeznaya tserkov'*).

The view of the taking of food, or the refectory, as divine worship goes back to the Old Testament Church. There, one of the kinds of sacrifice, namely the so-called peace offering, concluded with a sacred meal from its remnants, which was held in the temple itself, in its outer court, where for this purpose there were special rooms, and even kitchens [^1972]. And among the early Christians, scarcely any meal was not Eucharistic [^1973].

## Two Meals

The Typikon of the Church (as also monastic practice in general) recognizes only two meals: the first after the Liturgy, and the second after Vespers (for fasting days, however—only one, after Vespers). The midday meal, as the principal one, has a more elaborate liturgical order, which, in view of the fact that it includes a special rite over the prosphora of the Mother of God (*Panagia*), is called the *Order of the Panagia* (*chin o panagii*). The order of the evening meal constitutes an abbreviation of this Order of the Panagia, the order of the midday meal.

*(Orig. p. 485)*
## The Order of the Evening Meal

Upon entering the refectory (it being presupposed—directly from church following Vespers), there is read ('we say') the verse from Psalm 21:27 [LXX 21:27 / MT 22:26]: **"The poor shall eat and be satisfied, and they shall praise the Lord (preeminently for being satisfied) that seek Him; their hearts shall live unto the ages of ages"** (*Ядят убозии и насытятся...*), that is, the soul is enlivened forever by the Lord, as the body is for a time by nourishment. By this prayer the attention of those approaching the refectory is directed toward spiritual food, and thanksgiving is offered unto the Lord for the granting of the refectory even prior to partaking thereof. (This verse replaces the reading of the entire Psalm 144 [LXX 144 / MT 145] with the **"Our Father"**, appointed at the beginning of the midday meal). After the verse, the blessing of the priest is requested in the customary manner: **"Glory... both now... Lord, have mercy"** (three times), **"Bless"** (in the same manner the priest's blessing is requested for leaving the temple before the dismissal). **"And the priest blesses the refectory"**; in what words is not specified here (in Chapter 1 of the Typikon), but is indicated in the order of the midday meal ('On the Panagia') in Chapter 2 of the Typikon: **"O Christ God, bless the food and drink of Thy servants..."** (for the conclusion see the Sequenced Psalter [*Sledovannaya Psaltir'*]: **"for holy art Thou always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen"**).

Concerning the meal itself, it is remarked: **"we partake lightly of that which is set before us, that we may not be weighed down for the vigil."** Thanksgiving after the refectory is offered unto the Most Holy Trinity by the Little Doxology (as also at the midday meal), and then unto the Most Holy Theotokos by the troparia **"Thy womb hath become a holy table"** (*Бысть чрево Твое святая трапеза*, in place of **"It is truly meet"** at the midday meal) and **"More honorable"** (*Честнейшую*). Then (in place of Ps. 121 [LXX 121 / MT 122]: **"I was glad when they said unto me"**, appointed at the midday meal) there is read an excerpt from Psalm 91:5 and Psalm 4:8–10 [LXX 91:5; 4:8–10 / MT 92:4; 4:7–8]: **"Thou hast made us glad, O Lord..."** (*Возвеселил ны еси Господи...*), containing the glorification of God for being satisfied and a prayerful hope for peaceful sleep. All the remaining prayers of the Order of the Panagia, even those not directly pertaining to the Panagia, are omitted in this brief order of the refectory (for example, the Trisagion with the **"Our Father"**, the prayer **"We thank Thee, O Christ our God"**, the troparia **"O God of our fathers"** and **"By the prayers, O Lord, of all the saints"**); and immediately after **"Thou hast made us glad, O Lord"** the priest's blessing for dismissal is requested in the customary manner: **"Glory... both now... Lord, have mercy"** (three times), **"Bless"**. The dismissal likewise differs from the Order of the Panagia: (in place of: **"Blessed is God Who showeth mercy and nourisheth us"**)—**"God is with us by His grace and love toward mankind, always, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen"** (more suitable for nocturnal time; cf. at Great Compline **"God is with us"**). Thus, almost all the prayers of the evening refectory are distinct from the midday meal, but the very structure, the liturgical order of both is the same, excluding the rite of the elevation of the Panagia.

## History

In the earliest Typika, the order of the evening meal differed almost not at all from the midday meal; at it there likewise occurred the elevation of the dish *(Orig. p. 486)* with fragments (*ukrukhi*, corresponding to the Panagia). Thus in the Studite Typikon of Patriarch Athanasius of the 12th c. [^1974] and in the Typikon of the Pantokrator Monastery of 1136 [^1975]. The principal distinction of the evening refectory from the midday meal according to these Typika was that at the former, during the elevation of the fragments, there was proclaimed: **"Great is the name of the Holy Trinity"**, whereas at the latter: **"Most Holy Theotokos, help us."**

Yet already the Georgian Shiomgvime Typikon of Jerusalem type in a 13th-century manuscript possesses the order of the evening meal without the elevation of the Panagia.

'After Vespers they strike small bells (*kandya*?); the brethren gather quietly into the refectory and begin: **"The poor shall eat and be satisfied."** Arising, they say: **"The light of Thy countenance hath appeared upon us"** [Ps. 4:7 LXX / MT 4:6], and after **"Bless"** they depart unto their cells until Compline' [^1976].

The earliest Greek manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon (in Russia), Moscow Rumyantsev Museum, Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35 of the 13th c., has no order for either the midday or the evening meal. The earliest Slavic Jerusalem Typikon, Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 of the 14th c., has for the evening meal an order identical with the present one, with the following differences: **"The poor shall eat..."** is said thrice; after the meal the superior: **"By the prayers of the holy fathers..."**; the brethren: **"Glory to the Father..."**, and the elevation of the Panagia takes place according to the order of the midday meal; the dismissal is not **"God is with us..."**, but **"By the prayers of the holy fathers..."** [^1977].

*(Orig. p. 487)*

[^1927]: The Raskolniks [Old Believers] in this refrain pronounce *Alleluia* twice, replacing the third *Alleluia* with what is, in their opinion, its equivalent: **"Glory to Thee, O God."** Such a manner of chanting *Alleluia* in this refrain was prescribed by the Stoglav Council (1551), which designated the custom of tripling the *Alleluia* a "Latin heresy" (the Latins, according to the Council, "do not glorify the Trinity, but quadruple It," which the Council apparently deduced in some fashion from the *Filioque*). The Council appealed in defense of the twofold *Alleluia* to the *Life of St. Euphrosynus of Pskov* (†1481), to whom the Most Holy Theotokos Herself "revealed and forbade concerning the threefold *Alleluia*, commanding Orthodox Christians to say the twofold *Alleluia*" (*Stoglav*, ch. 42 / ed. D. Kozhanchikov, St. Petersburg, 1863, p. 148). In this *Life*, composed shortly before the Council, it is recounted moreover that St. Euphrosynus was in great sorrow concerning the mystery of the most holy *Alleluia* (that is, whether one ought to double or triple it) and, failing to find an answer to this in Russia, undertook a journey to Greece, where from a conversation with Patriarch Joseph and from the practice of the Hagia Sophia Cathedral and all the monasteries he became convinced of the necessity of doubling the *Alleluia* and introduced this custom into his monastery, on account of which he was denounced and persecuted by the people of Pskov headed by the Priest Job. The question of the *Alleluia* occupied other church figures of that era as well; even prior to St. Euphrosynus, St. Photius, Metropolitan of Moscow, in response to an inquiry from the people of Pskov on this point, expressed himself in an epistle of 1419 in favor of the threefold *Alleluia*; Bishop Gennadius of Novgorod did likewise in 1491. Consequently, in the 15th century the question was disputed, and ground for dispute was provided by diversity not only in practice, but also in liturgical books themselves. Whereas in some Greek and Slavic manuscripts of the 13th–16th centuries it was indicated to triple the *Alleluia* (for example, the Horologia of the Rumyantsev Museum, Sevastyanov Collection Nos. 496–497 of the 13th–16th c., the *Ordinance on Chanting the Great Compline [Mephemon]* in MS of the St. Sophia Library of the St. Petersburg Academy No. 1264); in others it was indicated to double it (for example, Slavic Typika of the Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 383 and 384 of the 14th–15th c., Potrebnik No. 259 of the 16th c.). Not only in Russia, but also in the East there was no uniformity in this regard, as witnessed by the observations of so impartial an observer in this matter as the monk Arseny Sukhanov in his *Proskynitarion* (*Pravoslavny Palestinsky Sbornik*, Vol. VII, fasc. 3, St. Petersburg, 1889, pp. 44, 205, 225). The presiding hierarch of the Stoglav Council himself, Metropolitan Macarius, in his *Great Menion Reader* (*Chet'yi Minei*) denounces those who double the *Alleluia* and symbolically grounds its threefold nature (April, fol. 816), on which basis scholars even suspect in the decree of the Stoglav Council, as well as in the remarks of the *Proskynitarion* concerning the twofold *Alleluia*, a later interpolation. (Concerning all this, see: Macarius, Metropolitan, *Istoriya russkoy Tserkvi* [History of the Russian Church], St. Petersburg, 1868, Vol. VIII, pp. 131ff.; Odintsov, N., *Poryadok obshchestvennago i chastnago bogosluzheniya v drevney Rossii do XVI v.* [The Order of Public and Private Worship in Ancient Russia up to the 16th Century], St. Petersburg, 1881, pp. 243–249; Dmitrievsky, A., "Bogosluzhenie v russkoy Tserkvi v pervye pyat' vekov" [Divine Service in the Russian Church in the First Five Centuries], in *Pravoslavny Sobesednik*, 1882, VII–VIII, pp. 359–362; Nikolsky, K., Archpriest, *Posobie k izucheniyu ustava bogosluzheniya pravoslavnoy tserkvi* [Manual for the Study of the Typikon of Divine Worship of the Orthodox Church], 7th ed., St. Petersburg, 1907, pp. 178–179). Originally and for a long period the *Alleluia*, following the example of the Old Testament church, was chanted, of course, neither twice nor thrice, but once; so it was in the 3rd century, according to the testimony of the *Testamentum* and Tertullian (see Introductory Chapter, p. 108). The dogma of the Trinity, brought to the forefront in theology by the heresies of the 3rd century, compelled Christians to make it the primary subject of their confession; hence, presumably, the extensive application of the number 3 in subsequent worship (the three-psalm group, the three-antiphon structure); hence could easily arise the tripling of the *Alleluia*. But direct witnesses to such a manner of chanting the *Alleluia* prior to the 13th century are lacking; on the contrary, wherever the chanting of the *Alleluia* is discussed, it is presented as if it were uttered once (Cassian, *De institutis coenobiorum*, II, 11; Horologion of the Sinai Library No. 863, 8th c.). Judging from the fact that in the Catholic Church the *Alleluia* is chanted once, twice, and thrice (*Missale Romanum*, Campidonae, 1823, p. 255; *Breviarium*, pp. 22, 29, and others), one may suppose that this threefold manner of its chanting existed. With three antiphons, on the first antiphon the *Alleluia* could be chanted once, on the second twice, and on the third thrice; thus in the order of monastic burial according to MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 396 of the 13th–14th c. (Dmitrievsky, A., *ibid.*). The single *Alleluia* is preserved among us in Psalm 136 and at the Liturgy. In the Ethiopian Horologion after the psalms our current refrain apparently possesses its simplest and most ancient form: "*Alleluia*. Glory to the Lord," 3 times (Turayev, B., *Chasoslov efiopskoy tserkvi*, p. 63).

[^1951]: In the Roman Catholic Church the Hours contain all the component parts of our Hours, but in a different order. As with us, the Hours there all possess a single scheme, excluding Prime (the First Hour), which also among us in antiquity differed in structure from the other Hours, as is evident from the Sinai Horologion of the 8th–9th c., concerning which discussion will follow in the explanation of Chapter II of the Typikon. Following the usual beginning in the Roman Catholic Hours there follows a hymn, proper to each Hour, corresponding to our troparion, ending with *Alleluia* (on Sunday) or another refrain, an "antiphon" (on weekdays). Next follow three sections from Psalm 118 [LXX 118 / MT 119] (which is divided among all the Hours into 11 sections; two of them are read at Prime together with other psalms, and three at each of the remaining Hours without other psalms); these three sections of Psalm 118 at each of the Roman Catholic Hours impart to it the same three-psalm type that our Hours possess (Psalm 118 was chosen for the Hours, presumably, because it speaks of ceaseless meditation upon the Law of God, of glorifying God seven times a day, and the like). After the three sections of Psalm 118, ending with *Alleluia* or another antiphon, there follow two brief excerpts (*capitula*) from the New and Old Testaments (from the Epistles and the Prophets) with concluding refrains (*responsoria*), occasionally changing according to feasts (corresponding to our "verse of the Hour," as well as to paremias and prokeimena). After these *capitula* follows *Kyrie eleison, Christe eleison, Kyrie eleison* (corresponding to our "Lord, have mercy" 40 times) and the *Pater noster* ["Our Father"], again with responsories (the first of which consists of the final words of the Lord's Prayer: "and lead us not into temptation...", corresponding to our exclamation). Then follows the collect/prayer proper to the day (among us, to the Hour), which is preceded and concluded by exclamatory responsories: "The Lord be with you" (*Dominus vobiscum*), with the response: "And with thy spirit. Let us pray (*Oremus*)." After the prayer there is likewise a responsory: "Let us bless the Lord (*Benedicamus Domino*)." The exclamation "The Lord be with you," which is also pronounced by a deacon officiating at the Hours in the absence of a priest, and even in his presence with his permission, is replaced, if a lector reads the Hours, by another: "O Lord, hear my prayer, and let my cry come unto Thee" (*Breviarium Romanum*, pp. 52, 62, 43ff., 24ff.). These responsories surrounding the concluding prayer of the Hour clearly correspond to our priestly exclamation after the Prayer "Thou Who at all times." This is the Roman order of the Hours; the Ambrosian (Milanese) and Mozarabic orders are somewhat longer (Seredinsky, T., Priest, *O bogosluzhenii zapadnoy tserkvi* [On the Divine Worship of the Western Church], St. Petersburg, 1849, Article 2, p. 6). The proximity of the Roman Catholic Hours to our order of the Hours clearly bears witness, of course, to the fact that the order of the Hours in its fundamental structure was formed both there and here during the period of living fraternal communion between the two Churches. Closely related to both the Roman Catholic and Orthodox orders of the Hours are the Hours of the Eastern Monophysites, for example, the Copts. According to the ancient Horologion of the Copts, the order of the Hour consists of an introductory troparion (cf. the hymn of the Hour among the Roman Catholics), 12 psalms (a vestige of the "Order of the 12 Psalms"), a Gospel reading corresponding to the commemoration of the Hour (cf. *capitula* and paremias), troparia with verses and Theotokia identical to our first troparia, a verse of the Hour identical to ours, other troparia largely coinciding with our second troparia (after the Trisagion), the Symbol of Faith [Creed], the Trisagion with "Our Father," and a concluding prayer proper to the Hour (Archimandrite Porphyrius, *Doctrine, Divine Service... of the Copts*, pp. 57ff.). Still closer to our order are the Hours according to the Ethiopian Horologion of the Coptic Church (14th c.): after the usual beginning, 12 psalms, the Gospel, our troparia of the Hour and Theotokia, the verse of the Hour, the Trisagion, *Alleluia*, troparia largely coinciding with our troparia after the Trisagion, the Creed, *Kyrie eleison* 41 times, a brief penitential prayer beginning "Holy, Holy, Holy...", a dismissal troparion, the prayer "Thou Who at all times," *Kyrie eleison* 41 times, and a prayer of blessing common to all the Hours—with the invocation of a succession of saints by name, similar to our litany prayer **"Save, O God, Thy people"** (Turayev, B., *Chasoslov efiopskoy tserkvi*, pp. 47ff., 33ff., 39ff.). In both Horologia there is a special office similar to the conclusion of our Matins and the First Hour; but this office is not called an Hour.
