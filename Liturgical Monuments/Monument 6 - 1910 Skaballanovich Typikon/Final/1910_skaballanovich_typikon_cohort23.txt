#### a) The Akoimetoi (Unsleeping Ones) and b) The Stoudios Monastery

The most immediate influence upon the development of divine worship must have been exerted by the monasteries of such great cities as Alexandria, [^1183] Jerusalem, Antioch, [^1184] Constantinople, and Rome. Above, the significance was noted which the monasteries of Jerusalem and its environs had in the history of divine worship, laying the foundation for a distinctive form of the ecclesiastical Typikon. An identical influence upon the latter could not fail to be exerted also by the monasteries of another comparable center of Christian life—Constantinople, which had only just become the capital of the entire world. Constantinople created a distinctive type of monastery and for a long time, until the first blows inflicted upon it by the Crusaders, became the lawgiver in the sphere of divine worship, both cathedral-parochial and monastic. Yet information on this subject from the epoch of the IV–V centuries is limited unto three brief accounts, which are nonetheless exceedingly characteristic and momentous.

St. Alexander founded first in Mesopotamia, then in Antioch, and finally ca. 420 AD in Constantinople, near the Church of St. Menas, a monastery which immediately attracted up to 300 monks of diverse nations (Romans, Greeks, and Syrians) by its distinctive organization: in it unceasing divine worship was instituted throughout the twenty-four hours of day and night, for which purpose the monks were divided into three (according unto others, into twenty-four) shifts. [^1185] Such an institution, on account of which the monastery received the flattering designation of the "Akoimetoi" (*ἀκοιμήτων*, the "Unsleeping Ones"), was clearly calculated to satisfy the religious needs both of all strata of the surrounding populace—who did not always have the same hour at their disposal for prayer—and of every individual at whatever time there might arise a need for common prayer, or an inclination and disposition toward it. It is not surprising that such a type of monastery immediately attained widespread expansion: the successor of St. Alexander in the hegumenate, St. Marcellus († 485), founded an identical monastery in Bithynia; [^1186] identical monasteries are mentioned in the V century in the Chalcedonian diocese and other places of the Greek empire. [^1187]

The Constantinopolitan community of the "Akoimetoi" acquired exceptional significance in view of the fact that it linked its destiny with the monastery that subsequently became the most renowned—the Stoudios Monastery. According unto the tradition preserved in the Stoudios Monastery and recorded in the *Life of St. Theodore *(Orig. p. 234)* the Studite* († 826, the *Life* was composed by a disciple of the venerable father, Michael the Studite, or by someone else in the X century [^1188]), "there came from the city of Rome a man of noble lineage and wealthy, by name Stoudios—which in our tongue would be Euprepios—who had already attained the rank of patrician and consul; having settled in our city (Constantinople), he founded this glorious church unto the great Forerunner," which was the principal church of the Stoudios Monastery. Among the twelve senators

=== LEAF p222 ===
who migrated from Rome unto Constantinople with Emperor Constantine the Great, a certain Stoudios is mentioned. [^1189] Yet hardly was this Stoudios the founder of the monastery. The Byzantine chronicler Theophanes (IX c.) and after him Kedrenos (XII c.) place under the 6th year of Emperor Leo the Great (457–474), consequently under the year 463: "in the same year Stoudios also built the church of the Forerunner and settled in it (*ἐν αὐτῷ κατέστησε*) monks from the monastery of the Akoimetoi ('Unsleeping Ones')." [^1190] According unto Kodinos (XIV c.), "the patrician Stoudios founded under Emperor Leo Marcellus the Stoudios Monastery, endowed it with extensive estates, and settled in it 1,000 monks." [^1191] According unto the Suda (X c.), "the Stoudios Monastery, before being converted into a monastery, was a parish church." [^1192] These accounts leave no doubt, at least, that the community of the Akoimetoi which appeared in Constantinople in the V century entered into a close relationship with the Stoudios Monastery that arose at that same time.

#### c) The Constantinopolitan Spoudaioi

It is possible that in this same epoch there appeared in Constantinople also a community of Spoudaioi monks. In an ancient synaxarion (of Sirmond) under June 7 is indicated the "memory of our venerable fathers Anthimus the Presbyter and Stephen, of the (monastery of the) Spoudaioi, which is near Florentius'." [^1193] The latter name designates an almshouse founded under Emperor Arcadius (395–408) by a certain Florentius. [^1194] This Anthimus must be none other than the admirer mentioned in the *Life of St. Auxentius* († ca. 470), the Bithynian ascetic, concerning whom the biographer of St. Auxentius, his disciple (George), recounts that he, "being first a deacon and later a presbyter in Constantinople, adorned and improved psalmody, having arranged at the vigils two choirs—a male and a female *(Orig. p. 235)* choir"; these vigils St. Auxentius also loved to visit. [^1195] In that same epoch in Constantinople, Theodore Lector (VI c.) indicates as a champion of the Council of Chalcedon, a "composer of troparia," and an organizer of vigils, likewise a certain Anthimus; all these consequently represent one and the same person. If so, then Anthimus, the first Constantinopolitan hymnographer, may be recognized as the founder of the Constantinopolitan community of Spoudaioi frequently mentioned in later times. From these accounts the true character of this community is disclosed: the Spoudaioi set as their primary aim the arrangement of magnificent divine worship and were originally chanters rather than monks; [^1196] such a character was possessed also by the Jerusalem Spoudaioi (the zealous guardians of divine worship in the Church of the Resurrection) until Patriarch Elias formed a monastery out of them. A tradition bearing a legendary character assigns the foundation of the monastery of *Spoude* (*Σπουδή*) unto the reign of Emperor Leo the Isaurian (716–741). [^1197]

=== LEAF p223 ===
Concerning divine worship in these Constantinopolitan monasteries of the IV–V centuries nothing is known, but one may think that, separated from desert monasticism and called to satisfy the spiritual needs of the urban population as well, these monasteries, even more than those of Jerusalem, had to take into account the orders of secular churches, which had by that time become so well developed. This must especially be said concerning the community of the Spoudaioi.

### The Church Year in the IV–V Centuries

Having done so much for the development of the order of the daily church services, the IV–V centuries did no less in establishing the festal ritual. In this regard: a) the conception of the sanctity of the feast was elevated; b) the number of feasts and fasts was increased by not less than twofold; c) the foundation was laid for the universal ecclesiastical veneration of saints, that is to say, the annual menologion. Yet very little was accomplished at that time toward the formation of a specifically festal order of divine worship: a feast was distinguished only by more prolonged, frequent divine worship, while the content and composition of festal worship still differed little from the ordinary. To create a distinctive festal worship remained a task to be accomplished by the VI–VIII centuries.

### Sunday

At the foundation of feasts, as previously, lay the Sunday day (the Day of the Lord), and if the feast now came to be venerated more than before, this occurred first of all with Sunday. Here the Church was indebted in many respects unto the secular government, which guaranteed the rest and sanctity of Sunday by legislative means. The beginning was laid by Constantine the Great. According unto the testimony of Eusebius, he *(Orig. p. 236)* "appointed for prayer the Lord's Day. Therefore the emperor inspired all Roman subjects with a disposition on the days eponymous with the Savior (the Lord's days, that is to say, Sundays) to cease from their occupations, and in like manner to venerate Fridays, [^1198]—it seemeth, in commemoration of those events which on these days were accomplished by the common Savior. As for the Sunday day, sometimes called also the day of light and of the sun, he inclined all his soldiers unto the zealous observance thereof; and unto those among them who had become partakers of the divine faith, he gave on that day freedom from military service, so that without hindrance they might keep vigil in God's church and that no one might impede them at that time from offering their prayers. And unto the other soldiers, who had not yet received the divine law, he prescribed by a special statute on Sundays to assemble in open plazas in the suburbs of the city and there, at a given signal and all together, to uplift unto God a prayer memorized beforehand." [^1199] In 321 AD Constantine the Great issued an edict:

> "All judges, the urban populace, and artisans of every

=== LEAF p224 ===
> manner in the venerable day of the sun let them rest. Only in villages may agricultural laborers freely and without hindrance work, since it frequently happeneth that on another day it may be less commodious to entrust grain unto the furrow or vines unto the trench, lest, losing the favorable opportunity, they be deprived of the bountiful season sent down by heavenly Providence." [^1200]

According unto an edict of Emperor Valentinian I (364–375), on Sunday "none of the Christians must be subjected unto the exaction of debts." [^1201] Emperor Theodosius the Great prohibited public spectacles on Sunday. [^1202] According unto an edict of 386 AD of Emperors Valentinian II and Theodosius the Great, on Sunday "the conduct of all lawsuits, the transaction of trade, and the execution of contracts must cease; there must be no judicial proceedings either through arbiters, or through appointed and elected judges"; violation of this law was punished as sacrilege (*sacrilegus*). [^1203] By an edict of Theodosius the Great of 389 AD the operation of this statute was extended unto Holy Week and Bright Week. [^1204] These laws, understandably, heightened the halo of sanctity surrounding the Sunday day; among church writers of this epoch counsels are heard unto Christians to take an example from the pagans and Jews in their reverent attitude toward feasts, [^1205] while the thought that for a Christian every day is a feast is expressed more rarely. [^1206] "The week hath seven days," said St. John Chrysostom; "these seven days God hath divided with us not *(Orig. p. 237)* in such a manner as to take more for Himself and give us less, nor even divided them equally—but He hath given thee six days, and reserved for Himself but one." [^1207] "Let us establish an immutable statute for ourselves, for our wives, and for our children, to dedicate one day in the week entirely unto listening and remembering that which we have heard (in church)." [^1208] By this the thought was advanced that properly the entire Sunday day must be consecrated unto divine worship. Everything worldly—labor as well as entertainment—profaneth it; the latter even more: according unto Blessed Augustine, on a feast day "it is better to plow than to dance." [^1209] Such views could only reinforce the ancient custom of especially solemn divine worship on Sundays. The Council of Sardica (347 AD) excommunicateth anyone "who, dwelling in a city, on three Lord's days during three weeks doth not attend the church assembly." [^1210] We have seen that in the Jerusalem Church Matins, which always began, as in other churches, [^1211] long before dawn (for which reason it is frequently called a nocturnal service, and among monks—*pannychis*, *vigilia* [^1212]), on Sunday commenced so deep in the night that it could be termed a vigil [^1213]). Thus it was in other churches as well. According unto Socrates and Sozomen, the Arians in Constantinople "on all significant feasts, as well as on the first and last day of the week (Socrates: 'on the feast days of each week, that is to say, on Saturday and Sunday'), assembled at night in the public porticoes, and dividing into

=== LEAF p225 ===
choirs, chanted hymns in two choirs, adapting the refrains unto their doctrine; and in the morning, chanting them (the refrains) publicly, withdrew unto the appointed places and held church assemblies there." In opposition unto these Arian vigils, St. John Chrysostom "began to incite the people unto similar psalmody" (Socrates: "countering them with the psalmody of his own people, he commanded that the confessors of the Consubstantial Trinity should likewise exercise themselves in the singing of nocturnal hymns"); "the Orthodox assemblies soon became more numerous and magnificent" than the Arian ones, "inasmuch as they were preceded by silver crosses accompanied by burning lanterns" [^1214] (litany processions).

At this time also, apparently, there definitively took root in the Eastern churches the custom of commencing festal celebration from eventide. In the work of Pseudo-Athanasius, *Questions unto Antiochus the Duke*, the question is already posed: "Why did God prescribe for us to begin the Lord's Day from Saturday evening?" Having observed that "many now argue vehemently whether day should precede night or vice versa," and that the Law commanded the Jews to begin the Sabbath from the evening "lest they be able to say that Christ arose on the Sabbath," the author perceiveth the foundation for the Christian celebration of Sunday from the evening not in this prescription of the Law, but in the fact that "God brought the nations forth from the darkness *(Orig. p. 238)* of ignorance and from the shadow of the Law into the light of divine knowledge and the Gospel; for this reason He very expediently prescribed to begin the day of His Resurrection from eventide and to conclude it with the light; for it would be exceedingly unfitting and improper to begin the day of Christ—the true Lamp—from the light and conclude it at night and in darkness." [^1215] St. John Chrysostom saith: "Earthly celebrations often cease already in the middle of the day, whereas heavenly ones are not so—they continue unceasingly." [^1216]

### Saturday

Saturday, which laid claim in the III century as well unto an exceptional position in the ranks of the days of the week, [^1217] was now in diverse places in the East venerated almost equally with Sunday. The *Apostolic Constitutions* seem to make no distinction regarding celebration between these two days, considering the attendance of divine worship on Saturday just as obligatory for a Christian as on Sunday, [^1218] commanding "on every Sabbath, except one (Great and Holy Saturday), and on every Lord's Day, assembling together, rejoice," [^1219] to feast them ("Celebrate the Sabbath and the Lord's Day, for the former is the memorial of the creation, and the latter of the Resurrection"), [^1220]—finally, prohibiting work on Saturday: "I Peter and I Paul decree: let servants work five days, but on the Sabbath and on the Lord's Day let them attend church for the sake of instruction in piety; for the Sabbath, we have said, hath the figure of creation, and the Lord's Day—of the Resurrection." [^1221] And this occurred not solely in that Church where the *Apostolic Constitutions* arose. Socrates, as we have seen, regardeth Saturday and Sunday as the festal days of the week and giveth it to be understood that

=== LEAF p226 ===
the divine worship on them was of equal solemnity in Constantinople. [^1222] Thus it was, besides Constantinople, in Antioch, Pontus, Cappadocia, Cyprus, and Egypt. [^1223] Yet, apparently, the celebration of Saturday for the most part was reduced unto the celebration of the Liturgy on that day, wherever it was not celebrated daily: "whereas all churches throughout the world celebrate the Mysteries on the Sabbath day of each week, the Alexandrians and Romans on the basis of a certain ancient tradition do not wish to do this; while the neighbors of the Alexandrians, the Egyptians and the inhabitants of the Thebaid, although they hold assemblies on Saturday, partake of the Mysteries after having dined in the evening." [^1224] This latter restriction is charac*(Orig. p. 239)*teristic, inasmuch as it showeth that on Saturday the Liturgy was not considered, after all, so indispensable as on Sunday, and being celebrated in the evening, pertained rather unto the Sunday day, perhaps even combining with the Sunday Eve Vespers, as with us on Paramonies (Eves). It is possible that in general Saturday was venerated not so much from Old Testament motives as on account of its significance in the life of the Savior, unto which Cassian clearly pointeth, [^1225] and as the eve of Sunday. [^1226] If so, then: a) its full festive observance in certain very few Churches, predominantly far-eastern ones (such as Antioch), must be regarded as an extreme, an excessive fervor [^1227] condemned by the Council of Laodicea: "Christians ought not to Judaize and rest (*σχολάζειν*) on the Sabbath, but must labor (*ἐργάζεσθαι*) on that day, and honor rather the Lord's Day, resting upon it if they can, as Christians; but if they be found Judaizing, let them be anathema from Christ"; [^1228] b) from this is likewise understandable the custom of fasting on Saturday that arose in this epoch among Western Christians, mentioned (disapprovingly) by Cassian: [^1229] the fast was an identical form of honoring Saturday as the day marked by Christ's burial, just as on the East was the Liturgy celebrated on that day.

### Wednesday and Friday

Of the remaining days of the week the more sacred, as previously, were Wednesday and Friday. At this time we first discover a rationale for the fast on these days: "Fast on Wednesday and Friday, for on Wednesday sentence was pronounced against the Lord, and on Friday because on it the Lord suffered death upon the cross under Pontius Pilate." [^1230] Concerning the fasting regimen on these days it is known only that the fast concluded at the ninth hour, that is to say, they did not eat until that hour; concerning the kind of food on these days nothing is known; judging by the comparison of these fasts with the Forty-Day Fast (Great Lent), [^1231] one may think that it was identical unto that in the Forty-Day Fast (see below). "This fast (of Wednesday and Friday)," saith St. Epiphanius of Cyprus, "is observed throughout the entire year and continueth until the ninth hour. The fifty days of Pentecost are excepted, when there occurreth neither genuflection nor fasting... It is equally unseemly to fast on Theophany, when our Lord was born in the flesh, even though it occur on a Wednesday or Friday." [^1232] Consequently, the fast of Wednesday and Friday was omitted during Pentecost and on the principal feasts. Of these two

=== LEAF p227 ===
weekly fasts Friday was considered more important, as in the III century: [^1233] on Friday Constantine *(Orig. p. 240)* the Great released soldiers from duty and abolished judicial sessions on that day, just as on Sunday. [^1234] In addition unto fasting, these days were honored also by divine worship—in certain places with the Liturgy, [^1235] in others with a special service that replaced the Liturgy, without the Eucharist: "the Alexandrians on Wednesday and on the so-called day of preparation (*παρασκευήν*—Friday) read the Scriptures, and the teachers expound them; on these days all things are celebrated which are customary, except for the Mysteries." [^1236] Probably this service—the Liturgy without the Eucharist—gave origin unto the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, which in certain ancient monuments is ascribed unto St. Mark and the Apostle Peter who preached in Alexandria, as well as unto St. Athanasius the Great. [^1237]

### The Forty-Day Fast (Great Lent): Lengthening of the Pre-Paschal Fast

From the IV century we possess the first clear testimonies concerning the forty-day duration of the pre-Paschal fast, which consequently only at that time (at the end of the III century) became the Forty-Day Fast (*Quadragesima*, *Tessarakoste*) in the proper sense. The first such testimony belongeth unto the Council of Nicaea of 325 AD, which requireth that local synods take place: "one before the Forty-Day Fast, so that upon the cessation of all animosity a pure gift may be offered unto God, and the other around the autumn season." [^1238] According unto Eusebius of Caesarea, "we celebrate Pascha by taking upon ourselves a forty-day ascetical labor for preparation unto it"; "before Pascha for six weeks we fortify ourselves with a forty-day fast." [^1239] St. Athanasius the Great repeatedly mentioneth the Forty-Day Fast, among other places in his Festal Letter of 330 AD, [^1240] as doth also St. Cyril of Jerusalem († 386). [^1241] The Council of Laodicea ca. 360 AD speaketh of it, [^1242] as doth St. Epiphanius of Cyprus ca. 375 AD. [^1243] In the West testimonies concerning it begin later: the first in St. Ambrose of Milan († 397). [^1244]

Several passing remarks among writers of that era shed light upon the very process of the expansion of the former one-week pre-Paschal fast into a forty-day fast. Socrates saith: "The fast before Pascha is observed differently in different places: namely, in Rome before Pascha they fast continuously for three weeks, except on Saturday and the Lord's Day; while in Illyria, throughout all Greece and Alexandria they observe a fast of six weeks before Pascha and call it the Forty-Day Fast; while others begin to fast seven weeks before the feast." [^1245] A younger contemporary of Socrates, Sozomen, in his *Ecclesiastical History*, *(Orig. p. 241)* heavily dependent upon that of Socrates, repeateth this passage from the book of his predecessor, but with characteristic additions and modifications: "The so-called Forty-Day Fast preceding the feast of Pascha,

=== LEAF p228 ===
wherein the people fast, some count as six weeks, for example, the Illyrians and the Christians dwelling in the West, all Libya, and Egypt with Palestine; others as seven, as the inhabitants of Constantinople and the surrounding regions as far as Phoenicia; during the course of these six or more weeks some fast for three weeks with intervals, others for three weeks consecutively before the feast, while yet others for only two, as, for example, the followers of Montanus." [^1246] Both testimonies belong unto that time (the V century) long before which a whole series of witnesses, as we have seen, establisheth the universal expansion of the Forty-Day Fast in the Christian world. Consequently even at that time the Forty-Day Fast not only was far from being definitively defined, but had not yet received universal recognition. In diverse places instead thereof there existed a fast occupying in duration precisely the middle between the ancient short pre-Paschal fast of one to six days and the Forty-Day Fast—namely, a twenty-day fast. In this regard it is characteristic that this latter kind of fast took place in the West, where, as we have seen, testimonies concerning the Forty-Day Fast are noticeably late in comparison with the East. One may think that everywhere a fast of shorter duration mediated the transition from the weekly fast unto the forty-day fast. Significant also is the fact that Sozomen no longer nameth the West, and Rome in particular, as adherents of the three-week fast, although the practice of such a fast was still known unto him. It is possible that by his time the Forty-Day Fast had pushed back still further the former, more lenient practice. [^1247]

Yet Socrates and Sozomen have preserved for us only distant echoes of the expansion of the Forty-Day Fast, which by their time was almost accomplished. Meanwhile there hath come down unto us a document that was, so to speak, a living witness of the gradual establishment of the forty-day fast in place of the former shorter one in one of the most prominent Churches of antiquity: the so-called *Festal Letters* of St. Athanasius of Alexandria, a contemporary of that very Council of Nicaea, one of whose canons is regarded as the first authentic testimony in time concerning the Forty-Day Fast. In the first of these epistles, of the year 329, the Forty-Day Fast is not mentioned at all, and the beginning of the "holy fast" is assigned unto the Monday of Holy Week. Inasmuch as the fourth and fifth epistles of 332–333 likewise *(Orig. p. 242)* presuppose this order, whereas in the two preceding ones (the 2nd and 3rd) the Forty-Day Fast also hath a place beside or before Holy Week, one cannot conclude from the first epistle that the Forty-Day Fast was not yet known in Egypt at that time. The fluctuation showeth only that the Church there was in a stage of transition from the former order unto the new. Complete certainty is imparted unto this supposition by the epistle of St. Athanasius unto Serapion, appended unto the 11th Festal Letter and written in the year 340 from Rome. Unto the Bishop of Thmuis, unto whom during the absence of the Alexandrian

=== LEAF p229 ===
Archbishop the oversight of the entire Egyptian Church was entrusted, in this epistle it is proposed "to proclaim unto the brethren the forty-day fast and to add an exhortation unto it, so that, while the whole world fasteth, we alone who dwell in Egypt may not become a laughingstock for our non-fasting and for surrendering ourselves unto rejoicing in these days." In the 19th Festal Letter of the year 346 the observance of the Forty-Day Fast is already presented by St. Athanasius as an indispensable condition for a worthy celebration of Pascha, saying: "Whosoever disregardeth the Forty-Day Fast, entering as it were heedlessly and uncleansed into the Holy of Holies, that man doth not celebrate the feast of Pascha." [^1248]

Where the six-week fast was adopted, they commenced it on the Monday of the second week of the present Orthodox fast. Consequently, the present First Week of Great Lent in such a practice was not included in the fast. As a memorial of such a practice, which is no longer followed even by its principal ancient adherent, the Roman Church, there hath remained in this latter Church the fact that the Secret Prayer of this Monday—which now in the Roman Church is already the sixth day of the fast—commenceth with the words: *Sacrificium quadragesimalis initii solemniter immolamus* ("We solemnly offer the sacrifice of the beginning of the Forty-Day Fast"). [^1249] The lengthening of the forty-day fast unto seven weeks was caused by the circumstance that among the number of fasting days there were not to be included not only the Sundays falling during the Forty-Day Fast, but, according unto the view of the Eastern Church, Saturdays as well. When, following the emergence in the West of fasting on Saturday, Eastern ecclesiastical legislation began to prohibit it with particular insistence, [^1250] then those Eastern Churches which hitherto had maintained a six-week fast before Pascha likewise adopted a seven-week fast. In that very Jerusalem Church, in which Eusebius and Cyril of Jerusalem (who said on Great and Holy Friday: "these elapsed days of the Forty-Day Fast" [^1251]) know a forty-day fast, the Aquitanian pilgrim lady of the IV century already witnesseth eight weeks of the pre-Paschal fast; [^1252] yet Peter, Patriarch of Jerusalem (524–544), testifieth [^1253] unto a seven-week fast in his church.

### The Manner of Fasting

In that same historian Socrates, who acquaints us with the gradual lengthening of the pre-Paschal fast from a one-week fast into a forty-day fast, we find also information concerning the development undergone by the very form of fasting.

> "The divergence," saith Socrates, "doth not concern solely the number of fasting days, but also the conception of abstinence from foods; for some abstain from the eating of every kind of animal, while others of all animate creatures partake only of fish, and certain ones

=== LEAF p230 ===
> together with fish eat birds also, saying that birds, according unto the narrative of Moses, likewise proceeded from water. Some abstain even from fruits and eggs, others feed only upon dry bread, certain ones do not partake even of that, while others, fasting until the ninth hour, partake thereafter of any food." [^1254]

Sozomen, who generally repeateth Socrates, doth not note this difference in the fasting of the Forty-Day Fast, pointing only unto the difference in its duration. Thus, very soon after there was accepted in the Church, as one of the possible forms of fasting, abstinence from flesh-meat, an exception was made for certain kinds of meat—and in this regard not only for fish, as now, but for birds as well. Yet this latter kind of exception, possessing no foundation for itself, soon fell away, and only the former remained. In permitting the use of fish during a fast they could be guided by the following considerations: 1) the curse of God following upon the Fall was directed specifically upon the earth, and not upon the waters or their inhabitants; 2) fish pertain unto the element with which baptism is connected; 3) in the narrative of creation it is said: "The Spirit of God moved over the water" (Gen. 1:2). [^1255]

The next degree of abstinence in fasting Socrates considereth the non-consumption of "fruits and eggs"; consequently, not everywhere was a distinction yet established between the one and the other. As a trace of this there hath remained among us the Cheese-fare Week (*Sedmitsa Syrnaya*).

The concluding words in the testimony of Socrates demonstrate that what was regarded as essential in fasting even in the V century was not the kind of food, but the duration of complete abstinence therefrom, that is to say, the hour of partaking thereof. In other words, what was essential in fasting was that on a fasting day there was no midday meal (*ἄριστον*, *prandium*), but solely dinner or supper (*δεῖπνον*, *coena*—among the ancients the midday dinner was not distinguished from supper). This is apparent also from other writers of that same epoch. "Wait a little (with food): soon is the end of the day," said St. Ambrose during Lent. [^1256] "Thou waitest for evening to take food, yet spendest the entire day in the lawcourts," reproached St. Basil the Great his listeners during the fast. [^1257] "Let us not think that the mere non-eating until evening is sufficient for us unto salvation," said in like manner at that time St. John Chrysostom; "what profit is there from fasting, tell me, if thou dost not eat for whole days, yet surrenderest thyself the whole day unto games, jesting, and even *(Orig. p. 244)* perjury and evil-speaking?" [^1258] "In the days of fasting," exhorteth Blessed Augustine, "that which we would consume for dinner, let us bestow upon the poor, and not upon the preparation of sumptuous suppers with delicacies for the most refined tastes." [^1259] "By as much as it drieth up in the body," reproacheth contemporaries Peter Chrysologus, "by so much is it fuller in"
