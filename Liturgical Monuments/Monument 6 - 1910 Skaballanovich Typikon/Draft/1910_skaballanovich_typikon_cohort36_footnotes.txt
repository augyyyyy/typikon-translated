The most important Athonite monasteries possess at present written texts of their Typikon (of Jerusalem type); thus the Lavra of St. Athanasius in 15th–16th century manuscripts; the Pantokrator Monastery in a 15th-century manuscript. The ordinances of the Dionysiou Monastery were recorded in 1624 by its hieromonk Ignatius, and this record enjoyed great authority—copies thereof exist in many Athonite monasteries; thus for the St. Panteleimon Monastery there was made (in its library there is an 18th-century manuscript) a revised copy from the Dionysiou Typikon under the title: 'Accurate Typikon of the Ecclesiastical Office, Collected from St. Sabas and Diverse Others'; here, besides the Athanasian Typikon, references occur unto the Athonite Typika of the 'Right Choir,' of the 'Left Choir,' and the 'Great Typikon of the Adrianopolitan.' From the Dionysiou Typikon a copy was likewise made in 1875 for the Iviron Monastery under the title: 'Abridged Typikon in Conjunction with Local Custom' (all unpublished and unresearched). Yet unto this day, as ever, the Holy Mountain follows in divine worship unwritten traditions more than written texts; for this reason divine worship here is guided not so much by the Typikon as by the *typikaris* (a most honorable personage in the monastery), and in difficult cases by a council of *typikareis* (Dmitrievsky, A.
