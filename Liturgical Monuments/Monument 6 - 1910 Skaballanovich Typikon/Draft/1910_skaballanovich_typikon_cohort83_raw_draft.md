=== LEAF p821 ===
striving to embrace all the spiritual needs and to touch all the cords of the worshiper's heart. The heirmoi of a laudatory character in the Sunday kanon almost alternate in each tone with heirmoi of a prayerful, sorrowful character. At the same time, the former strive to encompass the entire system of Christian doctrine, speaking first of the creation of the world (for the most part the 3rd heirmos, because the 1st develops the theme of its Biblical Ode), of the Incarnation of the Son of God (for the most part the 4th, sometimes the 4th and 5th, sometimes also the 3rd), of the Church (the 3rd or 4th heirmos); making use of the motifs of the Odes of Isaiah and Jonah, they depict the striving of the soul toward the light of Christ (the 5th heirmos) and supplication for deliverance from the abyss of sin and suffering (the 6th heirmos). Speaking of all this, the heirmoi of the Sunday kanon do not lose sight of their Biblical Ode, with which they must connect the troparia following them, and they express their relationship unto it, if not by direct references (in the 4th heirmos the Prophet Habakkuk is frequently mentioned, in the 6th—Jonah), then by the manner of expression: in the 3rd heirmos the expressions "be established" [*utverdisya*] are frequent; in the 5th—"enlightenment" [*prosveshchenie*], "light" [*svet*]; in the 6th—"abyss" [*bezdna*], "sea" [*more*], "surging" [*volnenie*]. The 1st, 7th, and 8th heirmoi, however, are not limited unto such a distant connection with the Biblical Odes, but occupy themselves entirely with the theme of these odes in view of the exalted prototypical significance of the events celebrated therein. The 9th heirmos, moreover, not only in the Sunday kanon but in almost all kanons, is dedicated exclusively unto the glorification of the Mother of God.

As regards the troparia, in the first kanon—the Resurrection kanon (*anastasimos* [Gr. ἀναστάσιμος])—they occupy themselves not so much with a picturesque description of the event as with explaining its meaning, uncovering its inner essence; the Theotokia of this kanon, however, are of general content—glorifying the Mother of God without reference unto the event of the Resurrection (in contrast unto the Theotokia of the Paschal kanon). The Cross-Resurrection kanon (*stauroanastasimos* [Gr. σταυροαναστάσιμος]), glorifying the Resurrection of the Savior, so to speak, through the prism of His suffering, compensates thereby for the impossibility for many to hear the services of Wednesday and Friday, which are specifically dedicated unto the latter commemoration—an impossibility that applied not only unto laymen, but also unto hermits; the Theotokia of this kanon are likewise of general content. The Theotokos kanon of Sunday Matins, although, like the Theotokia troparia of the two preceding kanons, it glorifies Her entirely without reference unto the event of the Resurrection, nevertheless differs essentially from the corresponding kanons at Matins of Wednesday and Friday and at all Small Complines in that it possesses not a sorrowful-penitential character, but a joyful-laudatory one, and is directed toward an equally detailed explanation of the significance for us of

=== LEAF p822 ===
the mystery of the Incarnation of Christ, just as the two preceding kanons explain the mysteries of the Resurrection and death, whereby the comprehensiveness of Christian edification from the kanon is attained.

*(Orig. p. 679)*
### Time of Origin

The first Sunday kanons undoubtedly belong unto John of Damascus. Judging from the fact that the oldest copy of the Alexis-Studite Typikon makes no mention of the Cross-Resurrection kanon and apparently presupposes only a single Sunday kanon [^2534], one may think that the Cross-Resurrection kanons are of later origin than the former. Nevertheless, they are mentioned under the name of the second Sunday kanon, or at least presupposed, by later (from the 13th century) manuscripts of the Alexis-Studite Typikon. All manuscripts of the Studite Typikon and the Evergetis do not know the Theotokos kanon; it is mentioned only by manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon, beginning from the oldest [^2535]. The manuscripts of the Octoechos, at least the Slavic ones, present significant variations among themselves and with the present Octoechos (corrected in the 17th century according unto Greek printed editions) in the composition of the troparia of all three kanons of Sunday Matins: most frequently different troparia or a different number of them (compared with the present Octoechos) appear in the Theotokos kanon, which some Octoechoi (*Parakletika*) of the 14th–15th centuries lack altogether; there is less difference in the Cross-Resurrection kanon, and least of all in the Sunday kanon, but even here the Theotokia are often different, and at times troparia of the present Cross-Resurrection kanon are found in the Sunday kanon, and vice versa. Not infrequently there are different heirmoi in the Cross-Resurrection and Theotokos kanons [^2536].

### Unto How Many Stanzas for Each Kanon?

In accordance with the degree of proximity of the various kanons of Sunday Matins unto the celebrated event, the volume in which they are chanted is determined—that is, the number of troparia in them, or according unto the technical expression: *unto* (*eis* [Gr. εἰς]) how many (verses) they are chanted. The first kanon, the Sunday kanon, at ordinary Sunday Matins unencumbered by any concurrences, is chanted unto 4, including the heirmos, which is chanted once; the Cross-Resurrection kanon is chanted unto 3, the Theotokos kanon unto 3, and that of the Menaion unto 4; the three latter kanons are without heirmoi. Here it is understood of itself that if for such a number there are insufficient troparia in the Octoechos, as sometimes happens with the Cross-Resurrection and Theotokos kanons, then it is necessary to

=== LEAF p823 ===
double the first troparion [^2537] (the first, as the most important). This is the standard order for combining kanons at Sunday Matins. Yet it is applicable in rare cases, since for the most part the Menaion—which, however, is not even always chanted on Sunday—has more than one kanon, most frequently two, or has a kanon unto more than 4. Cases of the latter kind are provided for in subsequent chapters of the Typikon—3, 4, and 5 [^2538]. Nowhere in the Typikon is *(Orig. p. 680)* provision made for the most frequent case, when there are two kanons in the Menaion; since two kanons have 8 troparia, it is most natural in this case to be guided by the rubric for a great (vigil) saint falling on Sunday and having a kanon unto 8; according unto this rubric, given in Chapter 3 of the Typikon, the Sunday kanon is chanted unto 4, the Cross-Resurrection kanon is omitted, the Theotokos kanon is chanted unto 2, and the Menaion unto 8. Such a rubric is likewise given for this case by the "Brief Explanation" [*izʺyavleniem vkrattsē*] printed at the beginning of the General Menaion.

### According unto Ancient Typika

The Studite Typika do not specify in what quantity the one or two Sunday kanons then in use were chanted, nor the kanon of the Menaion, which according unto these Typika was likewise chanted alongside the Sunday kanons [^2539].

The Evergetis: "The 1st Sunday kanon unto 6, the 2nd unto 4, and the saint unto 4; if it be a great saint, both Sunday kanons unto 4 and the saint unto 6" [^2540].

The Jerusalem Typikon: the Georgian manuscript does not define the quantity; the Greek manuscripts and printed editions likewise do not, but add: "If the saint of the Menaion is celebrated, the Cross-Resurrection kanon of the Octoechos is omitted; and at the kanon of the saint we appoint 6 verses from **'Until Thy people pass over'**" (*Dondeže proidut lyudie Tvoi*).

The oldest Slavic manuscripts: "The Sunday kanon with heirmos unto 4, and the Cross-Resurrection without heirmos unto 4, and the Theotokos unto 2, and unto the saint in the Menaion unto 4"; later ones—of the 15th and 16th centuries: "Two Sunday kanons, and of the Theotokos, and of the saint," without

=== LEAF p824 ===
definition of the quantity, as in the Greek ones; "if the Typikon appoints unto the saint unto 6, then we chant the Sunday kanon with heirmos unto 4, and the Cross-Resurrection unto 2, and the Theotokos unto 2 (later, 15th cent.: the Sunday unto 4 and the Theotokos unto 4) and the saint unto 6 (in still later, 16th cent.—there is nothing concerning a saint unto six); but when in the Menaion it is unto the saint unto 8, the Cross-Resurrection is omitted, and the Theotokos is unto 2, and the saint unto 8 (later: if the saint who is in the Menaion is celebrated, we omit the Cross-Resurrection kanon which is in the Octoechos, and chant the Sunday kanon unto 4, and the Theotokos unto 4, and the saint unto 6—and we recite the verses from **'Until the people passed over'** [Exodus 15:16]); likewise also unto two saints, if there are two kanons; likewise it is in forefeasts or between feasts" (that is, in the Menaion unto 8; in later manuscripts—absent) [^2541].

The Old Believer Typikon: Sunday 4, Cross-Resurrection 2, Theotokos 4, Menaion 4; if the saint is unto 6, Theotokos unto 2; unto 8, as with us, with the addition that it is likewise for 2 saints, and in forefeasts and afterfeasts [^2542].

The Constantinopolitan Typikon: "The Sunday kanon and that of the occurring Saint"; the *Hieratikon*: "The Sunday kanons unto 4 and the Menaion unto 4" [^2543].

The Greek Octoechos: "If the saint is celebrated *(Orig. p. 681)* unto 6 or 8, the Cross-Resurrection kanon is omitted" [^2544], against which the Old Believer Typikon states: "If the saint who is in the Menaion in course is celebrated unto 6, we do not omit the Cross-Resurrection kanon" [^2545]. In practice, the Old Believers at a minor saint chant, as with us—the Cross-Resurrection unto 3 and the Theotokos unto 3 [^2546].

### Katavasia

The troparia of the kanon, in their content, depart too far from the Biblical Odes with which they are connected by the heirmos; and thus, in order to restore their connection with these odes, their long series concludes, just as it opened, with an heirmos. Such a return of the ode of the kanon unto the heirmos is called a *katavasia* (*katavasia* [Gr. καταβασία] = *katabasis* [Gr. κατάβασις])—a descent, a returning (of an army from an expedition). On the greatest feasts the katavasia consists in the repetition of the opening heirmos; on lesser feasts, including Sunday, the heirmos of another, kindred or proximate feast serves as the katavasia; on weekdays the heirmos of the last kanon serves as the katavasia, and occurs only after the most important odes—the 3rd, 6th, 8th, and 9th; finally, during Lent the katavasia sometimes even replaces the heirmos, that is, the heirmos is chanted only in the capacity of a katavasia.

=== LEAF p825 ===
### History

The Studite Typika require the chanting of the heirmos of the same or a proximate feast at the end of each ode only on great feasts—sometimes even two heirmoi—yet they do not call this a katavasia, but express it simply: "and at the end the heirmos," or: "and again the heirmos" [^2547], and once: "in place of the *perisse*" [*v peris mesto*] [^2548]; the Alexis-Studite Typikon, however, calls the hypakoe a *katavasia* [^2549]. The Evergetis Typikon knows the present term *katavasia*, but likewise requires it only on great feasts [^2550]. So too do the oldest manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon [^2551]. In Greece, the katavasia of Odes 1–8, apparently, is chanted in its entirety after Ode 8.

### The Katavasia "I Will Open My Mouth"

Sunday Matins naturally has a katavasia of the festal type—that is, after each ode of the kanon. But even festal katavasia, as observed above, possesses various degrees. The most solemn is the repetition as katavasia of the heirmoi of the feast itself, moreover of both if the feast has two kanons. Such a katavasia is possessed only by the greatest of the Despotic Great Feasts and the Dormition. All other feasts have *(Orig. p. 682)* as katavasia the heirmoi of another feast, for the most part the nearest of the great feasts, with the aim of increasing their solemnity by such a katavasia.

Sunday belongs, by type of katavasia, unto feasts of the latter kind. The heirmoi of the proper kanon of Sunday Matins bear no relation unto the celebrated event and therefore are not suitable for a katavasia (for which reason they are not doubled at the beginning of the kanon). For the Sunday katavasia heirmoi in honor of the Mother of God were chosen—for the identical reason that a separate kanon unto Her is appointed at this Matins, and because every series of ecclesiastical hymns customarily concludes with a hymn in Her honor. More specifically, for the Sunday katavasia there were chosen from the heirmoi of Theotokos feasts the heirmoi **"I will open my mouth"** (*Otverzu usta moya*), used on all feasts of the Theotokos—yet not in the form in which these heirmoi are used on any of the Great Feasts of the Theotokos, when their text is adapted unto the celebrated event, but in the form in which they serve as the heirmoi of the majority of secondary Theotokos feasts, principally in honor of icons of the Mother of God, and consequently with a general glorification of Her as the Mother of God. In this form their content fully corresponds unto that purpose with which there is introduced into the Sunday service

=== LEAF p826 ===
this glorification. In these heirmoi, after a general invitation of the faithful to chant the wonders of the Mother of God—that is, instances of Her constant miraculous aid unto us (instances that serve as the reason for Her so frequent and fervent veneration, and in particular for veneration on every Lord's Day) (1st heirmos), and after a supplication that She accept this glorification (3rd heirmos), the doctrine concerning the mystery of the Incarnation from Her of the Son of God (5th heirmos) is unfolded on the foundation of the prophecy of Isaiah 19:1 (4th heirmos) and the prototypical saving of the 3 Holy Children (7th and 8th heirmoi)—whom the faithful are summoned to welcome directly with the clapping of hands on this feast as much of Her as of Her Son (6th heirmos), on a feast celebrated likewise in the heavens (9th heirmos). Such a katavasia, however, occurs only on Sundays that are unencumbered by any special concurrences.

### Time of Introduction

The katavasia **"I will open my mouth"** for the kanon of Sunday Matins is not mentioned not only by any redaction of the Studite Typikon (the Alexis-Studite appoints it together with the heirmoi of Palm Sunday as the katavasia for its temple on March 12) [^2552] and the Evergetis Typikon, according unto which, apparently, on Sundays as on weekdays there was no katavasia, but it is not mentioned either by any of the ancient manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon—Georgian, Greek, Greek printed, and Slavic of the 14th–15th centuries; an indication of this katavasia is found only in Slavic manuscripts of the 16th century [^2553].

Under Arseny Sukhanov [^2554] it was used in the East; but it is not mentioned by the present Greek liturgical books—the Octoechos, the Typikon of the Great Church, and the *Hieratikon*, nor by the Old Believer Typikon. In *(Orig. p. 683)* practice, however, among the Old Believers it is used during the identical periods (with minor exceptions) as with us [^2555].

### Order of Chanting and Melody of the Kanon

The kanon, like every series of hymns, is chanted alternately by both choirs so that all the verses of the Biblical Odes and all the troparia with the heirmoi in each ode are distributed equally between the choirs, for which reason the number of these verses and troparia is always even. If before the heirmos a portion of the Biblical Ode without refrains is appointed, one choir (the beginning choir) chants the 1st verse of the ode, the other chants the second, and so forth; then the first choir chants the verse of the Biblical Ode appointed as the refrain unto the heirmos, and immediately after this the heirmos; the second choir chants the next verse of the ode and the 1st troparion, and so forth, and at the end

=== LEAF p827 ===
"Glory"—the penultimate troparion, and "Both now"—the Theotokion. If before the heirmos no Biblical Ode is appointed, as occurs on Sundays, feasts, and certain other days (when **"Let us sing unto the Lord"** [*Poim Gospodevi*] is appointed), the first choir chants the heirmos, the 2nd choir—on days other than Sunday—chants the heirmos a second time, but on Sunday chants the refrain **"Glory to Thy Holy Resurrection, O Lord"** (*Slava, Gospodi, svyatomu voskreseniyu Tvoemu*) and the troparion, again the first choir chants the 1st verse of the Biblical Ode and the second troparion, and so forth [^2556]. Owing unto the fact that the chanting of the troparion begins with the chanting of the refrain, the refrain and troparion constitute a single whole: the troparion represents, as it were, the development of the thought of the refrain, its application unto the celebrated event. And the refrain is chanted in such a manner that the most characteristic sounds of the troparion (and heirmos) melody are preserved in it, namely, of its final musical strophe. Since for this strophe only the concluding words of the refrain are needed, its first and greater part is chanted in recitative and identically for all tones (a pattern of such execution of the refrain familiar unto everyone is **"We praise, bless, and worship the Lord..."** [*Khvalim, blagoslovim, poklanyaemsya Gospodevi...*] before the katavasia of Ode 8). As for the troparia themselves, they must be chanted entirely like the heirmoi, for which reason the heirmos is indicated before each kanon, even if the kanon is chanted without heirmoi. Inasmuch as the chant of the kanon is distinct from the sticheraric and troparic chants (for dismissal troparia and at "God is the Lord"), and since the entire kanon normally constitutes a series of 112 strophes (14 × 8), there is introduced by it into Matins an indefinitely long stream of this distinctive, complex melody, interrupted only twice by the troparic melody (the Sessional Hymns and Kontakia after Odes 3 and 6).

### When and Why Did They Cease Chanting the Troparia of the Kanon?

Already at the time of the Stoglav Council (1551) there was, apparently, a significant distinction in the manner of executing, on the one hand, ecclesiastical hymns, and on the other—the Introductory Psalms, kathismata with the Sessional Hymns and troparia connected with them, and kanons, as may be deduced from the following expressions of the Council concerning both manners of execution: "And as for the rest of ecclesiastical *(Orig. p. 684)* chanting, everything is to be performed according unto the Typikon: the Doxology to be chanted, and the katavasia chanted with the choirs coming together; but at Vespers and at Matins the psalms and the Psalter are to be spoken quietly and unhurriedly, with all attentiveness; likewise the troparia and Sessional Hymns they should recite according unto the rite and unhurriedly, and thereafter read, and of psalms and the Psalter at one and the same time

=== LEAF p828 ===
they should not speak, nor canonarch two kanons together" [^2557].

Yet until the middle of the 17th century and somewhat later, both the kathismata and the troparia of the kanons were in one way or another chanted, as is evident from the ever-spreading custom of so-called polyphonic chanting (*mnogoglasie*): since chanting absolutely everything at the service lengthened it exceedingly, they began at one and the same time to chant—one the Six Psalms, another "God is the Lord" and the troparia, a third the kathismata, a fourth one kanon, a fifth another kanon, while the deacon at this time was reciting litanies, and so forth. "Give heed, holy master," wrote a certain Agathonikos unto Archbishop Sophrony of Suzdal in the 17th century, "unto the voice of the Apostle, that he shows those who all speak at once to be as ones possessed; is it not evident among us, when in two voices, and in three, and in four, and in five, and at times in six and in seven, not only unbelievers, but also a believer who loves piety and ecclesiastical good order, will say of us: are we possessed?" [^2558] This circumstance must have brought about also the legislative prohibition on the part of one of the Moscow Councils of the 17th century against chanting the entire kanon. Arseny Sukhanov notes that in the East they chant every troparion of the kanon in its entirety, and not merely the ending [^2559]; consequently, in Russia during his time the transition from chanting the troparia unto reading them was taking place: they chanted only their endings. At present in the East they chant the entire kanon with its troparia, and not the heirmoi alone, for which reason the chanting of the kanon occupies the greater part of Matins there, although sometimes someone chants alone on each choir [^2560].

### Melody of the Kanons at Sunday Matins

However distinct in each tone the kanon melody may be from the sticheraric and troparic melodies, nevertheless the general character of the tone (indicated above, p. 539) is preserved in it as well; and since the kanon represents the longest series of hymns in the entire Vigil (on condition, of course, that the troparia are chanted), by its tone it imparts musical coloration unto the Vigil above all. For the character of the latter it is far from indifferent whether these 80 troparia of the Octoechos kanons ((4 + 3 + 3) × 8) at Sunday Matins are chanted in the most joyful tone (Tone 1), in a less joyful one (Tone 4), or in tones as mournful as Tones 6 and 2.

It must be observed, however, that the Cross-Resurrection and Theotokos kanons are chanted with a melody not entirely identical unto that of the Sunday kanon, but—for the sake of variety and greater correspondence unto their content—with another. Especially the first of these kanons, as following immediately upon the Sunday kanon, always differs significantly

=== LEAF p829 ===
from it in melody, although this melody is in the identical tone. Specifically, the melodies for the Cross-Resurrection kanons are chosen to be more artistic *(Orig. p. 685)* and compunctionate than those for the Sunday kanon, while for the Theotokos kanon they are at times the identical ones as for the Cross-Resurrection, at times new ones again. This melody is indicated in the Octoechos in such a manner that before the troparia of the Cross-Resurrection and Theotokos kanons the heirmos or its opening words are inscribed: since these kanons are chanted without heirmoi, such an inscription has as its aim to show that the troparia of the kanon must be chanted after the model of the inscribed heirmos; consequently, it is the identical thing as the heading in the stichera and Sessional Hymns: "Prosomoion" [*podoben*]. Specifically, the Cross-Resurrection kanons are chanted according unto the melody of the following heirmoi: Tone 1 according unto the melody of **"Christ is born"** (*Khristos raždaetsya*); Tone 2 **"Untrodden, unusual"** (*Netrenu, neobychnu*); Tone 3 **"Let us sing a new song, O ye peoples"** (*Pēsn' novuyu poim lyudie*); Tone 4 **"I will open my mouth"** (*Otverzu usta moya*); Tone 5 **"Unto God the Savior"** (*Spasitelyu Bogu*); Tone 6 **"By the wave of the sea"** (*Volnoyu morskoyu*); Tone 7 **"He who covered the deep"** (*Pontom pokry*); Tone 8 **"Having traversed the water"** (*Vodu proshed*). In this manner, on a simple Sunday, under the exact observance of the Typikon, the church could resound with the thrillingly sweet melodies of Great Saturday, the Nativity of Christ, the Ascension, and Pentecost. The effect is heightened by the fact that the troparia of the Cross-Resurrection kanon, executed with these touching melodies, are likewise in their verbal expression close in places unto the heirmoi whose music they imitate. For example, the 1st and 2nd troparia of the Cross-Resurrection kanon of Tone 1 are constructed analogously unto the heirmos **"Christ is born"**: "Christ deifieth me becoming incarnate; Christ exalteth me humbling Himself; Christ maketh me passionless." But most frequently the troparia of the Cross-Resurrection kanon borrow from their originals the concluding words that constitute the final and richest musical strophe; thus, in Tone 1, Ode 3 concludes: "Who hast lifted up our horn, Holy art Thou, O Lord"; Ode 7: "Praised and exceedingly glorified is the God of our fathers"; Tone 6, Ode 3: "There is none holy save Thee, O Lord, crying aloud"; Ode 7: "O God the Redeemer, blessed art Thou"; Ode 8: "O children, bless; O priests, sing praises; O peoples, supremely exalt Him unto all ages"; Ode 9: "Unto Thee who with faith and love magnify Thee." It is noteworthy that not all Cross-Resurrection kanons imitate the heirmoi of such great feasts. For instance, for the kanon of Tone 2 one could have chosen as a model the heirmoi of Theophany; for Tone 3—the Meeting of the Lord; for Tone 4—Palm Sunday or the Transfiguration; for Tone 8—the Exaltation of the Cross. Yet this was not done.

As regards the Theotokos kanon, it seems, as it were, to hesitate to imitate such exalted models; and when the Cross-Resurrection kanon has a melody from heirmoi that are too festive, the Theotokos kanon is chanted unto the melodies of the Sunday kanon; thus, in Tone 1 it has the melody of **"Thy triumphant right hand"** (*Tvoya pobeditel'naya*); in Tone 5 **"The horse and rider"** (*Konya i vsadnika*); in

=== LEAF p830 ===
Tone 6 **"As on dry land"** (*Yako po sukhu*); in Tone 7 **"By Thy nod"** (*Maniem Tvoim*) (excluding Ode 9, where it follows the model of the Cross-Resurrection kanon: **"Mother of God and Virgin"** [*Mati Bozhiya i Deva*]). Where the Cross-Resurrection kanon has a more modest model, the Theotokos kanon continues its melody: therefore in Tone 2 the Theotokos kanon likewise has as its model the heirmos **"Untrodden, unusual"**; in Tone 3 **"A new song"**; in Tone 4 **"I will open my mouth"** (a more suitable model could not exist here—in view of the katavasia). Only in Tone 8 do all three kanons have different models, although not for all odes (namely, excluding Odes 4 and 7).

*(Orig. p. 686)*
The customary katavasia of Sunday Matins, **"I will open my mouth"**, has a tone likewise fully corresponding unto Sunday joy: the 4th heirmos tone, in which this katavasia is chanted, is the tone for kanons on feasts of, so to speak, moderate solemnity: with it are chanted the kanons of more than half of the Great Feasts. Lacking that boundlessness in its joy possessed by Tone 1, especially in its Paschal and Nativity melodies, this tone for heirmoi nevertheless shimmers and glitters entirely with a light, triumphant joy; its somewhat rapid melody at times resembles the sound of the clapping of hands unto which one of the heirmoi of the Sunday katavasia invites.

### Historical Note

In Slavic manuscript Octoechoi, especially in the so-called *Parakletika* (an Octoechos containing only kanons) of the 14th and 15th centuries, not infrequently the heirmoi for the Cross-Resurrection and Theotokos kanons are indicated differently from those in the present printed Slavic Octoechos (which is in full agreement in this regard with the Greek printed edition), but the difference is less than in the troparia [^2561].

### Censing at the Beginning of the Kanon According unto Ancient Typika

As constituting an independent portion of Matins, as it were a distinct service, the kanon, like other services, had according unto ancient Typika a censing at its beginning. The *Diataxis* [*Chin*] of Patriarch Philotheos provides the detailed rite of this censing: "With the beginning of the kanon the priest comes forth and stands in the right choir at the customary place; and the deacon, having first received the blessing for the censer, censes first the sanctuary entirely, then goes out by the northern door and traces a cross before the holy doors, and censes first the icons on the right
