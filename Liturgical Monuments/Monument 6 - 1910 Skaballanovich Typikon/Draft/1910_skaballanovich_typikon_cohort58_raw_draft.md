## The Order and Content of the Ninth Hour

The Ninth Hour is dedicated to the commemoration of the very death of the Saviour upon the Cross, since, according to the Evangelists Matthew and Mark, it was precisely around this hour (according to Matthew 'about the ninth hour', *περὶ δὲ τὴν ἐννάτην ὥραν*, according to Mark 'at the ninth hour', *τῇ ὥρᾳ τῇ ἐννάτῃ* [^1952]—not: *in* the ninth hour) that Christ made His penultimate cry upon the Cross: **"My God, My God, why hast Thou forsaken Me?"** (*Боже, Боже Мой, вскую Мя еси оставил*), which was *(Orig. p. 476)* immediately followed by the offering of vinegar to the Saviour to drink and (after an unspecified interval of time) by the final cry recorded only by the Evangelist Luke: **"Father, into Thy hands I commend My spirit"** (*Отче, в руце Твои предаю дух Мой*) [^1953], with which the Lord died. Beginning from this first moment, the *Instructional Notice* (*Uchitel'noe izvestie*, appended to the *Sluzhebnik*) exhorts the priest to commemorate at the Ninth Hour the final events of the life of Christ. But the office of the Ninth Hour dwells not so much upon this striking yet external aspect of the Saviour's final minutes, which is already embraced by the office of the Sixth Hour, as upon the inner and more essential aspect—the very act of Christ's death as the separation of soul from body and the descent of His soul into Hades (an aspect of the redemptive work of Christ unfolded with special depth by the Apostle Peter [^1954]), and subsequently upon the death of Christ as the definitive consummation of our salvation.

Among the Psalms of the Ninth Hour, the 83rd [LXX 83 / MT 84], **"How beloved are Thy dwellings, O Lord of hosts"** (*Коль возлюбленна селения Твоя*), which was presumably a song of the Jerusalem pilgrims [^1955] and portrays the beauty of God's habitation on Zion, is applicable to the soul's disposition of the Saviour upon the Cross, to the yearning of His soul from thence into the mansions of the Heavenly Father [^1956].

The following Psalm of the Ninth Hour, the 84th [LXX 84 / MT 85], **"Lord, Thou hast been favorable unto Thy land"** (*Благоволил еси Господи землю Твою*), which celebrates the blessing of God poured out upon the holy land following its repopulation by the returning captives (from Babylon), directs our thought from Christ dying for us unto the good things acquired by His death: preeminently, the forgiveness of sins and the cessation of God's wrath toward us. It is true that (according to the Psalm) although salvation has been accomplished, it is not yet fully realized (so long as salvation has not been appropriated by all mankind)—hence the prayer **"Turn us, O God of our salvation"** (literally from the Hebrew 'restore us'), and so forth. Nevertheless, the psalmist as it were overhears the divine decision concerning us, concerning peace with us, and in ecstasy contemplates how, instead of God's former enduring wrath upon us and His strict justice over the earth, now the earth is overshadowed only by God's saving mer*(Orig. p. 477)*cy and His truthful faithfulness (that is, to His former promises); these mercy and truth [faithfulness] are personified in the Psalm in

=== LEAF p572 ===
the highly poetic portrait of two benevolent spirits meeting upon the earth and kissing one another [^1957]. The final Psalm of the Ninth Hour, the 85th [LXX 85 / MT 86], **"Bow down Thine ear, O Lord, and hear me"** (*Приклони Господи ухо Твое*), inscribed 'a prayer of David' (perhaps during the rebellion of Absalom, at any rate during some mortal peril), returns our thought from the work of Christ—from the redemption accomplished by Him upon the Cross, considered in the second Psalm of the Hour—unto His Person (with which the first Psalm was concerned), unto His condition upon the Cross; but in this condition the present Psalm directs attention unto another moment (in comparison with the first Psalm), namely, unto the state of Christ, of His soul, after death, that is, unto His descent into Hades. The Psalm continually speaks of the soul (in the Old Testament this word, however, for the most part signified 'life'), and unto the salvation of this Soul of the suffering Righteous One from enemies, from the lowest Hades itself, there is attached such significance that all nations shall worship God if God works upon His Servant a 'token' (*знамение*), a miracle of salvation (the Resurrection). The final words of the Psalm concerning the 'token' [v. 17: **"Work in me a token for good"**], in view of their special importance, are read twice (as is done elsewhere only at the introductory Psalms of Vespers and Matins—a remnant of the ancient chanting of Psalms with refrains).

In the subsequent hymns and prayers of the Ninth Hour, the sacred commemoration connected with this Hour is unfolded from other aspects. Thus, in the Lenten troparion of the Hour, **"Thou Who at the ninth hour"** (*Иже в девятый час*), prayer is offered [^1958] unto Christ Who died for us to mortify in us carnal 'mindings' (lusts); in the verses to the troparion (Ps. 118:169–171 [MT 119:169–171]) [^1959] the persecuted righteous one (Christ or the Christian) entreats the hearing of his prayer. The Theotokion **"Thou Who for our sake wast born"** (*Иже нас ради рождейся*), which is already of Sunday character, commemorating the Nativity, Death, and Resurrection of Christ, beseeches Him for lovingkindness and salvation through the intercessions of the Mother of God for a despairing people (*λαόν*, Slav. *люди*, *ἀπεγνωσμένον*). The verse of the Hour, borrowed from the Song of the Three Holy Youths [Dan. 3:34 LXX] [^1960], **"Deliver us not up utterly"** (*Не предаждь нас до конца*), prayerfully reminds God of His covenant with the chosen people (a covenant realized through the death of Christ). In the Lenten troparia after the Trisagion in Tone 8, **"When the thief beheld"** (*Видя разбойник*) and **"Between two thieves"** (*Посреде двою разбойнику*), and in their Theotokion **"The Lamb and Shepherd"** (*Агнца и пастыря*), the sacred commemorations of the Hour are augmented by moments of great importance not previously indicated in the office: *(Orig. p. 478)* the repentance of the thief and the suffering of the Mother of God beside the Cross. The prayer of the Hour, attributed (as is also the prayer of the Sixth Hour) to St. Basil the Great and addressed to Christ (as is elsewhere only the prayer of the First Hour; the prayer of the Third Hour is addressed to the Holy Trinity, and that of the Sixth to God the Father), entreats Him, by virtue of His sufferings for our sake, to cleanse our frequent and grievous sins, to spare, to save us, to deliver us from the devil, and to mortify our carnal

=== LEAF p573 ===
mindings unto a righteous life, wherein mention is made also of eternal rest (evening) in the dwelling of all those who make merry with Christ, rejoicing with the gladness and joy (the Resurrection) of those who love Him.

## History of the Ninth Hour

Sanctified by prayer already by the Apostles [^1961], the Ninth Hour is subsequently indicated as an hour of prayer by a succession of 3rd-century writers and monuments, wherein as sacred commemorations for it there are specified the darkening of the sun at this hour during Christ's Crucifixion, the renewal of the covenant with the chosen people (cf. the present verse of the Hour), and the descent of Christ into Hades; moreover, in distinction from the other Hours, the comparative joyfulness of the commemorations associated with this Hour is noted [^1962]. Concerning the structure and content of the office of the Ninth Hour prior to the 8th century, no definite accounts have survived (Sylvia of Aquitaine [Egeria] in the 4th century speaks, as we have seen, only concerning the general order of the service of the Hour, as does also Canon 18 of the Council of Laodicea in the same century, according to which the service of the Ninth Hour must be the same as Vespers). In the Sinai Horologion No. 863 of the 8th–9th c., at the Ninth Hour all our present Psalms are indicated, and in addition to them the canticle: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers, and praised and glorified is Thy Name unto the ages"** [Dan. 3:26 LXX]; as verses to the *Alleluia* [^1963] there is appointed Psalm 22 [LXX 22 / MT 23], **"The Lord is my shepherd"** (*Господь пасет мя*), divided into two sections; the troparion is close to the present one: 'Thou Who, hanging upon the Tree at the ninth hour, didst yield Thy soul unto the Father', with a conclusion that served as a refrain: 'O Christ our God, glory [to Thee]'; the verse of the Hour is our present one, with the portion preceding 'for Abraham's sake' repeated thrice. In the Sinai Horologion No. 864 of the 9th c., clearly intended only for cell use, there are appended to the present Psalms at the Ninth Hour four others: 68, 112, 110, 120 [LXX 68, 112, 110, 120 / MT 69, 113, 111, 121], and following them stand an additional 12 Psalms, presumably of the nocturnal office; the troparion is different from the present one (*Ἐν γαστρὶ ὡς τὸν ἕν... / Έν γαστρί ώς τον έν άηνεγκάστης Τριάδος*); there is already also a Theotokion, but different from the present one: **"Thou Who didst contain the Word in Thy womb"** (*Иже во чреве Слово вместившая*). In subsequent Horologia appear both our present Psalms and our present troparion with its Theotokion. The troparia following the Trisagion in ancient Horologia appear from the very beginning almost in their present form; variation exists only regarding the second troparion: thus, the Athos St. Andrew Horologion No. 12 of the 11th c. and Sinai No. 867 of the 14th c., as well as the Greek Typika of the Moscow Rumyantsev Museum (Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35) and the Moscow Synodal Library No. 381, lack the second troparion. The concluding prayer, which in the earliest manuscripts is entirely absent, is in the Vatopedi Horologion No. 350 of the 11th c. *(Orig. p. 479)* brief and similar to a troparion: 'Thou Who hanging upon the Tree didst yield Thy soul', while in the Athos St. Panteleimon Horologion No. 71 of the 12th–13th c. and in

=== LEAF p574 ===
the aforementioned Greek Typika—it is our present prayer of the Third Hour appointed also for both the Sixth and Ninth Hours; but in Sinai No. 865 of the 11th c. there appears already our present prayer and another special one. In the Athos St. Andrew Horologion No. 12 (11th c.), Sinai No. 866 (12th c.), and Vatopedi No. 352 (13th c.)—there is only our present prayer [^1964].

## The Typikon on the Ninth Hour Preceding Sunday

The celebration of the Ninth Hour on Saturday is presupposed by the Typikon to take place in the narthex, but it permits its celebration also in the temple; in the latter case the Ninth Hour, as merging more closely with Vespers, has no dismissal. Concerning the celebration of the Ninth Hour itself, the Typikon limits itself to the rubric: 'and the reader reads the Ninth Hour according to the custom' (the reading of the most important parts of Vespers and Matins is entrusted not to a reader, but to the superior or the ecclesiarch).

In the earliest of the currently known Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon, Moscow Rumyantsev Museum (Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35) of the 13th c., the description of Sunday services begins with Little Vespers. But the earliest Slavic manuscripts, for example, Moscow Synodal Library No. 328/383 of the 14th c., speak also of the Ninth Hour before Little Vespers: 'When the brethren are gathered in church, they chant the Ninth Hour' [^1965]. The present Old Believer Typikon (of the Edinoverie edition) speaks of the Ninth Hour like our own, only without the note for the case when the Ninth Hour is not celebrated in the narthex. The *Pomorian Answers* (*Pomorskie otvety*, an Old Believer polemical work of the early 18th c.) reproaches the Orthodox for celebrating the Ninth Hour together with Vespers, rather than before dinner prior to the Liturgy or immediately *(Orig. p. 480)* after it [^1966]. Such a celebration of the Ninth Hour was indeed prescribed by ancient Typika, but only for fasting days, so that the meal would occur as it were in the evening; such a direction is given, for example, by the earliest manuscript of the Studite Typikon [^1967].

Inasmuch as the Ninth Hour still belongs to the preceding liturgical day, the troparion and kontakion at it are read for the expiring day. This is evident from the direction of the Typikon concerning the Ninth Hour preceding St. Thomas Sunday: at it there are appointed the troparion **"Thou didst descend from on high"** (*С высоты снизшел еси*) and the kontakion **"Though Thou didst descend into the grave"** (*Аще и во гроб*) (and not **"When the tomb was sealed"** and **"With his curious right hand"**). By reason of this, there occurs within a single sequence of services at times a conjunction of the most diverse commemorations; thus, before Sunday Vespers of Meatfare Sunday there will be at the Ninth Hour a funeral troparion and kontakion, while before Sunday Vespers of the Sixth Sunday of Lent there will be the troparion **"That which was commanded secretly"** (*Повеленное тайно*), and the like.

# LITTLE VESPERS

## General Character of the Service

Vespers incorporated into the composition of the All-Night Vigil, and for that reason termed Great Vespers, is celebrated later than its customary time. It falls upon an hour designated rather for Compline than for Vespers. In order not to leave the customary hour of Vespers without its usual prayerful sanctification, Little Vespers is appointed for it. By omitting all litanies, as prayers that are most solemn and lengthen the service, the order of Little Vespers thereby, on the one hand, approaches the Hours and services homogeneous with them; on the other hand, through the singularity of its structure, through the predominance of changeable and festal portions of the service (stichera) over fixed ones, and given the comparative rarity of this service, Little Vespers receives a coloring no less festal than the Vigil itself.

## History

A rich development of festal ritual was required before the conception of such a service could arise. Certain manuscripts even of complete Jerusalem Typika of the 13th c., for example, the Georgian manuscript of the Shiomgvime Monastery, do not yet possess Little Vespers. But Greek Typika of the 13th c. (Moscow Sevastyanov Collection No. 491/35) and 14th c. (Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 456 and 381) and Slavic Typika of the 14th c., such as Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 328/383, 329/384, and 332/385, already contain it in its present form. Consequently, the service arose in the 12th–13th centuries.

*(Orig. p. 481)*
## The Opening Exclamation and Psalm

Notwithstanding that Little Vespers is preceded by the Ninth Hour (which ordinarily is without a dismissal), it begins with the exclamation **"Blessed is our God"** (*Благословен Бог наш*). In this respect Little Vespers is placed on a par with full Daily Vespers, which likewise has this exclamation notwithstanding that it is preceded by the Ninth Hour, whereas Great Lenten Vespers is joined to the Hours without an exclamation. The opening exclamation of Little Vespers, as of Daily and even Great Vespers, is pronounced before the Holy Doors (the exclamations of Matins and the Liturgy, in view of the greater

=== LEAF p576 ===
solemnity of those services, are pronounced before the Holy Table). After the exclamation, there is immediately read **"Amen. O come, let us worship..."**, inasmuch as the 'customary beginning' took place before the Ninth Hour. The Introductory [^1968] Psalm at Little Vespers is appointed to be read not by the superior, as at full Vespers (and as the Six Psalms at Matins), but by the reader [^1969], yet with the requirement that the reading be more solemn and reverent than ordinary: 'in a quiet (slow) and gentle (contrite—*ἡσύχῳ καὶ ἠρεμαίᾳ* / *ήσύχω καί ήρεмаία*) voice'. Following the customary conclusion of the evening Psalm: **"Glory... both now... Alleluia, alleluia, alleluia, glory to Thee, O God"** (three times); in place of the Great Litany there is a threefold **"Lord, have mercy"** (corresponding to the petitions of the litany) and **"Glory... both now..."** (corresponding to its exclamation); consequently, the Introductory Psalm receives the conclusion usual for Psalms at the Hours.

## Stichera at "Lord, I Have Cried"

At **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Господи воззвах*) there are appointed only 4 stichera—a number that full Vespers never has (at it the smallest number of stichera is 6). An even number of stichera is appointed in view of their chanting by two choirs. Such a number of stichera (4) is also appropriate for Vespers because the stichera thereby begin with the verse: **"From the morning watch until night"** [Ps. 129:6 / MT 130:6], which is most suitable for the evening hour.

Four stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** are appointed in the Evergetis Typikon (12th c.), the Typikon of the Monastery of St. Nicholas of Casole (13th c., Turin Library), and others for an Alleluia Vespers [^1970], that is, for the most ferial and penitential service.

The stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"** for Sunday Little Vespers are taken from Great Vespers; namely, the first three stichera serve as these (probably the most ancient). In order not to append to these stichera a heterogeneous sticheron (from the Anatolian stichera or the aposticha), the first sticheron is chanted twice to form the number of 4 (moreover, the repetition of stichera is a distinguishing mark of festal services).

*(Orig. p. 482)*
## The Little Dogmatika

As Theotokia at **"Lord, I have cried"** of Sunday Little Vespers there are appointed the so-called 'Little Dogmatika'. The Little Dogmatika, judging from their use only in such a comparatively late service as Little Vespers, must be of later origin than the Great Dogmatika, and therefore do not

=== LEAF p577 ===
belong to St. John of Damascus. This is proven also by internal evidence—their theological content.

In content, the Little Dogmatika are somewhat more complex than the Great Dogmatika. Having, like the latter, as their primary subject the dogma of the incomprehensible union of the two natures in Christ, they dwell, for example, also upon the manner of the union of the two wills in Christ (the Dogmatikon of Tone 8). In them there is also greater refinement of expression and, in general, more theological detail. For example, among Old Testament types there are mentioned also the closed gate of Ezekiel's temple [Ezek. 44:2] (Tones 2 and 5); the Most Holy Virgin is termed the 'treasury of the union of the two natures'; 'Emmanuel... thus came forth from Her womb as He entered through hearing' (Tone 2). There is an adaptation to the commemoration of the Resurrection of Christ (Tones 5 and 7). There are more poetic apostrophes; for example: 'Today is a virginal festival, brethren; let creation leap for joy, let nature dance' (Tone 1); 'O most great mystery! Beholding wonders, I proclaim the Godhead' (Tone 2); 'O strange wonders and most glorious mysteries! Who will not marvel, giving ear unto this?' (Tone 5).

## The Prokeimenon

Following the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, Little Vespers has the hymn **"O Joyful Light"** (*Свете тихий* / *Phos hilaron*) and the prokeimenon of Great Vespers, **"The Lord is King"** (*Господь воцарися*, Ps. 92:1 / MT 93:1), which is sung not 4 1/2 times, but 2 1/2 times. After it follows **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Сподоби Господи*) and the aposticha.

## Aposticha

The first sticheron of the aposticha at Little Vespers is in common with Great Vespers—a Resurrection sticheron. The remaining 3 stichera are in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos. Such stichera were assigned to Little Vespers on the same ground on which in the entire Sunday service whole sections are dedicated to the Most Holy Theotokos, such as: the dismissal Theotokion of Vespers, the third canon at Matins, and the katavasia **"I shall open my mouth"** (*Отверзу уста моя*). Celebrating the Resurrection, we simultaneously perform the 'divine and all-honorable' festival of the Mother of God, and Sunday is dedicated, together with the Saviour, to the honoring of His Mother, just as other days of the week are dedicated to other saints. Occurring frequently, the celebration of Sunday cannot so completely absorb the prayerful attention of the believer as do rare feasts, such as the Twelve Great Feasts, the offices of which are therefore entirely occupied with the celebrated event (in those services there are therefore no Theotokia even at 'Both now'). Constituting the subject of independent

=== LEAF p578 ===
glorification in the Sunday service alongside Her Son, the Mother of God in all the hymns dedicated to Her on Sunday is praised not in connection with the Resurrection (as, for example, in the Theotokia of the Paschal canon: **"Beholding Thy Son risen..."**), but from a gen*(Orig. p. 483)*eral point of view. So it is also in the present stichera. Specifically, the aposticha of Little Vespers now glorify the Mother of God, depicting a picture of the benefactions received by us through Her, now contain sorrowful, penitential supplications unto Her. At the same time, in the stichera of certain tones the first theme predominates, in others the second, but in the majority these themes alternate and are even combined within one and the same sticheron; the stichera of Tone 8 bear the most laudatory character (being composed of a series of 'Rejoice' acclamations); those of Tone 2 are the most sorrowful; at times (for example, Tone 5) the first sticheron is sorrowful, while the remainder are laudatory. The refrains to the stichera are those verses of Psalm 44 [LXX 44 / MT 45] that serve as refrains for the aposticha of the majority of feasts of the Mother of God (for example, the Nativity of the Theotokos): **"I shall remember Thy name..."** [v. 18 / MT 17]; **"Hear, O daughter, and see..."** [v. 11 / MT 10]; **"The rich among the people shall entreat Thy face..."** [v. 13 / MT 12]. As the sticheron at 'Glory... both now...', as in the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, there serves a 'Dogmatikon', consequently, a sticheron of more exalted content than those preceding it, and devoid of mournful themes.

## Troparion, Litany, and Dismissal

Following the aposticha at Little Vespers there immediately follows **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** (*Ныне отпущаеши* / *Nunc dimittis*), the Trisagion with the **"Our Father"**, the Sunday troparion with its Theotokion, and an abbreviated augmented litany (termed the 'Little Augmented Litany') consisting of 4 petitions: the initial (introductory), the petition for the Reigning House (without specifying the objects of petition), for the Holy Synod and the local Ruling Hierarch; while all the remaining petitions of the augmented litany are united in the concise: **"Again we pray for all the brethren and for all Christians"** (which is why the petition for the Holy Synod is without the addition: 'and for all our brethren in Christ'). This litany occupies at Little Vespers the same place as the augmented litany at Daily Vespers, that is, the very end of the service, as its most solemn part. An identical litany and in the same position is found in the initial, 'Royal' section of Matins.

From thence it was borrowed into Little Vespers, since this portion of Matins, as we shall see, is more ancient than Little Vespers. In turn, into that portion of Matins the litany was borrowed, probably, from the ancient *pannychis* (*παννυχίς*), an office that preceded Matins and for the most part consisted of: Psalm 50 [LXX 50 / MT 51], the canon, the Trisagion, and this litany, like the present service before the Paschal Matins, where there is likewise

=== LEAF p579 ===
such a litany.

Little Vespers concludes with a Little Dismissal, like the Hours, Compline, and Midnight Office, and with the Polychronion (*Mnogolietie*, **"The Most Pious, Autocratic..."**), as does every Vespers.

## Alterations in the Order from the Concurrence of Commemorations

The rich content of Little Vespers becomes still more varied when there coincides on Sunday any Great Feast or even a Medium (Polyeleos) feast. In that case the festal aposticha from Great Vespers, at which they cannot be chanted on account of the Sunday stichera, are transferred unto Little Vespers. Thus, if a Great Feast of the Mother of God *(Orig. p. 484)* or the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*) coincides with Sunday, their aposticha can be heard only at Little Vespers.

In the case of such a concurrence (of a feast, or of a great or polyeleos saint) with Sunday, Little Vespers undergoes also a series of other alterations in its composition. These alterations concern the stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**, the aposticha, and the troparion. At Little Vespers—though not on Sunday—there may even be a paremia (if the Meeting of the Lord coincides with Cheesefare Saturday). All these deviations of Sunday Little Vespers from its usual (set forth above) composition will be discussed in the rubrics for feasts whose concurrence with Sunday induces such alterations.

# THE EVENING REFECTORY

## Liturgical Character of the Refectory

According to the commandment of the Apostle: **"Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God"** [1 Cor. 10:31] [^1971], the Orthodox Church regards even the partaking of food as divine worship, in essence not differing from other church services. Therefore, among the rank of the latter, she possesses a special 'order' (*chin*), or church office of the refectory, which is why meals in monasteries are served in a church (a special refectory temple, *trapeznaya tserkov'*).

The view of the taking of food, or the refectory, as divine worship goes back to the Old Testament Church. There, one of the kinds of sacrifice, namely the so-called peace offering, concluded with a sacred meal from its remnants, which was held in the temple itself, in its outer court, where for this purpose there were special rooms, and even kitchens [^1972]. And among the early Christians, scarcely any meal was not Eucharistic [^1973].

## Two Meals

The Typikon of the Church (as also monastic practice in general) recognizes only two meals: the first after the Liturgy, and the second after Vespers (for fasting days, however—only one, after Vespers). The midday meal, as the principal one, has a more elaborate liturgical order, which, in view of the fact that it includes a special rite over the prosphora of the Mother of God (*Panagia*), is called the *Order of the Panagia* (*chin o panagii*). The order of the evening meal constitutes an abbreviation of this Order of the Panagia, the order of the midday meal.

*(Orig. p. 485)*
## The Order of the Evening Meal

Upon entering the refectory (it being presupposed—directly from church following Vespers), there is read ('we say') the verse from Psalm 21:27 [LXX 21:27 / MT 22:26]: **"The poor shall eat and be satisfied, and they shall praise the Lord (preeminently for being satisfied) that seek Him; their hearts shall live unto the ages of ages"** (*Ядят убозии и насытятся...*), that is, the soul is enlivened forever by the Lord, as the body is for a time by nourishment. By this prayer the attention of those approaching the refectory is directed toward spiritual food, and thanksgiving is offered
