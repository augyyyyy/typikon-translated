[^1614]: Concerning the beginning of monasticism on Athos there is no reliable information; Athonite traditions trace the emergence of monasteries here as far back as pagan times (Emperor Caracalla, 211–217 AD) or at least unto the flourishing of Egyptian-Palestinian asceticism; the Arab devastations of Athos circa 670 AD and subsequently circa 830 do not permit us to say anything certain concerning Athos prior unto the 9th century, although the foundation of nearly all present Athonite monasteries traces its origin back unto this most ancient period. The first historically attested Athonite hermit ('hesychast') was St. Peter (who lived on Athos, 681–734 AD); during the era of iconoclasm many monks sought refuge here; St. Euthymius (circa 840 AD) restored monasticism on Athos after the second Arab devastation; his disciples established the first cenobium on Athos—Vatopedi, and John Kolobos built the first stone monastery on the isthmus at Hierissos and obtained from Emperor Basil the Macedonian in 872 AD a chrysobull whereby Athos was granted unto the exclusive possession of monks. But thereupon enmity arose between the cenobites and anchorites; the latter separated and established in Karyes their own administration ('Protat') headed by the Protos. After a new Saracen devastation circa the middle of the 10th century, monasticism on Athos was restored by St. Athanasius, who founded the Great Lavra on Athos with donations from his friend Emperor Nikephoros Phokas. Following a complaint of the anchorites against the predominance of the cenobitic life, alien unto ancient Athos, Emperor John Tzimiskes by his Typikon (*τυπικόν* or *tragos*) of 971 AD recognized equal rights for both modes of ascetic life on Athos. Unto this period belong the first reliable accounts concerning the majority of present Athonite monasteries, such as Iveron (founded by Georgians), Docheiariou or Daphne, Panteleimon or Thessalonian, Esphigmenou (founded by inhabitants of the Thessalian village of Sphigou), Chilandar (named after the founder's surname), Zographou, founded by the Greek George the Iconographer (in Greek *zōgraphos*), Koutloumousiou (after the founder's name), Xeropotamou ('dry torrent'), and Dionysiou, founded by Alexios Komnenos in 1118 [or later by Alexios III in 1374] at the request of Dionysius who asceticized here. Emperor Constantine Monomachos by his Typikon of 1046 forbade Athos commercial relations with laity. From this time onward the present strict order became established on Athos (absolute exclusion of women). Alexios Komnenos in 1090 recognized Athos as independent of the Metropolitan of Thessalonica and subject solely unto its own Protos. In the 11th century Bulgarian monks appeared on Athos, settling the Zographou Monastery; in the 12th century Serbian monks (headed by Prince Stefan-Simeon Nemanja and his son St. Sabbas), unto whom Chilandar was granted; and Russian monks, unto whom was given first the Monastery of Xylourgou, and in 1169 also St. Panteleimon ('Rossikon'). The majority of Athonite monasteries are dedicated unto the Most Holy Theotokos; there are also churches of the Forerunner, Chrysostom, and Nicholas. There was on Athos (in the 10th century) likewise a Latin monastery with the Rule of St. Benedict—Karakallou (after the surname of the founder). (Bishop Porphyrius Uspensky, *Istoriia Afona* [History of Athos], Kyiv, 1876, *passim*; Sokolov, I., *Sostoianie monashestva v Vizantiiskoi tserkvi s pol. IX v. do nach.
