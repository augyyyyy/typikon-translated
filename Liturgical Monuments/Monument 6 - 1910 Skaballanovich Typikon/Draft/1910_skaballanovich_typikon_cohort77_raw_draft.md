...harmonizes in Tone (for symmetry) with the preceding troparion. Thus, if on Sunday—as *(Orig. p. 630)* is almost always the case—there occurs some commemoration with a troparion, the Theotokion is sung not in the Tone of the Sunday, but the Resurrection Theotokion (the so-called "first") is sung according to the Tone of the troparion of the saint. The Sunday Theotokion of the current Tone, remaining unsung because of this, is transferred unto the sessional hymns after the 1st Kathisma, replacing the Theotokion there—concerning which, indeed, the Typikon makes note already in Chapter 52. The same transfer, it goes without saying, must be made if a troparion is appointed in place of the Theotokion, as happens at the concurrence with Sunday of feasts of the Mother of God, forefeasts, afterfeasts, and certain other commemorations. In that same Chapter 52, it is noted that if on Sunday there be two saints, each with a distinct troparion, then the Sunday troparion is sung once, then the troparion to the 1st saint, **"Glory..."** (*Slava*)—to the other, **"Both now..."** (*I nyne*)—the Theotokion in the Tone of the last troparion.

### According to Ancient Typika (*Po drevnim ustavam*)

According unto the Studite (Alexian) Typikon as well, the Sunday troparion was sung twice and after it the Theotokion; but if a troparion to a saint was joined (which was done only for great saints), then it was sung once [^2417].

According unto the Evergetis Typikon, the troparion to the saint was, apparently, sung in place of the Theotokion.

It is noteworthy that in the Typikon of St. Nicholas of Casole (*Typikon Casulanum*) the troparion even at this place is called "dismissal" (*apolytic*) [^2418].

Of the Jerusalem Typika: the Georgian MS gives nothing; the Greek MSS prescribe: "We sing the troparion, doubling it; and at **Glory...** the troparion of the daily saint, if he be commemorated with a feast; but if not—the Theotokion of the tone, the 1st"; so likewise the printed Greek Typikon and Euchologion; the Slavic MSS and the Old Believer Typikon are like our present one, yet the oldest Slavic MSS add a rubric concerning the transfer of the Theotokion unto the kathisma.

In the East in the 17th century, troparia at Matins were sung, if they were not known by heart, with the assistance of a canonarch; **"Glory... Both now..."** was not sung, but was recited quickly, as in Russia as well [^2419].

### Sunday Troparia (*Voskresnye tropari*)

Replacing at certain services all other hymns, the troparion depicts in concise form the very essence of the celebrated event. This is likewise done by the Sunday troparia, sung at **"God is the Lord,"** at the Hours, and at the Divine Liturgy. Yet while some of them paint more a picture, the external side of the Resurrection (Tones 1, 4, 6), others...

...dwell upon its internal side; moreover, each successive troparion directs attention to ever different features of the event, drawing sufficient material for this from that sublime subject, so that not one of the troparia repeats the thoughts of another. The Theotokia to the troparia are dedicated predominantly to unfolding the mystery of the Incarnation of the Son of God, which became manifest from the time of His Resurrection, only occasionally dwelling upon His death and Resurrection (Tones 3, 6, 7, 8).

*(Orig. p. 631)*

### Their History (*Istoriya ikh*)

Judging from the fact that troparia are the most ancient part of festival services, known to that primary formation of divine worship represented also by the Typika of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th century, one might have expected that the Sunday troparia would be older than the stichera and canons, and that they appeared before St. John of Damascus; their anonymity likewise speaks in favor of this. Yet in surviving monuments they appear concurrently with the works of Sts. Cosmas and John of Damascus. Thus, together with certain of the latter works, they are indicated by a MS of the Jerusalem Patriarchal Library (No. 43 of the Monastery of the Holy Cross) containing the "Order of Passion and Paschal Week"; here they are placed as "sessional hymns" (*kathismata*) at the end of Vespers, after **"Now lettest Thou..."** (*Nyne otpushchaeshi*), beginning from the Vespers of Holy Saturday until Bright Friday, Tones 1–7 [^2420].

# THE PSALTER KATHISMATA AT MATINS (*Kafizmy utreni*)

With the troparion concludes the first, brief part of Matins, just as with it concludes Vespers and the whole of Matins (and the first part of the service of the Hour). The second part of Matins, like every service, begins with psalms and consists primarily of psalms and readings, interrupted only by litanies and a small number of hymns. For this abundance of psalms and readings Matins is indebted unto its nocturnal designation: by night, more than by day, prayer requires external support, which it finds in psalmody and edifying reading. Therefore Matins represents preeminently a service of psalms and readings. On the other hand, there has been transferred to Matins preeminently that part of divine worship which in antiquity consisted in the singing of the entire Psalter over the course of the daily cycle of services: the oldest rule concerning this distribution of psalms, the Sinaitic, assigns to Matins two-thirds of the entire Psalter—100 psalms, leaving for Vespers and the other services only one-third (Introductory Chapter, p. 218). Therefore in the present Typikon as well, out of the 20 Kathismata into which the Psalter is divided, the greater part falls upon Matins—two, and in certain periods of the year even three kathismata, whereas upon all the remaining services falls only one (at Vespers) or 3 kathismata for the entire daily cycle of the Hours (during Great Lent). Finally, of all the services, only at Matins does the Polyeleos occur, consisting of two full psalms. For this reason psalmody constitutes the most extensive and stable part of Matins; whereas for variable festive hymns (stichera, canons) the Typikon makes countless reductions in the psalmody of Vespers and the Hours, at Matins psalmody is never abbreviated, being retained even at Pascha at least as the Polyeleos at Matins of Bright Week (in the Greek Church) and as kathismata at its Vespers; psalmody at Matins is canceled only in exceptional cases: at the Matins of the great feasts (the Nativity of Christ, Theophany), when Great Compline (with its 6 psalms) precedes Matins and when it is joined with Vespers (at which a kathisma has already taken place); at the Matins of the first three days of Passion Week in view of the extraordinary abundance of readings (Gospels); and at the Matins of Holy Saturday for the sake of the Blameless (*Amomoi*, Psalm 118 [LXX; Masoretic 119], likewise a psalm). The completeness and stability of psalmody at Matins is all the more remarkable in that, judging by the content of the psalms, it is difficult to find suitable material in the Psalter for Matins: psalms possess for the most part an evening (penitential) character. In view of this, psalmody at Matins takes on more the character of a continuous, orderly reading of the entire Psalter ("regular kathismata") *(Orig. p. 632)* rather than a selection of psalms suited to the occasion (such a selection is made only for the beginning and end of Matins: the Six Psalms and the Praise Psalms). The Psalter at Matins is sung for the sake of its sacred text itself, which instills a religious disposition in general, as if to sanctify the beginning day by this divinely inspired word, as if to implore a blessing upon the impending day by holding in remembrance as many great divine truths as are scattered throughout the psalms. The Psalter is, as it were, laid as the foundation of the coming day, and is, as it were, confirmed by it.

### History of the Kathisma (*Istoriya kafizmy*)

*Kathisma* (Gr. κάθισμα) literally means "sitting." The word indicates that during this part of Matins one may sit. In the Greek Church to this day the parishioners sit at the beginning of the kathismata in the stalls (*stacidia*). Originally, however, they stood throughout the entire time of divine worship, so that standing (Gr. στάσις, *stasis*) was a constant characteristic of Christian worship; it was even considered the finest external expression of the essence of New Testament worship—the spirit of filial freedom before God, alien to servile fear; therefore the most ancient apostolic canon forbade kneeling not only on Sundays, but also on all days from Pascha until Pentecost, prescribing that on these days one pray unfailingly standing. Hence in the primary period of the development of the church order, which coincided with the greatest development of Christian enthusiasm (1st–3rd centuries) and proceeded without being under the predominant influence of monasticism (1st–4th centuries), the custom of sitting during psalmody could not have arisen. It appeared when in the services they began to strive to sing the entire Psalter within a single day and night or over a longer span. Cassian first mentions this custom in the 5th century (Introductory Chapter, p. 218), and the tone of justification in which he describes this custom of the Egyptian monks bears witness unto its novelty. Reports from the 6th–8th centuries, both...

=== LEAF p764 ===
...for the East and for the West, speak of sitting only during the (edifying) readings, and for the East standing is even emphasized during the singing of the entire Psalter at the nocturnal service: its sections in the well-known description of the Sinaitic Matins are called *staseis*, Gr. στάσις—standing, and it is stated that for their singing the monks stood up; and these sections (of 50 psalms each) according to their position at Matins correspond fully unto our kathismata. Standing during the kathismata is demanded also by the *Diatyposis* (see below, "Order of the Kathisma"; Introductory Chapter, pp. 295, 310). But transferred to the West, perhaps by Cassian, the custom of sitting during the psalms soon received wide dissemination there and passed over unto other parts of the service. In the East, by the 9th century there already exists the term *kathisma* (Gr. κάθισμα), which, however, is applied more, as among the Greeks today, to the hymns preceding the reading than to the sections of the Psalter, which are for the most part called *stichologiai* (Gr. αἱ στιχολογίαι [Orig. printed στιχολογίοα]), while the "Glories" of the kathismata are called *staseis* [^2421]; yet already in the *Hypotyposis* they are called *kathisma psalms* (Gr. κάθισμα ψαλμούς), and in the *Diatyposis* simply *kathisma* (Gr. κάθισμα) [^2422]. A trace of such a practice—sitting only during the readings and standing during the psalms of the kathismata—remains in the present Typikon as well: sessional hymns are appointed only after those kathismata which are followed by readings; thus, sessional hymns are not appointed after the kathismata of Vespers, which are not followed by readings; conversely, sessional hymns are appointed even without kathismata before readings, as, for example, after Ode 3 of the Canon.

### The Order of the Kathisma (*Chin kafizmy*)

The execution of the kathismata at Matins differs considerably from Vespers, both Great and daily. "The choir saith: **Lord, have mercy** (3). **Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit.** The reader: **Both now and ever, and unto the ages of ages**, and chanteth the kathisma of the Psalter" (*stikhologisuet kafismu Psaltira*). At Vespers, as we have seen, the kathisma is not preceded by **"Lord, have mercy,"** **"Glory... Both now..."**—this is because there the kathisma follows immediately after the Great Ektenia, where **"Lord, have mercy"** is repeated far more than 3 times, and where there is its own doxology in the exclamation, more extensive than this present lesser one. Concerning the division of the kathisma into parts, the Typikon nowhere speaks in detail, mentioning it only in passing in the order of Matins for the 1st Monday of Great Lent. In the Psalter with Order (*Psaltir' sledovannaya*), the kathisma is divided into three parts designated by the word "Glory" (*Slava*); this designation, unexplained in the liturgical books, is in practice considered equivalent to that doxology wherewith are divi- *(Orig. p. 633)* -ded the psalms of the Six Psalms, wherewith the psalms of the Hours conclude, and which consists of: **"Glory... Both now...; Alleluia, alleluia, alleluia, glory to Thee, O God** (3); **Lord, have mercy** (3); **Glory... Both now..."** In practice, it is accepted at the...

=== LEAF p765 ===
...kathismata for the reader to recite the beginning of this doxology: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit,"** the singers the middle, and the reader the end: **"Both now and ever, and unto the ages of ages"** (in the second lesser doxology) [^2423]. Such a division is made in order to link the kathisma more closely with prayerful invocation and to instill the thought that the reader and the choir vie with each other in glorifying God (they cannot wait for the end of the doxology). At the conclusion of the kathisma, if an ektenia follows it, at Sunday Matins and on other festive and semi-festive days, the doxology, now recited entirely by the reader, concludes (as is accepted by practice) with **"Alleluia, alleluia, alleluia, glory to Thee, O God,"** since it is followed by the Little Ektenia, wherein there is both **"Lord, have mercy"** and an extensive doxology.

— Of the kathismata at Sunday Matins, there are appointed the following regular ones after the 1st, sung at Vespers: the 2nd and 3rd, just as the regular kathismata are not omitted for any feasts (except Pascha), thereby instilling the thought that the singing through of the entire Psalter in designated periods, established by the ascetics, must not be interrupted even for feasts.

### Ancient Singing of the Kathismata (*Prezhnee penie kafizm*)

Both the Studite and ancient Jerusalem Typika required the singing, and not the reading, of the kathismata, a trace whereof has remained in the present Typikon, in its indefinite expressions regarding the execution of the kathismata: "the choir saith," "the reader" (it does not say: "readeth") "stichologizeth"—reciteth by verses, consequently, alternately with someone else. According unto the Studite-Alexian Typikon, the kathismata were not only always sung to a tone, but within the limits of the tone there were, apparently, different melodies for them, "alleluiaria," 9 for each tone; for certain kathismata, moreover—besides the 1st, also for the 7th, 19th, and 20th—these melodies were particularly intricate and solemn (Introductory Chapter, p. 365). The oldest manuscript of the Alexian-Studite Typikon has a section: "Alleluiaria of the kathismata of the eight tones. Tone 1 (and further, all under neumatic notation): **I will give thanks unto Thee, O Lord** (the beginning of the 1st Glory of the 2nd Kathisma) *alleluia*. **Save me, O Lord** (beginning of the 2nd Glory) *alleluia*. **Lord, who shall abide** (beginning of the 3rd Glory), *alleluia* (3), *glory to Thee, O God*." Further, in the same manner are cited under musical notation the opening words of each Glory of the 3rd and 4th Kathismata with the same refrains; but on that same leaf of the Typikon begin the Gradual Antiphons (*anabathmoi*) with musical notation [^2424]; consequently, the kathismata were intentionally not finished: of the 8 tones, notation is given only for Tone 1, and within its bounds only for the 2nd–4th Kathismata. A Psalter of the Sophia Library at the St. Petersburg Theological Academy, MS No. 62/Soph. of the 13th century, cites only half of each verse in a psalm; consequently, it was designated for one of the two choirs and...

When the singing of the kathismata was abandoned among us and in the East, it is difficult to say: a transition to their reading was, perhaps, their singing by a single chanter. The Stoglav Council already permits them "not to be sung, but to be chanted by verses (*stikhologisati*)."

As regards the interpolations between the psalms: according unto St. John Cassian (5th century), in the West each psalm was sung by some one person, and at the end of it all standing sang loudly the lesser doxology, whereas in the East they concluded the antiphon with it, that is, [a group of] psalms (Introductory Chapter, p. 217); Pope Vigilius († 546) and the Council of Narbonne of 589 (according to Baronius) mention this same custom. According unto Canon 12 of the Council of Toledo of 633: "at the end of all the psalms we say: Glory and honor to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, unto ages of ages, amen" [^2427]. Alleluia as a refrain to the psalms was common among the ascetics, being inserted, for example, as even now frequently at the kathismata, after every three psalms [^2428], or between designated sections of a psalm; but, according to Cassian, in the Egyptian monasteries Alleluia was sung as a refrain only to the Alleluia psalms (Introductory Chapter, pp. 190, 393, 218). In both sources are given such "antiphons" to the psalms besides **"Alleluia"**: **"Direct"** (*Ispravi*), **"Save us, O Lord,"** **"Preserve me, O Lord,"** **"Hear me, O Lord,"** **"Have mercy upon me, O Lord,"** **"Help me, O Lord,"** **"Protect me** (or: **us**), **O Lord,"** **"Be gracious unto me, O Lord,"** **"Have compassion upon me, O Lord,"** **"Remember me, O Lord."** For the combination of **"Alleluia"** with **"Glory"** a parallel exists in Abyssinian worship: "Alleluia to the Father, Alleluia to the Son, Alleluia to the Holy Spirit" [^2432]. — **"Lord, have mercy"** in the interpolated refrain at the kathismata has as its prototype the prayer wherewith from antiquity each psalm concluded (Introductory Chapter, pp. 189, 207, 217, 393); in the Vatican Library there is a Syriac "priestly book with prayers at Vespers, the nocturnal office, and Matins before and after each psalm" [^2433].

Gradually these prayers became shorter and began to be pronounced not after every psalm, but several times; as such a prayer at the conclusion of psalmody the *Diatyposis* (in certain MSS) indicates: "Lord, have mercy; Christ, have mercy" (according to other MSS, **Lord, have mercy** 20 times); judging by the use of this invocation in the West, where *Kyrie eleison* alone without *Christe eleison* is not used, one might think that this was the original form of the brief Psalter prayer; a transition unto our present form is the Abyssinian: "Lord, have mercy, O Christ" (cf. the Jesus Prayer). According unto the *Diatyposis*: "at the conclusion of the reading (between the kathismata), standing up (consequently, they sat only during the reading), we say 12 times **Lord, have mercy**, and thus psalmody begins again" [^2434]; this, presumably, replaced our **"Lord, have mercy"** in the pauses of the kathisma, before it and after it—in all likewise 12 times.

# THE SESSIONAL HYMNS AFTER THE KATHISMATA (*Sedal'ny po kafizmakh*)

With the kathismata are inseparably linked, as we shall see below, the (patristic) readings. The preparation for these readings, the transition unto them from psalmody, is served by the sessional hymns (*sedal'ny*). The very name "Sessional Hymn" indicates the connection of these hymns with the readings: sessional hymns (Gr. κάθισμα, *kathisma*) are that during which one sits; yet during the sessional hymns themselves the Typikon does not permit sitting, permitting sitting only during the readings: "After the kathisma, the sessional hymn... and we sit and read... in the commentary..." (order of Sunday Matins). Thus, the sessional hymns received their name not from being sung while sitting, but from anticipating the readings during which one sits; they are the introduction unto the readings, a sessional, so to speak, prelude (just as the Lucernarium psalms and prayers received their name from the kindling of the lamps performed after them). Indeed, according unto other Typika, they sat during the sessional hymns themselves as well (see below).

In accordance with such a meaning of theirs, the sessional hymns are appointed in order to give rest unto the worshipers from long standing during the psalmody and to direct their thought unto a definite subject of remembrance before listening to an extensive reading concerning that subject. At Sunday Matins, where sessional hymns follow after two kathismata, they prepare the worshipers for the comprehension of the mystery of the Resurrection of Christ, of which will be spoken in the readings. In view of this, the Sunday sessional hymns according to their content represent reflections upon the Resurrection of Christ, and for this reason they differ from the Sunday troparia, which depict the very event of Christ's Resurrection. The sessional hymns are a reverent meditation upon the mystery of the Resurrection, an elucidation of its significance for the redemption of humanity, for the crushing of Hades and victory over death; they convey feelings of joyful wonder before the incomprehensibility of the accomplished miracle and of gratitude toward its Accomplisher.

### Melodies of the Sessional Hymns (*Napevy sedal'nov*)

In accordance with such a meditative content of the sessional hymns is also their melody. First of all, the customary melody of the sessional hymns differs somewhat from the melody of the troparia. It is, above all, more protracted than the troparion melody; this is because sessional hymns are for the most part sung once, at most twice, whereas the troparion is performed in the course of the daily services up to 7, 12, and even 16 times. In addition, a sessional hymn is easier to listen to, since it is listened to while sitting, and its protracted singing is not so tiring for the worshipers as such singing of a troparion would be. Besides its protractedness, the melody of the Sunday sessional hymns is distinguished from the troparion melody by greater...

=== LEAF p769 ===
...evenness and tranquility; from them breathes, moreover, a certain sorrow concerning the burial of the Savior; although they glorify the Resurrection of the Savior, like the troparia, yet this glorification passes in them through the remembrance of the burial and death of the Savior (**"Life lay in the grave and a seal was set upon the stone; as a sleeping King, soldiers guarded Christ"**—sessional hymn of Tone 6 after the 2nd Kathisma; cf. **"The noble Joseph"**—sessional hymn of Tone 2); for this reason the melodies of the sessional hymns approximate in character the melodies of stichera, full of deep contemplation and tender contrition. In general, they dispose the soul more unto tranquil meditation, such as is required for the impending readings, and give more rest to the soul than the joyful, but stirring melodies of the troparia. They serve also as a transition from the joyful hymns of **"God is the Lord"** and the troparia unto the readings, which constitute the least solemn and most monotonous part of the Vigil. Of the merit of the Sunday sessional hymns according to their melody speaks also the fact that these melodies are independent and have no model melodies (*prosomoia*, *podobny*), with the exception of the sessional hymns after the 2nd Kathisma: Tone 1 is patterned on the model **"While the stone was sealed"** (*Kameni zapechatannu*), Tone 3 on **"Awed by the beauty of thy virginity"** (*Krasote devstva*, the Theotokion to the sessional hymn), Tone 4 on **"Speedily anticipate"** (*Skoro predvari*, the Theotokion of the Sixth Hour on Great Lent Wednesdays and Fridays, taken as mournful in contrast to the joyful troparion melody of Tone 4).

*(Orig. p. 637)*

As regards the comparative merit of the sessional hymns according to their content with other hymnographic compositions, in this respect they stand on an equal footing with the troparia and surpass the stichera and canons in poetic height. In places they strike by the power of their expressions (**"Death was fiercely despoiled,"** **"The tomb opened, Hades weeping,"** **"No longer, O death, dost thou hold dominion"**—Tone 3 after the 1st Kathisma), by the vividness of depiction (Hades saith: **"I tremble at the flesh of the incorruptible hypostasis, I behold the Invisible One secretly contending against me, therefore even they whom I hold cry aloud: Glory, O Christ, to Thy Resurrection!"**—Tone 3 after the 2nd Kathisma), and by the depth of psychological analysis of the persons involved in the event of the Resurrection (the Myrrh-bearing Women at the tomb think within themselves: **"Can it be that He is risen, Who even before the Passion proclaimed the Resurrection?"**; after the Resurrection, **"Peter cried aloud unto Thee: The women took courage, but I was afraid; the thief theologized, but I denied Thee; wilt Thou, then, call me henceforth to be a disciple, or wilt Thou show me again as a fisher of the deep?"**—Tone 5 after the 2nd Kathisma; Mary Magdalene saith unto the Jews: **"Either give me my Lord, or cry aloud with me: O Lord, glory to Thee!"**—Tone 6 after the 1st Kathisma). Of the high merit of the Sunday sessional hymns speaks also the fact that several of them serve as troparia or sessional hymns of the greatest feasts: of Holy Saturday—**"The noble Joseph,"** **"Unto the Myrrh-bearing Women"** (with additions); of St. Thomas Sunday—**"While the tomb was sealed."**

In view of such merits of the Sunday sessional hymns according to their melodies and content, they are never canceled at the Sunday service,...

=== LEAF p770 ===
...even upon the concurrence with Sunday of the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*) and the Twelve Great Feasts of the Mother of God; they are then sung in their entirety, even with their Theotokia, whereas at least the latter it would have been natural to replace with the sessional hymns of the feast.

Of Sunday sessional hymns after each kathisma two are appointed, just as on great feasts after each kathisma there are two sessional hymns, or else, if one, it is sung twice, evidently with the aim of affording singing unto each of the two choirs. In this connection they are linked by a refrain, so that the first is sung "simply" and the second with a refrain. As such a refrain for the sessional hymns after the 1st Kathisma serves the verse of Psalm 9:33 [LXX; Masoretic 10:12], as it were inviting or summoning the Savior unto the Resurrection: **"Arise, O Lord my God, let Thy hand be lifted up, forget not Thy poor unto the end"**; and after the 2nd Kathisma—the opening verse of that same psalm—the vow of the faithful for the fulfillment of this sublime prayer of theirs: **"I will give thanks unto Thee, O Lord, with my whole heart, I will tell of all Thy marvelous works"** (these same verses are the prokeimenon and its verse for Tone 7). Out of the series of psalmic verses that can be applied unto the Resurrection of the Savior, precisely these were selected for this present place, because the direct glorification of the Resurrection has not yet begun at Matins (it begins only with the Gospel and **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"**), and because here a somewhat mournful and penitential tone is required (**"forget not Thy poor"**). Unto the two sessional hymns after each kathisma is joined, through **"Glory... Both now..."**, a Theotokion, which customarily concludes every series of Sunday hymns, as well as hymns in honor of the saints.

The Theotokia of the sessional hymns, like the Theotokia of the troparia, are dedicated unto the mystery of the Incarnation and just as rarely as those dwell upon the Resurrection *(Orig. p. 638)* of the Savior, upon the relationship of the Mother of God unto that event; the latter occurs in the Theotokia of Tone 2 after the 1st and 2nd Kathismata, Tone 6 after the 2nd Kathisma, Tone 7 after the 1st and 2nd Kathismata, and partly Tone 1 after the 2nd Kathisma and Tone 5 after the 1st Kathisma. Some of these present Theotokia strike by the power of their expressions, the depth and originality of their thoughts; such are, for example, the Theotokia of Tones 3 and 4 after the 1st Kathisma: the 1st, **"Awed by the beauty of thy virginity"** (*Krasote devstva*), conveying the thought that at the spiritual beauty of the Most Holy Virgin Gabriel himself was awestruck and was at a loss what to say unto Her, how to name Her; the 2nd, **"Joseph was astonished"** (*Udivisya Iosif*), depicting the feelings and reflections of Joseph at the Nativity of Christ, how he had to recall the types of the seedless birth (the fleece and the bush) and, being convinced, began to prove unto the priests of Jerusalem (who, it is implied, had brought an accusation against him for failure to fulfill the obligation he had assumed) that a seedless birth is possible.
