preceded Matins and, having after itself also a reading, together with it and with Matins constituted a single 'vigil' (*agrypnia* / ἀγρυπνία) [^1662]; in this the Evergetis Typikon drew close unto the Jerusalem Typikon, toward which it inclines in other cases as well.

### The Jerusalem Typikon According unto Nikon of the Black Mountain

From the 11th century we possess the first report regarding the existence and dissemination in the East of more or less complete texts of Typika in the strict sense according unto the order of the Palestinian Lavra of St. Sabas (near Jerusalem). This report belongs unto Nikon of the Black Mountain, who was at first a certain official ('instructor' / *nastavnik*), and later hegumen in the monasteries of Rhaithou on *(Orig. p. 370)* Sinai and of St. Symeon the Wonderworker of the Admirable Mountain upon the Black Mountain near Antioch during the reign of Emperor Constantine Doukas (1059–1067), and who personally did much both in general for the development of the church Typikon and, in particular, for the replacement of the Studite Typikon by the Jerusalem Typikon [^1663]. Prompted by disorders in the monasteries of that era (disobedience toward superiors, striving toward idiorrhythmic / kellion living as granting greater freedom, discord among liturgical orders), Nikon resolved to provide a written manual for monastics, a consolidated typikon or *Taktikon*, through the collation of diverse typika. During the preliminary work for this composition, there was revealed unto Nikon not only the existence of a multitude of diverse typika—such as that of the Great Church, the Studite, the Jerusalem, the Hagiorite (Athonite), the typikon of the Patriarchate of Antioch, of the Monastery of St. Auxentius, and others (not to mention the ancient rules of St. Pachomius, Basil the Great, and Cassian)—but also a significant discrepancy among them, even between the different recensions of one and the same typikon: 'I read and collected diverse Typika, Studite and Jerusalem, and they did not agree one with another, neither a Studite with another Studite, nor a Jerusalem with another Jerusalem' (in all likelihood, Nikon had at hand ktetorial typika) [^1664]. For the resolution of his perplexities, Nikon turned unto the 'experienced fathers' in the monastery of St. Sabas, and upon their counsel 'took care concerning the ancient Jerusalem' Typikon [^1665]; the hegumen of this monastery directed him for an accurate manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon unto Laodicea (evidently in the Lavra of St. Sabas they were guided, as in part at present upon Mount Athos, by an unwritten Typikon), and lo, 'at the Savior in Laodicea,' recounts Nikon, 'we found preserved in the library an ancient Jerusalem Typikon, which the blessed Nicodemus, former hegumen of St. Sabas (chronology unknown), set forth for the Metropolitan of Laodicea from the beginning; and that one I took, as thou knowest, and I have it here at St. Symeon the Wonderworker (upon the Black Mountain), so that those who wish copy from it. All the others, as we have said,

=== LEAF p352 ===
were found incorrect and do not equate unto the ancient one. Likewise did I find the Studite typika diverse, and neither do they equate one with another; and on account of this inequality, and still more because they possessed not any testimonies from the Divine Scriptures, but merely spoke thus: that we perform this and that' [^1666].

*(Orig. p. 371)* Thus Nikon accords preference unto the Jerusalem Typikon over the Studite: (a) on account of the possibility of utilizing its ancient and complete manuscript; (b) because the Jerusalem Typikon substantiates its prescriptions upon the 'Divine Scriptures' and does not impart them, like the Studite, without motivation—an example of which Nikon points out further in the stricter rules of fasting according unto the Jerusalem Typikon, which are consonant with the canons of the holy Apostles, councils, and ancient ascetics (for example, regarding the non-mitigation of the fast on Wednesday and Friday on account of feasts, and regarding the hour of breaking the fast on Great Saturday). In view of such advantages of the Jerusalem Typikon, Nikon also labored for its dissemination, and composes his consolidated rule ('Typikon', *Taktikon*) in its spirit, noting nevertheless in places also the Studite practice, and occasionally borrowing prescriptions from the Hagiorite Typikon. (The disciplinary portion of this *Typikon* of Nikon was subsequently incorporated into the Slavonic manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon and exists in our present Typikon).

### The Domain and Recensions of the Jerusalem Typikon

Thus in the 11th century and earlier, the Jerusalem Typikon enjoyed dissemination not only in Palestine [^1667], but also in the Patriarchate of Antioch and Asia Minor. Around this time, if not earlier, the Jerusalem Typikon was adopted upon Sinai, as is evident from its manuscripts [^1668] and other liturgical books arranged according unto this Typikon in the Sinai Library, occasionally dating back unto the 10th century and earlier [^1669], although the Jerusalem Typikon that developed upon Sinai exhibits certain peculiarities in comparison with the genuine Jerusalem one [^1670]. Somewhat later (in the 12th century), in all probability, the Jerusalem Typikon was adopted in the Diocese of Constantinople, judging by the fact that Nikon says nothing regarding its dissemination there. Yet Patriarch Nicholas III Grammatikos of Constantinople (1084–1111), in his 'Epistle unto the Protos of the Holy Mountain' [^1671], resolves the perplexities of the Athonite monastics—arising upon the grounds of the conflict between the less strict Studite practice concerning fasts and the stricter Jerusalem practice—in the spirit of *(Orig. p. 372)* the latter. The ktetorial Typikon given by Empress Irene, consort of Emperor Alexios Komnenos († 1183), unto the Monastery of the Mother of God, Full of Grace (*Kecharitomene*), founded by her in Constantinople—which in its liturgical portion refers in general unto

=== LEAF p353 ===
the 'Synaxarion' (generally accepted), presupposes Jerusalem practice: *agrypniai* (vigils) and inter-hours (*mesoria*) are mentioned [^1672]. Unto the Palestinian Typikon the Typikon of Neilos, Hegumen of the Monastery of the Mother of God of Machairas, likewise refers [^1673]. The Typikon of the Monastery of the Archangel Michael upon Mount St. Auxentius near Chalcedon, given unto it by its founder, Emperor Michael Palaiologos (1250–1282), requires that psalmody be celebrated according unto the ancient custom of the Palestinian monasteries 'or according unto the Jerusalem Typikon,' except for Sunday vigils, as being exceedingly burdensome [^1674]. The consolidation of the Jerusalem Typikon in Constantinople was, in all likelihood, facilitated by the devastation of the Studion Monastery by the Crusaders, from which it was restored only by Emperor Constantine Palaiologos in 1298. Around this same time, in the 12th century, the Jerusalem Typikon found dissemination upon Mount Athos. Even if at the beginning of this century the Jerusalem regimen of fasting still seemed strange unto the Athonites, in this very century there appear upon Mount Athos Menaia of Jerusalem liturgical structure, several copies of which were brought from there by Arseny Sukhanov unto Russia and are preserved at present in the Moscow Synodal (former Patriarchal) Library (MSS Nos. 446–451: Jan., Mar., June, Aug., Oct., Dec.). As upon Sinai, the Jerusalem Typikon upon Mount Athos acquired certain local peculiarities, retaining at times the Studite practice deeply rooted there (for example, regarding fasts) and forming a distinct form of the Hagiorite Typikon [^1675]. — From the 12th century is likewise known the first manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon *(Orig. p. 373)* for Georgia, namely written for the monastery of St. Shio of Mghvime (near Tiflis) in 1172, in all probability from an original of the Wonderworking Mountain Monastery, and preserved in a 13th-century copy bearing the title: 'Typikon of the Church Office of the Well-Ordered Lavra of the Holy and God-Bearing Sabas; Such Also Occurs in All Other Monasteries of Palestine.' Among published manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon this is the most ancient (there is no Little Vespers; the Markian Chapters are without the name of Mark and are exceedingly brief; there are no commemorations of: the Holy Mandylion [*Ubrus*], established in 944; Athanasius of Athos, † 1000; Lazarus of Mount Galesios, † 1053; Michael Choniates, † 1266 [read 1222]; or even George the Chozebite of the 7th century) [^1676]. In the 13th–14th centuries the Jerusalem Typikon attains the widest dissemination throughout the entire East, as is evident from a series of its Greek manuscripts preserved from these centuries, such as, for example, its most ancient manuscript in Russia, yielding little unto the Shiomghvime manuscript, in the Moscow Public (Rumyantsev) Museum from the Sevastyanov Collection No. 35/491 (13th c.); the Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library, Greek Department No. 381, written, judging by the names in the diptychs, under Patriarch Athanasius of Constantinople (1289–1332); the Typikon of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 456 with the dubious date 1298, more likely 14th century; and the Typikon of the Vatican Library No. 784, written in 1317 in *Tēphrei tēs chōras Rōsias* (in Russia, in Tver?) [^1677]. How *(Orig. p. 374)* widely disseminated in Greece the Jerusalem Typikon had become by the 14th century is attested by the so-called *Diataxis tēs hierodiakonias* (Order of the Holy Ministry / *Chin svyashchennosluzheniya*), attributed unto

Patriarch Philotheus of Constantinople, the celebrated 14th-century liturgist, yet having appeared possibly even earlier [^1678]; appointed, evidently, for the widest universal application, it provides the Jerusalem order of the All-Night Vigil, striving to supply the omissions of ancient manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon concerning the liturgical actions of the priest and deacon during divine worship [^1679]. In Serbia, the first complete text of the Jerusalem Typikon was its translation in 1319, executed by Archbishop Nicodemus, former archimandrite of the Hilandar Monastery, from a Greek original obtained from the Patriarch of Constantinople (not from Mount Athos, where the Jerusalem Typikon existed not in its pure form), from the Monastery of the Forerunner (in all probability the Studion, where consequently the Jerusalem Typikon had already been introduced) [^1680]. Prior unto this time, Serbia was guided by the Hilandar Typikon, unto which there is an implicit reference in the preface unto Nicodemus's Typikon. In 1331, Nicodemus's Typikon was transcribed also for Hilandar by a certain Roman [^1681]. Following Nicodemus's Typikon, there appeared in Serbia a series of other Typika, for the most part close unto it [^1682]. — Unto the 14th century likewise belong the most ancient Slavonic translations of the Jerusalem Typikon made in Russia, such as Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 328/383 of the 14th century (their Russian origin is evident from the presence of several Russian saints and the absence of South Slavonic ones), exceedingly close both in contents and still more in the structure of the daily office unto ancient Greek and Serbian codices. The appearance in Russia of complete manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon was preceded by the gradual penetration of its practice from Greece, beginning from the 13th century. Unto the Synod of Vladimir in 1274 and that of Constantinople in 1276, Russian bishops already express perplexities concerning certain Studite customs (for example, regarding the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts on Cheesefare Wednesday and Friday and on Great Friday) and receive an answer in the spirit of the Jerusalem Typikon [^1683]. Yet even in the 15th century Studite practice persists in Russia, as shown by manuscripts of the Studite Typikon from that century and the queries of the Pskov clergy unto St. Metropolitan Photius (1410–1431) [^1684]. Unto the dissemination of the Jerusalem Typikon in Russia, *(Orig. p. 375)* St. Metropolitan Cyprian (1380–1406 with interruption) contributed greatly through his (manuscript) editions of the 'Horologion according unto the Typikon of the Lavra of our Venerable Father Sabas in Jerusalem' (the Sequenced Psalter / *Sledovannaya Psaltir*) and the *Sluzhebnik* of 1397, into which was incorporated the *Diataxis* of Patriarch Philotheus [^1685]. Under Cyprian as well, the first attempt at a Russian recension of the Jerusalem Typikon was made in the form of the *Tserkovne Oko* (*Oko Tserkovnoye*) by Hegumen Athanasius of Vysotsky, translated by him in the Peribleptos Monastery at Constantinople and furnished with additions—among others, disciplinary articles from Nikon of the Black Mountain [^1686]. This endeavor laid the foundation for an entire series of Russian recensions of the Jerusalem Typikon, concluding only with the appearance of its printed edition [^1687].

### The Character of the Daily Office According unto the Jerusalem Typikon

In contrast unto the Studite Typika, the Jerusalem Typika present a uniform structure of Vespers and Matins, identical with our present one. Although this is explained partly by the later date of these Typika, yet if one takes into consideration that this structure coincides also with that given by the Holy Sepulchre Typikon of the 9th century (the century unto which belong also the earliest monuments of the Studite Typikon) and at times remains truer than the Studite unto the ecclesiastical practice of the 4th–5th centuries (litiyas, as in Silvia/Egeria; vigils, which in the 4th–5th centuries occurred at least several times a year), then we may recognize as the first valuable characteristic in this domain—and one that contributed unto the ascendancy of the Jerusalem Typikon over the Studite—a greater stability, immutability, and fidelity unto antiquity in the daily office in comparison with the Studite Typikon. Another equally advantageous characteristic (from a Christian-ascetic perspective) of the Jerusalem Typikon was the greater duration of church services according unto it, which depended upon the greater proximity of this Typikon (both geographical and spiritual) and its fidelity unto the traditions of ancient asceticism (a characteristic manifested still more vividly in the comparatively greater strictness of this Typikon in its fasting regimen); most noticeably regarding the daily office, this characteristic revealed itself in the indispensable celebration of the Hours, which were not omitted for any feasts whatsoever. In relation unto the most important daily services—Vespers and Matins—this characteristic of the Jerusalem Typikon was manifested above all in a greater number of kathismata: at Matins there was always one kathisma more than according unto the Studite Typikon, except during Great Lent, Saturdays, Sundays, and feast days, when the number of kathismata according unto both Typika was identical. If one recalls that among Eastern monasticism there once existed a tendency *(Orig. p. 376)* to chant the entire Psalter every twenty-four hours (see above, pp. 294–295, the narrative of the visit unto Nilus of Sinai by John and Sophronius, and p. 299, 'Armenian Liturgy'), then in this characteristic of the Jerusalem Typikon as well one cannot fail to perceive its greater fidelity unto the traditions of asceticism, which attributed such paramount significance unto the Psalter (see above, pp. 189 ff.). Connected with this is likewise the greater number of stichera and troparia according unto the Jerusalem Typikon: the latter permits at **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Gospodi vozzvakh*) 10 stichera, and at the canon usually 14 and sometimes 16 verses, heirmoi, and troparia, whereas in the Studite Typika the maximum for the former was 9, and for the latter 14, usually 12. The same must be said regarding vigils: under the influence precisely of ascetic practice, with its customary all-night prayers, the Jerusalem Typikon, in place of the pre-dawn Matins customary in antiquity for Sundays and feast days, introduced all-night vigils (*agrypniai*) for both. Not unconnected with that same spirit of asceticism was the melodic simplification of divine worship in the Jerusalem Typikon: here drop away the requirements of the Studite Typikon,

=== LEAF p356 ===
which constituted within it the heritage of the Sung Office (*asmatike akolouthia*), regarding the special, melodic execution of kathismata [^1688], hypakoai, and the like. — Other characteristics of the Jerusalem Typikon depended upon its place of origin: the Holy Land. From this perspective an explanation is provided for the litiyas which this Typikon requires, as was the case in Jerusalem practice as early as the 4th–5th centuries (see above, pp. 140 ff.), for every Vespers and Matins. The homeland of this Typikon, which compelled a more intense reliving of the celebrated sacred events, manifested itself also in the fact that the Jerusalem Typikon establishes a far sharper distinction between weekday and festal worship than does the Studite. This distinction is achieved—leaving aside vigils—on the one hand by the addition unto festal services of the Polyeleos (*Polyeleos*), whereas the Studite Typikon in such cases merely substituted certain (likewise chanted) kathismata for others; and on the other hand by the so-called 'Great Doxology,' which constitutes a technical term designating a completely different conclusion of Matins for feasts in comparison with weekdays: it signifies the transposition of the prayer **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (*Spodobi, Gospodi*) into direct conjunction with the Great Doxology, and the fusion of the aposticha stichera with the stichera at the Praises for feast days.

### Vespers and Matins of Mixed Type

All these, although characteristic, are not exceedingly significant peculiarities in the structure of the daily office, more specifically in the order of Vespers and Matins. In general, these two services in both Typika possess one and the same order and structure, diverging with almost equal *(Orig. p. 377)* sharpness from the so-called 'Sung Office' (*asmatikos typos*), or the court-cathedral Typikon of Constantinople. This latter order not only persisted intact in many churches until the 15th century, but also strove to assimilate unto itself the monastic, Studite-Jerusalem Vespers and Matins. The imprint of such a tendency is borne by the orders of Vespers and Matins in certain Euchologia of the 12th–16th centuries. Vespers in them exhibits the following peculiarities: According unto the Euchologia of the 12th century (1153) of the Sinai Library No. 973 and of the 13th century of the Patmos Library No. 105, Vespers begins with the exclamation **"Blessed is the Kingdom..."** (*Evlogēmenē hē basileia...* / *Blagoslovenno Tsarstvo...*). According unto the Euchologion in a 16th-century manuscript of the Tiflis Ecclesiastical-Archaeological Museum No. 450, at the beginning of Vespers the Introductory Psalm is not indicated; it begins with three 'antiphons' with Little Litanies and special prayers at each of them. Following **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Kyrie ekekraxa* / *Gospodi vozzvakh*), prior unto the stichera, there is again a Little Litany, yet of a distinctive type, similar unto the Second Litany of the Faithful at the Divine Liturgy ('In peace let us pray unto the Lord... For the peace from above... For the peace of the whole world... For this holy temple... Help us, save us... And for all who require aid from God. Calling to remembrance our all-holy, immaculate... For unto Thee is due...'). The Augmented Litany **"Let us all say"** (*Rtsem vsi*) is appointed in its present place—following the prokimenon and before **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"**—yet possesses a distinctive composition, identical, except for the first four petitions, with our present Great Litany; consequently it represents the Augmented Litany and the Great Litany still in the period of their union. According unto the aforementioned Patmos Euchologion of the 13th century, which provides fully our present order of Vespers in its festal form, after the exclamations of the Great Litany and the three Little Litanies there is appointed **"Peace be unto all,"** so that in the course of Vespers it is repeated six times (and before the prokimenon it is pronounced by the deacon) [^1689]; compare the Milanese (Ambrosian) and Mozarabic order of Vespers (see above, pp. 353 and 355). According unto the aforementioned Sinai Euchologion of the 12th century, the Litany of Supplication is appointed after **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** (*Nyně otpushchayeshi*) and the troparion; whereas according unto the Euchologion of the Tiflis Ecclesiastical-Archaeological Museum, in this place at festal Vespers there is indicated the litany **"Have mercy on us, O God..."** (*Pomiluy nas, Bozhe...*), and the Litany of Supplication before **"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** (consequently, the conclusion of Vespers is as at present on weekdays). All the indicated Euchologia distribute the evening prayers throughout the entire course of Vespers at all its litanies. — No fewer variations in comparison with our present order are presented by the Euchologia of the same era for Matins. According unto the Sinai Euchologion of the 12th century, it begins with the exclamation **"Blessed is the Kingdom..."** Following the Hexapsalmos and the Great Litany, the Sinai and Patmos Euchologia indicate: the former prior unto the *kathisma* (sessional hymn) and Polyeleos, and the latter prior unto

=== LEAF p358 ===
the Anabathmoi / Antiphons of the Degrees (*stepenny*—the Studite type of festal Matins) and the Gospel, the prayers of the 'three antiphons' with a Little Litany following the 2nd (Sinai) or 3rd (Patmos) among them, beginning **"Again and again"** (*Paki i paki*) or **"Help us, save us"** (*Zastupi, spasi*—Sinai, in accordance with the Sung Office), and with the exclamation **"Peace be unto all"** before it (Patmos) and after it (Patmos; Tiflis No. 450). In place of these 'antiphons,' the Tiflis Euchologion and the Georgian manuscript *(Orig. p. 378)* of the Imperial Public Library No. 208 of the 16th century indicate prayers and Little Litanies following each of the three 'psalms' of the kathisma (in all probability, there is presupposed a single sung-festal three-psalm kathisma, as in the Italo-Sicilian branch of the Studite Typika); after the kathisma there follow: the sessional hymn, reading (both Georgian monuments), Anabathmoi / Degrees (MS No. 208), the Blameless / Amomos (Psalm 118 [119]), the Evlogetaria / **"The Assembly of Angels"** (*Angelskiy Sobor* / *Ton angelōn ho dēmos*—No. 450), the Gospel, and Psalm 50 [51]. The most ancient of the aforementioned Euchologia, the Sinai manuscript, indicates Psalm 50 [51] after the 'kontakion,' that is, after the 6th ode of the canon (or a triodion with the kontakion at the beginning is presupposed—see below), and the Gospel after the Great Doxology (in accordance with the Sung Office). This same Euchologion indicates after the 'kathisma' the 'Prayers of the Sunday Polyeleos in Holy Lent' (a hint at the original use of the Polyeleos exclusively in Lent), wherein it further apparently cites these prayers: the first having no heading whatsoever, and the subsequent ones designated: 'Prayer of the 5th Antiphon' (consequently the preceding was of the 4th), 'of the 6th Antiphon,' '7th Prayer'—four in all; after the last (8th) follows the rubric: 'Then the kontakion, the 50th (Psalm), and immediately the deacon: **"In peace let us pray unto the Lord,"** the 9th prayer..., **"Help us, save us..."** **"The morning and..."** (*tēn orthrinēn kai tēn...*), **"An angel of peace..."** **"Pardon..."** and immediately the odes, and after the 9th ode: **"Again and again..."** with the prayer.' Thus, after the antiphons of the Polyeleos or of some festal kathisma, this Euchologion indicates immediately the kontakion, Psalm 50 [51], the Litany of Supplication with an opening taken from the Litany of Peace and the Little Litany, and only then the canon ('the odes'), yet a canon evidently of exceedingly brief composition (since there is no mention of the customary inter-ode hymnody), perhaps a diodion or triodion; moreover, earlier in this Euchologion, prior unto the discussion of the Polyeleos prayers, occurs the rubric: 'after the 6th ode the kontakion'; perhaps at weekday Matins, in place of the Polyeleos, there was a full canon (precious vestiges of the ancient structure of Matins at the transition from the kontakion-based system unto the canon-based system). All other aforementioned Euchologia, after Psalm 50 [51], presuppose the customary full canon; yet the Georgian manuscripts place before it, after Psalm 50 [51], a Litany of Peace of a distinctive composition differing in both manuscripts (one of 6 petitions, the other of 9), and with our present exclamation (which precedes the canon). The Georgian monuments appoint a litany with prayer also at the 7th ode of the canon, and a prayer at the 8th ode. (It is possible that litanies formerly occurred at every ode, and here we have a survival of this practice, as at present at Paschal Matins and in the Cryptaferrata / Grottaferrata Typikon at Nativity Matins [^1690]). The order of litanies at the conclusion of Matins is in all of them of our present festal type, yet in the Patmos codex it is of our present weekday type (although Matins is presupposed to be

=== LEAF p359 ===
festal). The Georgian codices do not name the Augmented Litany, but indicate only its prayer, the first with a ninefold **"Lord, have mercy"** [^1691].

If the Studite and Jerusalem Typika possessed so much in common in the structure of the most important services of the daily cycle—Vespers and Matins—then in the secondary services, such as the Hours, Compline, Midnight Office, and Typika (*Izobrazitelnye*), *(Orig. p. 379)* they were rendered completely uniform. These services possessed their own distinct history, taking no account of the peculiarities of the one or the other Typikon.

### The Hours

As regards the Hours, we have seen that already in the 9th century their order had developed up unto the Trisagion, where the order of the Hour concludes both according unto the Holy Sepulchre Typikon and according unto the Sinai Horologion of the 8th–9th century according unto the order of the Lavra of St. Sabas (see above, p. 298), which represent in general a complete coincidence in the order of the Hours. The latter Horologion provides likewise all our present psalms of the Hours, and both monuments provide our present troparia of the Hours (their first series following the psalms) and the 'verses' of the Hours (**"Order my steps..."** / *Stopy moya...* and the like). If in this Horologion and certain subsequent ones (for example, Sinai No. 864 of the 9th century), other psalms are joined unto our present three psalms of the Hours, this is because these Horologia, evidently, were intended for cell use rather than for church celebration. For this reason, one must suppose, these Horologia do not contain the priestly element in the service of the Hours. A supplement unto them may be found in the ancient Euchologia, which provide—as, for example, the Greek Sinai MS No. 958 of the 10th century, Paris National Library MS No. 213 of the 11th century (1027), and the Slavonic *Trebnik* of the Moscow Synodal Library MS No. 371/675—the priestly prayers of the Hours. Of such prayers at each Hour there are indicated here six: 'Prayer of the 1st Antiphon,' 'of the 2nd Antiphon,' 'of the 3rd Antiphon,' 'Prayer of the Dismissal,' 'of the Bowing of Heads,' and a special concluding prayer without title; each with an exclamation (it is not everywhere marked: 'exclamation'); before the 5th, **"Peace be unto all,"** **"Bow your heads..."**; before the concluding prayer, the deacon: **"Wisdom."** In their contents the prayers are adapted unto the time of day and the sacred commemorations of the Hour, frequently containing expressions from our present psalms and verses of the Hours; in the prayer of the Third Hour commemoration is made of the Descent of the Holy Spirit; of the Sixth Hour, of the Crucifixion; of the Ninth Hour, of the going up unto prayer at that hour of the Apostles Peter and John [^1692]. Judging by the fact that these prayers are located in Euchologia that presuppose the Sung Office structure of Vespers and Matins, they were intended, in all probability, for Sung Hours. Such Hours are appointed for Lent by the Western ktetorial Typika of the Sicilian recension (see above, p. 402). Their order was as follows: a single psalm of the Hour was chanted, followed by the Great Litany; the priest recited the prayer lying prostrate before the altar; then the 'stichology' (*stichologia* / στιχολογία), that is, a kathisma, at each antiphon of which a Little Litany was said (prayers are not mentioned); then the troparion of the Hour (Lenten) with its Theotokion;

=== LEAF p360 ===
a reading; the Litany of Supplication; the verse of the Hour (**"Order my steps..."** and the like); the Trisagion through the **"Our Father"**; **"Lord, have mercy"** 12 times on each choir; **"Glory to the Father..."**; **"In the Name of the Lord, bless, father"**; the priest: **"O Lord Jesus Christ, our God, have mercy on us"**; three great prostrations; the prayer **"Unto all who lie prostrate"**; and the dismissal [^1693] (in this same manner, as we have seen, the Ninth Hour was chanted according unto the Holy Sepulchre Typikon during Passion Week). *(Orig. p. 380)* It is possible that in churches where the Sung Office was maintained, such Hours—arising, in all probability, under the influence of the *trithekte*—were chanted outside of Great Lent as well, or at least that ordinary Hours there possessed priestly prayers in such or a smaller number.

In addition unto the indicated series of prayers, the Paris Euchologion in separate rubrics provides yet another prayer, rather lengthy, for each Hour—perhaps precisely for the occasion when the Hours were celebrated with less solemnity. This latter practice—of only a single priestly prayer at each Hour—prevailed; yet this priestly prayer of the Hour was gradually abbreviated unto the brief exclamation **"God be bountiful unto us..."**, borrowed from Psalm 66:1 [67:1] and recalling the Old Testament priestly blessing, or unto the exclamation **"O Lord Jesus Christ, our God, have mercy on us"** (see above), later augmented by the addition at the beginning of **"Through the prayers of our holy fathers."** That these exclamations replaced the former (lengthy) prayer of the priest at the Hours is evident from the fact that in ancient Typika they are customarily accompanied by the note: 'and the priest prays' (*euchetai* / εὔχεται), and at times are even directly replaced by this note or designated the 'prayer of the Hours' or 'of Compline' [^1694]. Yet the memory of the former practice prompted the introduction, in place of the priestly prayers that had disappeared from the Hours, of prayers recited by the reader—originally one for each Hour and brief, in the manner of a troparion [^1695], and later several (two or three), among which appears the prayer **"Thou Who at all times"** (*Hō en panti kairō* / *Izhe na vsyakoye vremya*) [^1696]. The latter, on account of its universal contents, was in the course of time appropriated unto each Hour, which received in addition unto it another specialized prayer as well [^1697]. Beside the transformation of the prayer element in the Hours, the present era brought unto the service of the Hour certain additions as well. Such above all was the repeated **"Lord, have mercy,"** which was an echo of ascetic practice (see above, p. 190) [^1698]. *(Orig. p. 381)* Later than other parts, there entered into the composition of the service of the Hours the troparia after the Trisagion, concerning which ancient Horologia and Typika not only exhibit, but also expressly state [^1699], a great vacillation.

### The Inter-Hours (Mesoria)

Besides the Hours, the Jerusalem Typikon from its most ancient manuscripts (at least among those published) possessed also the inter-hours (*mesoria*) or intermediate hours (*pochasiya*). Them
