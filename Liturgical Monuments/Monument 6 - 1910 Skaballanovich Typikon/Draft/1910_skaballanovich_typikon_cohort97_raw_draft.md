1. Plutarch, *De virtute*. Wilhelm Pape, *Griechisch-Deutsches Handwörterbuch*, Braunschweig, 1880, pp. 1162, 262. Later, the word *typos* (*τύπος*) acquired the meaning of an imperial decree or law; the word already possesses this meaning in Philostratus († 240) and the church historian Socrates in the 5th century (E. A. Sophocles, *Greek Lexicon of the Roman and Byzantine Periods*, New York–Leipzig, 1888, p. 1099); but *typos* with this meaning does not form the adjective *typikos* (*τυπικός*) in any writer; among ancient Christian writers (Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Basil the Great), this adjective signifies "symbolic," "figurative," or "typological" (see *ibid.*). The root meaning of the word cannot entirely disappear in all instances of its usage; and if *typos* sometimes signifies a law or rule, it is of a distinctive character. Thus, in Gregory of Nazianzus it is used in relation unto the Symbol of Faith [the Creed]; in Justinian it signifies the Divine Law (for citations see Sophocles); Emperor Constans so entitled his famous doctrinal decree [*Typos of Constans*, 648]. A religious law or rule, however, differs essentially from any other, and its distinctive nature is aptly expressed by the primary meaning of the word *typos*. More precisely than "rule" [*устав*], *typikon* (*τυπικόν*) would be translated as "order of service" [*чиновник*], Latin *ordinarium* (thus Toscani translates it in *Ad typica graecorum*, Rome, 1864, p. 5).

25. By the word *kodon* (*κώδων*), the Septuagint designates the little bells upon the vestment of the High Priest (Exodus 28:34 [LXX 28:30/34]). At present among the Greeks, a small bell of about three pounds' weight is called *kodon* (*κώδων*); a larger one is called *kampanon* (*κάμπανον*). On Mount Athos, *kodon* denotes an altar bell shaped like an inverted cup with a little hammer at the top; this bell is struck at the end of the Proskomedia, and at this signal the brethren, standing each in his place with klobuks removed and heads bowed, commemorate their kinsfolk, both living and departed, while the priest in the altar takes out particles; at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts, at the signal of this bell, all prostrate themselves during the transfer of the Holy Gifts, and then rise at a fresh signal. refrain was prescribed by the Stoglav Council (1551), which labeled the custom of tripling the *Alleluia* a "Latin heresy" (the Latins, according unto the words of the Council, "do not glorify the Trinity, but make a quaternity," which the Council, apparently, deduced in some manner from the *Filioque*). The Council appealed in defense of the twofold *Alleluia* [*sugubaya alleluia*] unto the Life of St. Euphrosynus of Pskov († 1481), unto whom the Most Holy Theotokos Herself "did reveal and forbid the threefold *Alleluia*, commanding Orthodox Christians to say the twofold *Alleluia*" (*Stoglav*, ch. 42; ed. In this Life, composed shortly before the Council, it is related, moreover, that St. Euphrosynus grieved exceedingly over the mystery of the most holy *Alleluia* (that is, whether it ought to be doubled or tripled); and finding no answer unto this in Russia, he undertook a journey unto Greece. There, from conversation with Patriarch Joseph and from the practice of the Cathedral of Hagia Sophia and of all the monasteries, he became convinced of the necessity of doubling the *Alleluia*, and introduced this custom into his own monastery, for which he was rebuked and persecuted by the people of Pskov headed by the priest Job. The question of the *Alleluia* occupied other church figures of that era as well. Even prior unto St. Euphrosynus, St. Photius, Metropolitan of Moscow, answering an inquiry of the Pskovians on this point by an epistle of 1419, pronounced in favor of the triple *Alleluia*; Bishop Gennadius of Novgorod did the same in 1491. Consequently, in the 15th century the question was controversial, and ground for it was provided by disparity not only in practice, but in the liturgical books themselves. While certain Greek and Slavonic manuscripts of the 13th–16th centuries directed to triple the *Alleluia* (for example, the Horologia of the Rumyantsev Museum, Sevastyanov Collection Nos. 496–497 of the 13th–16th centuries; the "Rule on the Chanting of the *Mephemon*" in MS No. 1264 of the Sofia Library of the St. Petersburg Academy); others directed to double it (for example, the Slavonic Typika of the Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 383 and 384 of the 14th–15th centuries; the *Potrebnik* [Euchologion] No. 259 of the 16th century). Originally and for a long period, *Alleluia*, following the example of the Old Testament Church, was chanted, of course, neither two nor three times, but once; such was the case in the 3rd century, according unto the testimony of the *Testamentum Domini* and Tertullian (see Introductory Chapter, p. 108). The dogma of the Trinity, brought to the fore in theology by the heresies of the 3rd century, compelled Christians to make it the primary subject of their confession; hence, presumably, the extensive application of the number three in subsequent liturgical worship (the three-psalm grouping, the three-antiphon structure); from this the tripling of the *Alleluia* could easily have arisen. Yet direct testimonies concerning such a manner of chanting *Alleluia* prior unto the 13th century are lacking; on the contrary, everywhere that the singing of *Alleluia* is mentioned, it appears as though it is pronounced once (Cassian, *De institutis coenobiorum* II, 11; Horologion of the Sinai Library MS No. 863 of the 8th century). Judging from the fact that in the Catholic Church *Alleluia* is chanted once, twice, and three times (*Missale Romanum*, Kempten, 1823, p. 255; *Breviarium*, pp. 22, 29, and elsewhere), one may suppose that this threefold manner of singing it existed. With three antiphons, *Alleluia* might have been sung once at the first antiphon, twice at the second, and thrice at the third; thus it appears in the Order of Monastic Burial according unto MS No. 396 of the Moscow Synodal Library of the 13th–14th centuries (A. Dmitrievsky, *ibid.*). The single *Alleluia* has been preserved among us in Psalm 136 [LXX 136 / MT 137] and at the Liturgy. In the Ethiopic Horologion after the psalms, our present refrain, apparently, preserves its simplest and most ancient form: "Alleluia. Glory to the Lord" (thrice) (B. Turaev, *Ethiopic Horologion*, p. 63).

117. In the Roman Catholic Church, the Hours contain all the constituent elements of our Hours, but in a different order. As with us, the Hours there all possess a single scheme, with the exception of the First Hour (which also among us in antiquity differed in structure from the other Hours, as is evident from the Sinai Horologion of the 8th–9th centuries, of which discussion will be made in the exposition of Chapter 2 of the Typikon). Following the customary beginning, there follows in the Roman Catholic Hours a hymn, proper unto each Hour, corresponding unto our troparion, concluding with *Alleluia* (on Sunday) or another refrain, an "antiphon" (on weekdays). Then follow three sections from Psalm 118 [LXX 118 / MT 119] (which is divided among all the Hours into 11 sections; two of them are read at the First Hour alongside other psalms, and three at each of the remaining Hours without other psalms); these three sections of Psalm 118 at each of the Roman Catholic Hours impart unto it the same three-psalm type possessed by our Hours (Psalm 118 was chosen for the Hours, presumably, because it speaks of unceasing meditation upon the Law of God, of praising God seven times a day, and the like). Following the three sections of Psalm 118, concluding with *Alleluia* or another antiphon, there follow two brief readings (*capitula*) from the New and Old Testaments (from the Epistles and Prophets) with concluding responsories (*responsoria*), sometimes varying according unto feasts (corresponding

Following these *capitula* comes *Kyrie eleison, Christe eleison, Kyrie eleison* (corresponding unto our "Lord, have mercy" 40 times) and the *Our Father*, again with responsories (the first of which is formed by the final words of the Lord's Prayer: "and lead us not into temptation...", corresponding unto our priestly exclamation). Then follows the prayer proper unto the day (in our rite, unto the Hour), which is preceded and concluded by exclamation-responsories: "The Lord be with you" (*Dominus vobiscum*), with the response: "And with thy spirit. Let us pray" (*Oremus*). Following the prayer there is likewise a responsory: "Let us bless the Lord" (*Benedicamus Domino*). The exclamation "The Lord be with you," which is pronounced also by a deacon who conducts the Hours in the absence of a priest, and even in his presence with his permission, is replaced, if the Hours are read by a lector, by another: "O Lord, hear my prayer, and let my cry come unto Thee" (*Breviarium Romanum*, pp. 52, 62, 43 ff., 24 ff.). These responsories surrounding the concluding prayer of the Hour clearly correspond unto our priestly exclamation following "Thou Who at all times." This is the Roman order of the Hours; the Milanese [Ambrosian] and Mozarabic rites are somewhat longer (Priest T. Seredinsky, *On the Divine Worship of the Western Church* [*О богослужении западной церкви*], St. Petersburg, 1849, article 2, p. 6). The affinity of the Roman Catholic Hours unto our order of the Hours bears fine witness, of course, unto the fact that the order of the Hours in its fundamentals was formed both there and here during the period of living fraternal communion between the two Churches. Close unto both orders of the Hours, the Roman Catholic and the Orthodox, are the Hours of the Eastern Non-Chalcedonians, for example, the Copts. According unto the ancient Horologion of the Copts, the order of the Hour consists of an introductory troparion (compare the hymn of the Hour among Roman Catholics), 12 psalms (a trace of the "order of the 12 psalms"), a Gospel reading corresponding unto the commemoration of the Hour (compare *capitula* and paremias), troparia with verses and Theotokia identical with our first troparia, a verse of the Hour such as ours, other troparia largely coinciding with our second troparia (after the Trisagion), the Creed, the Trisagion with the *Our Father*, and a concluding prayer proper unto the Hour (Archimandrite Porphyrius, *Doctrine, Divine Worship... of the Copts*, pp. 57 ff.). Still closer unto our rite are the Hours according unto the Ethiopic Horologion of the Coptic Church (14th century): after the customary beginning, 12 psalms, Gospel, our troparia of the Hour and Theotokia, verse of the Hour, Trisagion, *Alleluia*, troparia largely coinciding with our troparia after the Trisagion, Creed, *Kyrie eleison* 41 times, a brief penitential prayer beginning "Holy, Holy, Holy...", dismissal troparion, the prayer "Thou Who at all times," *Kyrie eleison* 41 times, and a prayer of blessing common unto all the Hours—with the invocation of a series of saints by name, similar unto our litany prayer "Save, O Lord, Thy people" (B.
