**"is the Lord"** or **"Alleluia"**, kathismata and sessional hymns (*sedalny*); on Sunday the *Amomos* (Psalm 118 [119]) with the Resurrection Troparia (*Evlogetaria*), a sessional hymn, Psalm 50 [51] always with troparia, the canon, following the 3rd ode a sessional hymn, hypakoe, or kontakion, following the 6th ode the kontakion and ikos, at times the Gospel preceded by **"Let every breath"** and the prokeimenon, following the 9th ode—the exapostilarion, the Psalms of Praise (*Ainoi* / *Laudes*), and the Great Doxology. Following the Doxology the order is as at present, differing for festal and weekday Matins. The Hours are three-psalm offices of the present composition, yet following the Trisagion the dismissal is immediately indicated (for example, on Great Monday). Consequently, the structure of the daily offices according unto the Holy Sepulchre Typikon is completely unlike the Sung Office. With the latter it shares in common here only the order of the pannychis (on Great Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday), and furthermore, at the Ninth Hour (on those same days) there is appointed in a 'sung' manner only one of its psalms and a litany, with the remaining composition being ordinary.

## Monastic Divine Worship of the 9th and Subsequent Centuries

As regards the Eastern monasteries of the 9th and subsequent centuries, we do not exaggerate if we say that in the majority of them the daily office was of one and the same type, identical with the described worship of the Church of Jerusalem of the 9th–10th centuries and our present usage, and distinct from the Sung Office. This is evident from the preserved records concerning this worship, and chiefly from the ancient liturgical Typika of diverse monasteries that have reached us.

At the head of the monasteries of the 9th and immediately subsequent centuries stood now, at least for the western part of the Byzantine Empire, the Studion Monastery of Constantinople. A series of Typika of the 11th–12th centuries constantly either refer unto the Studite Typikon or even designate themselves as Studite; from the 12th century onward, Typika ever more frequently designate themselves as Jerusalemite and appeal unto the customs of the Lavra of St. Sabbas. Yet the daily worship of both groups of Typika, one may say, is completely identical in structure, with the exception of two or three not particularly significant peculiarities, which furthermore depended not so much upon the character of the Typika as upon their comparative antiquity (manuscripts of Studite Typika are for the most part older than Jerusalemite ones). The difference between the two groups of Typika concerns rather the liturgical year and the Menology. In order to demonstrate the scope of dissemination of the type of daily worship under consideration and to acquaint the reader with those peculiarities which

=== LEAF p342 ===
diverse Typika possessed with respect unto the latter, it is necessary to provide here an historical survey of these Typika.

### The Studite Typikon

The Studion Monastery, founded perhaps as early as the 5th century (see above, pp. 233–234), received in the 9th century in liturgical respects from its hegumen, St. Theodore the Studite († 826) [^1608], so firm and definite an organization *(Orig. p. 357)* that it immediately became the model for monasteries throughout all Byzantium. This circumstance indicates that unto daily worship as well—which constitutes the central nerve of all monastic life—St. Theodore must have imparted, so to speak, a distinct character, differing from the practice of other contemporary monasteries and in some measure surpassing it. In that same practice throughout the Christian East of that time there was perhaps the same diversity that existed in the West, where, as we have seen, a whole succession of abbots of the 6th–8th centuries provided at times very diverse orders for the daily office. In the 9th century, as we have seen, there were two principal orders of daily worship in the East: the first might be termed Jerusalemite, the second Constantinopolitan. It is possible, and very probable, that Constantinopolitan and other monasteries, while following in their cloistered discipline one or another monastic rule (for example, of Pachomius the Great or Basil the Great), possessed a divine worship differing little from cathedral and parish usage. Only upon such a supposition can one explain why the liturgical reform of St. Theodore in the Studion Monastery so immediately brought the latter unto prominence and established it for whole centuries as the legislator of liturgical practice for the entire western half of the Greco-Byzantine Church. If now the liturgical order of St. Theodore presents a literal agreement with the Holy Sepulchre order of the same epoch, then the most natural, if not necessary, supposition is that St. Theodore the Studite borrowed this order thence (he did not invent it, which in general is not done by isolated individuals—and which is especially improbable given such agreement with another order). In favour of this speaks likewise the early adoption into the Studite Typikon—in all likelihood as early as St. Theodore himself—of the works of the Palestinian-Sabbaite hymnographers: Andrew of Crete, Cosmas of Maiuma, and John of Damascus. If the liturgical order of the Studion Monastery had been common in the epoch of St. Theodore unto neighboring monasteries, it would not have received the name of Studite. But in borrowing the order of the daily office from the Church of the Resurrection and from Palestinian monasteries, St. Theodore had to introduce into it certain modifications that entitled it unto a distinct designation. And it is understandable in what direction these modifications would tend. In Constantinople it was natural to adapt the Palestinian order of divine worship unto the Typikon of Hagia Sophia and cathedral churches. Such an adaptation we observe in the Studite Typika. Its traces in the daily

=== LEAF p343 ===
office include the omission of the Hours on many days and of kathismata on certain days; in the church year, for example, the Presanctified Liturgy celebrated daily in Great Lent. These peculiarities, it is true, belong unto monuments of the Studite Typikon that arose later than St. Theodore; yet the significance that they possess therein, as well as their common presence in all these Typika, guarantees that they trace their origin back unto St. Theodore.

### Daily Worship in the Studion Monastery Under St. Theodore

In the absence of a manuscript copy of a church Typikon that might be attributed unto St. Theodore himself, on the question of the composition and form of the daily offices in the Studion Monastery of his time one is obliged to turn exclusively *(Orig. p. 358)* unto the writings of the holy father. The latter allow us to state that divine worship was celebrated in the monastery seven times in twenty-four hours, beginning from midnight [^1609]. The brethren were summoned unto the services by the sound of the semantron (*klepalo* / *bilo*). At the Hours the following psalms are mentioned: at the First Hour—Psalm 5; at the Sixth—Psalm 90 [91]; at the Ninth—Psalm 85 [86]; at Vespers—Psalm 103 [104]. At Matins there is mentioned the reading of the Life of the commemorated saint [^1610]; at Compline—a catechetical instruction (*oglashenie*) of the brethren by the superior or by one of the senior monks, thrice weekly [^1611]. The Liturgy, in all likelihood, was not always celebrated on weekdays; at the Sunday Liturgy, seemingly, not all the brethren received Holy Communion; yet certain ones communicated at every Liturgy [^1612].

### The Hypotyposis and Diatyposis and Daily Worship According unto Them

More information concerning daily worship in the Studion Monastery is provided by the *Hypotyposis* (*Ὑποτύπωσις καταστάσεως τῆς μονῆς τῶν Στουδίου* / "Outline of the Constitution of the Studion Monastery") attributed unto St. Theodore [^1613], and by the liturgically very similar testament of St. Athanasius of Athos († circa 1000), founder of the most ancient lavra on Athos [^1614]: the *Diatyposis* (*Διατύπωσις τοῦ *(Orig. p. 359)* ὁσίου πατρὸς ἡμῶν Ἀθανασίου*), which forms part of his *Typikon* (*Τυπικὸν ἤτοι Κανονικόν*, wherein, alongside the monastic-liturgical rule, there is also an account of the foundation of the Lavra of St. Athanasius) [^1615]. These monuments mention at Vespers the Prooemiac Psalm, **"Blessed is the man"** with three 'beginnings' (antiphons), and **"Lord, I have cried"** with stichera; at Matins: the Six Psalms (Hexapsalmos), **"The Lord is God"**, 'kathismata of the psalm' [psalter kathismata], on Saturday the *Amomos* (Psalm 118 [119]), 'troparia of the kathismata' (sessional hymns / *sedalny*), Psalm 50 [51], the canon, readings, the exapostilarion, and the Praises (*Ainoi* / *Laudes*).

### The Sinai Kanonarion

Approximately such a structure of the services is presupposed by the so-called Sinai Kanonarion (*κανονάριον*), that is, rubrical notes primarily concerning readings from Holy Scripture for the movable and immovable feasts (the Triodion and Menology), appended unto

=== LEAF p344 ===
a liturgical Gospel Book. This Kanonarion has been preserved in two redactions closely related unto each other—one of the 9th–10th century, the other of the 10th–11th century [^1616]. In its Menology *(Orig. p. 360)* the Kanonarion in places coincides with the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople; yet as regards the daily office, although one can judge concerning it here only from the order of Paschal worship, there remains no doubt that in this instance we are dealing with the practice not of Hagia Sophia and the Sung Office, but either with the Holy Sepulchre-Studite usage, or, more accurately, with a combination of both practices with a predominance of the second: thus, at Matins there is a canon, preceded by the Litany of Peace (which is not mentioned at all at Matins in the Typika of the Great Church of the 9th–10th centuries), and the troparion **"Christ is risen"** (unknown unto those Typika) with verses, as in the Studite and Holy Sepulchre Typika.

### The Ktetorial Studite Typika

Finally, the most important daily offices—Vespers and Matins—are provided fully in the present type by a series of so-called complete ktetorial Typika, that is, Typika with a detailed order of service for every day of the ecclesiastical year, given unto diverse monasteries by their founders. The majority of these Typika designate themselves as Studite and are closest unto the customs of the Studion Monastery, in so far as one can judge concerning the latter from the aforementioned monuments; although in places they reflect also the practice of the Great Church, they do not do so with respect unto daily worship. The most ancient of such ktetorial Typika is 1) that given by Patriarch Alexis of Constantinople in 1034 unto the Monastery of the Dormition of the Mother of God founded by him near Constantinople. It has reached us only in Slavonic translations and manuscripts of the 11th–15th centuries (consequently nearly contemporary with its compilation). Since all the most ancient monuments of the ecclesiastical rule in Rus' transmit this Typikon alone, it is believed that it was precisely this rule that was introduced into the practice of the Kyiv Caves Monastery by one of its founders, St. Theodosius of the Caves [^1617]. In its disciplinary section it is entitled *(Orig. p. 361)*: 'The Ustavnik [Rule-Book] examining concerning the food and drink of monks and every other order, and concerning conduct both in church and everywhere, instituted not in writing in the Studite Monastery by our venerable father and confessor Theodore, formerly hegumen therein, but delivered in writing by Alexis, the holy and ecumenical patriarch, in the monastery established by him in the name of the Divine Mother' [^1618]. With this redaction *(Orig. p. 362)* of the Studite Typikon there coincides in the structure of the daily office a series of other more or less pure and unadulterated redactions of it, which in chronological order may be arranged in the following manner. 2) The Typikon of the Monastery of Grottaferrata (Cryptaferrata), 30 versts from Rome, founded by the monk Nilus († 1004), who gave it a rule; unto the latter frequent reference is made

### Matins According unto the Ktetorial Studite Typika

At Matins on weekdays one kathisma was appointed from Antipascha until the Exaltation of the Cross, two from the Exaltation until Great Lent (except Cheesefare Week, when one was read), and three during Great Lent; on Saturday and Sunday according unto some Typika one kathisma was read (San Nicola di Casole), according unto others two (the *Hypotyposis*, Studite-Alexis, Evergetis), wherein on Saturday there was unfailingly read **"Blessed are the undefiled"** (the *Amomos* / Psalm 118 [119]), and on Sunday the 2nd, or the 2nd and 3rd Kathismata [^1634]; thus the Psalter was chanted through at times in less than *(Orig. p. 365)* a week, at times in more than a week (compare the Ambrosian rite, p. 317), for example being chanted through only once during the entire period from Antipascha until Mid-Pentecost [^1635]. The kathismata were always chanted in the appointed tone of the week, furthermore, at least according unto the Studite-Alexis Typikon, with certain 'alleluiaria'—it is unclear whether with special verses resembling current alleluiaria, or with a simple **"Alleluia"** set unto diverse melodies—the latter being more probable; of these alleluiaria St. Theodore the Studite 'instituted' 9 for each tone, 72 in total [^1636]. ### The Hours and Compline in the Studite Typika

Almost the most prominent peculiarity of the Typika under consideration is constituted, following the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* [^1657] and under the influence of the Typikon of Hagia Sophia, by the omission of the Hours on Sundays and feast days, even minor ones. The Studite-Alexis Typikon is interspersed with the remark *porozn' chasom* ('the Hours are omitted' / individual celebration of the Hours), for example, on September 1, 5, 6, 7, etc. [^1658]; the Western Typika and the Evergetis simply maintain silence concerning the Hours, yet the Grottaferrata Typikon takes a step toward Jerusalem practice when, for example, on the Nativity of the Theotokos, and consequently on other feasts likewise, it instructs to chant after Matins the 'dismissory' (troparion) thrice in place of the First Hour, and to celebrate the Hour itself individually (*katamonas*), while the Third and Sixth Hours with the Beatitudes of the feast the presbyters 'chant while vesting' for the procession before the Liturgy [^1659].
