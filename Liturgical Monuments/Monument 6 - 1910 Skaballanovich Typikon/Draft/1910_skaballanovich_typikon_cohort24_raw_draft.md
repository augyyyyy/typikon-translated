wallet; rather, fasting, let us bestow our dinner upon the poor." [^1260] In agreement with this our present *Typikon* likewise accounteth as an indispensable condition of a fast "a single meal" (*odnu trapezu*). [^1261] In monasteries, moreover, where throughout the entire year they were accustomed to take food once a day [^1262] in the evening, [^1263] during the Forty-Day Fast they did not eat for several days together. The pilgrim lady, reputed to be Sylvia, saith concerning the Jerusalem monks (*apotaktites*) that certain of them during the Forty-Day Fast, "having tasted food on Sunday after the Liturgy, that is to say, at the fifth or sixth hour, do not eat until Saturday"; on this day they taste food in the morning after the Liturgy, which on their account is celebrated before daybreak; "those who cannot spend an entire week in fasting eat in the middle thereof on Thursday; those unable to do even this fast for two days together throughout the entire Forty-Day Fast; and whosoever cannot accomplish even this tasteth food from evening unto evening." [^1264]

Originally, perhaps, upon the expiration of the day in the evening they partook during a fast of every kind of food, only, of course, in moderate measure. [^1265] Yet very soon, by the time of Socrates (V century), they arrived at the conception of the necessity of restricting oneself in fasting by the kind of food. Since the kinds and degrees of this restriction strikingly coincide with the anchoritic practice of fasting, [^1266] which antedated *(Orig. p. 245)* the establishment of the Forty-Day Fast, it is indubitable that we have here a borrowing thence. Since, furthermore, the most accepted food among the ascetics was bread and vegetables, such food soon became everywhere customary for the Forty-Day Fast as well. Sylvia saith concerning the Jerusalem monks that in the days of the Forty-Day Fast they "taste neither a crumb of bread, nor oil, nor tree fruit, but only water and a little flour gruel." [^1267] Gradually such food became customary during the fast for the laity as well: the Council of Laodicea requireth "to fast throughout the entire Forty-Day Fast with xerophagy (*νηστεύειν ξηροφαγοῦντας*)." [^1268]

The lengthening of the fast unto forty days posed for ecclesiastical practice a difficult task: how to act regarding the Sundays falling during the fast, upon which fasting would be unfitting, and regarding Saturdays, which in that epoch were honored in certain places almost equally with Sundays. The answer unto this was given already in the III century by St. Hippolytus of Rome. In the inscriptions upon his statue (with the chair) discovered in Rome there is also this rule: "the fast must be interrupted when a Sunday occurreth." [^1269] "During the Forty-Day Fast they fast throughout whole days, except for Saturday and Sunday," saith St. Ambrose. [^1270] St. Basil the Great saith that they fasted only five days in the week. [^1271] St. John Chrysostom calleth Saturday and Sunday days of rest for the strivers of the fast. [^1272] Yet unconditionally this rule was observed so long as fasting was placed in the duration of non-eating and not in the kind of food—that is to say, so long as it was accounted permissible in the evening on a fasting day to eat anything. When, however, the kind of food was restricted for the fast as well, naturally on the days of mitigation of the fast (Saturday and Sunday), as also at the hours of this mitigation (the ninth hour or evening), they came to partake only of certain kinds of food (bread, vegetables, fish, and in certain places perhaps milk). [^1273]

### Divine Worship of the Forty-Day Fast

Besides fasting, the days of the Forty-Day Fast were honored by a distinctive divine worship. Now for the first time, seemingly, it was acknowledged unfitting for fasting days to celebrate the Liturgy, [^1274] as is evident from the explicit canon of the Council of Laodicea concerning this: "that except for Saturday and Sunday, during the Forty-Day Fast it is not permitted to offer bread (*ἄрτον προσφέρειν*)." [^1275] The proximate cause of such a prohibition was that the Liturgy in certain places continued to be united with the agape, and consequently was accompanied by a more *(Orig. p. 246)* or less abundant and sumptuous meal. In Egypt, where the agape was retained especially long, the Liturgy was celebrated on every Wednesday and Friday without Communion, [^1276] which could have served as the germ of the Presanctified Liturgy. Yet the first testimony concerning this latter pertaineth unto Patriarch Sophronius of Jerusalem (VII century). [^1277] The non-celebration of the Liturgy during the Forty-Day Fast had perforce to elicit its compensation by other services. According unto the testimony of the pilgrim lady, reputed to be Sylvia, in the Jerusalem Church, with the exception of Saturday and Sunday, "during the Forty-Day Fast there was no offering (*oblatio*)," and as if in place of the Liturgy, unto the customary services of the day—Matins, the Sixth Hour, Ninth Hour, and Vespers—there was added the Third Hour: "at the third hour they go again (after Matins) into the Anastasis (the church), and everything is performed that throughout the whole year is performed at the sixth hour, since during the days of the Forty-Day Fast there is added also this: that they go into the church at the third hour." [^1278] In that same Jerusalem Church, according unto the witness of the same monument, on all Saturdays of the Forty-Day Fast there was celebrated a solemn vigil, commencing on Friday immediately following the Ninth Hour and Vespers and continuing until Saturday morning, when the Liturgy was joined directly unto it: "throughout the whole night there are sung alternately now psalms, now antiphons, and there are alternately diverse readings, and all this continueth until morning." [^1279] The other services of the Forty-Day Fast in the Jerusalem Church did not differ from the customary ones. The pilgrim lady noteth only a certain peculiarity in the divine worship of Wednesday and Friday of the Forty-Day Fast: the Ninth Hour was celebrated in the Church of Sion (and not in the Church of the Anastasis). [^1280] In general the Forty-Day Fast in the Jerusalem Church was distinguished by divine services so solemn that its days were as if a continuous festival: they were called *eortae*, "paschal"; concerning them the pilgrim lady expresseth herself that they "are celebrated" (*celebrantur*). [^1281] Whereas the Council of Laodicea enacteth "that during the Forty-Day Fast one ought not to celebrate the birthdays of the martyrs, but to perform their commemorations on Saturdays and Sundays," [^1282] in the Jerusalem Church the commemorations of the martyrs falling during the Forty-Day Fast were celebrated at their proper time, and for them certain peculiarities of the fasting service were abrogated (for example, the aforesaid celebration of the Ninth Hour on Sion). [^1283] During the Forty-Day Fast

=== LEAF p233 ===
ecclesiastical preaching was intensified: according unto the witness of the Jerusalem pilgrim lady, there during the Forty-Day Fast, "in order that the people might ever be instructed in the Law, the bishop and the presbyters preach unceasingly (*assidue*)." [^1284] By the luminaries of ecclesiastical *(Orig. p. 247)* preaching in the IV–V centuries the greatest number of their finest homilies was delivered during the days of the Forty-Day Fast.

### The Catechumenate during the Forty-Day Fast

Considerable peculiarities were imparted unto the divine worship of the Forty-Day Fast by assigning the catechumenate unto its days. The catechumenate, which in that epoch lasted about three years, [^1285] was solemnly brought to conclusion during the Forty-Day Fast. The principal acts of this conclusion were the naming of the candidate preparing for baptism (*competens*) at the beginning of the Forty-Day Fast, his daily exorcism (from evil spirits) throughout the entire fast, [^1286] final instruction in the truths of the faith, the learning and exposition of the Creed (*traditio symboli*), and lastly, baptism itself on Great and Holy Saturday; certain of these acts formed part of the daily round of divine services, while others were appended thereto, by virtue whereof the entire divine worship of the Forty-Day Fast was as it were concentrated around the catechumens and might be called the liturgy of the catechumens. Nor is this surprising, in view of the fact that the Forty-Day Fast itself arose out of the fast of the catechumens and of the faithful with them and on their behalf prior unto baptism. [^1287] Sylvia thus describeth the catechumenate during the Forty-Day Fast:

> "He that declareth his name doth this before the day of the Forty-Day Fast, and the presbyter recordeth the names of all; this takes place eight weeks beforehand, during which, as I have said, the Forty-Day Fast continueth. [^1288] When the presbyter hath recorded the names of all, on the following day—the day of the Forty-Day Fast itself, that is to say, from which the eight weeks of the fast commence—a cathedra is set for the bishop in the midst of the great church, that is to say, in the Martyrium; on either side the presbyters sit upon chairs, and all the clerics stand; then those seeking baptism (*competentes*) are brought forward one by one: if they be men, they come with their fathers (sponsors); if women, with their mothers (sponsors). And then the bishop questions one by one the neighbors of him that hath entered, saying: whether he leadeth a good life, whether he honoreth his parents, whether he be not a drunkard or a boaster; and he questioneth concerning all the vices that are more grievous in man. And when he is convinced that he is blameless in all things, he inscribeth his name with his own hand. Those who approach unto baptism, during the forty days of the fast, early in the morning

=== LEAF p234 ===
> the clerics exorcise, as soon as the morning dismissal takes place in the Anastasis. Immediately following this a cathedra is set for the bishop in the great church, in the Martyrium, and all who approach unto baptism, both men and women, sit around the bishop, while the fathers and mothers stand; in like manner, of the people, all who wish to listen enter and sit down, but only the faithful. A catechumen doth not enter thither at the time when the bishop instructs them (who are prepared for baptism) in the Law. Thus, beginning from Genesis, throughout these forty days he traverseth all Scripture, first expounding it carnally (literally), and *(Orig. p. 248)* then explaining it spiritually. In like manner also during these days they are instructed concerning the resurrection and concerning everything pertaining unto the faith, and this is called catechesis (*cathecisis*). And when five weeks have elapsed from the beginning of instruction, then they receive the Creed (*traditio symboli*); the meaning of this Creed in its individual words the bishop explains unto them in like manner as the meaning of the Scriptures, first carnally, then spiritually; so also he explains the Creed. And it cometh to pass that in these places all the faithful follow the Scripture when it is read in church, since all are instructed during these forty days, beginning from the first until the third hour, for the catechesis continueth three hours. And the Lord knoweth, reverend sisters, that the faithful who enter to hear the catechesis attend with greater attention unto that which is spoken and explained by the bishop than when he sitteth and preacheth in church concerning individual passages interpreted in such manner. Upon the conclusion of the catechesis, at the third hour, immediately they conduct the bishop with hymnody into the Anastasis, and the dismissal of the third hour takes place: and thus, for the space of three hours they are instructed daily throughout seven weeks. But in the eighth week of the Forty-Day Fast, that is to say, which is called the Great Week, their instruction ceaseth, in order that that which is more momentous may be accomplished. In the Great Week (doubtless on Monday) the bishop comes in the morning into the great church, into the Martyrium; behind the altar in the apse a cathedra is set for the bishop, and thither they approach one by one—a man with his father, a woman with her mother—and recite (*reddet*, return) the Creed unto the bishop. Following the reading of the Creed unto the bishop, he addresseth all" [^1289] with a brief exhortation.

Thus the catechumenate was conducted in other churches also (for example, in the Roman

and African churches). At the recitation (*redditio*) of the Creed in certain churches a promise was exacted to recite it daily in the morning, at midday, and in the evening. [^1290] In the history of divine worship this rite of the catechumenate is significant in that it fixed permanently in the Liturgy the litany for the catechumens, and in Great Lent also that for those preparing for illumination (baptism). In addition, it gave rise unto a special ancient service during the Forty-Day Fast, specifically for the catechumens: the *tritekte*, so named from the time of its celebration (*τριτέκτη*, "third-sixth," between the 3rd and 6th hours) and related on the one hand unto the service of the Hours, especially the Third, and on the other unto the Liturgy of the Catechumens; this service achieved full development by the IX century, yet its origin may be placed in this epoch, and its germ accounted to be the Third Hour celebrated in Jerusalem only during the Forty-Day Fast and probably adapted unto the catechesis taking place during this service, albeit in another church (in the Martyrium); at all events Psalm 24 [MT 25], which formeth part of the Third Hour, is entirely suited unto the rite of the catechumenate. [^1291] *(Orig. p. 249)*

### Penance and Communion during the Forty-Day Fast

Having assigned unto the Forty-Day Fast, as unto the most sacred season of the year, the catechumenate and baptism, the Church relegated unto this season also the administration of two other mysteries which demanded no less purifying preparation. So it was first of all with penance, this second baptism. The discipline of penance, so complex and rigorous in the first centuries, was most conveniently applied in the pre-Paschal fast. Already the Council of Ancyra (314–315 AD) appointeth Pascha as the time both for receiving the penitent into the Church and for transferring him from a lower order of penance unto a higher: "let them be received into the rank of those that hear the Scriptures until the Great Day of Pascha," it enacted regarding those who had partaken of idol-sacrifices during the persecutions. [^1292] According unto St. Gregory of Nyssa († after 394 AD), "on the day of Pascha we not only lead unto God those who are transformed by regeneration in the font of grace, but also those who through repentance and conversion from dead works approach unto eternal life." [^1293] Thus the present custom of preparing for Communion (*govet'*) in Great Lent arose already in this epoch. Thence deriveth also the predominantly penitential content of divine worship in Great Lent. (As a memorial of such an appointment of the Forty-Day Fast there serveth in the Roman Church the rite of the so-called "blessing of ashes" on the first day of the Roman Catholic Lent, on the Wednesday of the first week of our fast). Under the influence of this ancient assignment of penitential discipline unto the Forty-Day Fast, subsequently all the faithful came to be regarded during this fast as being in the status of penitents (and in part of catechumens), and upon this ground were deprived of the full Liturgy. [^1294] When the custom of weekly Communion of all the faithful was abandoned, the Forty-Day Fast became a preparation also for the worthy

=== LEAF p236 ===
reception of this mystery, which was assigned now unto the day of its institution by the Saviour—Great and Holy Thursday—now unto Pascha. "Formerly many," saith St. John Chrysostom, "approached the Holy Mysteries thoughtlessly and without discernment, even on that day whereon Christ instituted Them. Therefore, seeing how much harm ariseth from indiscreet approach unto Them, the fathers came together and appointed forty days of fasting, prayers, hearing of the word of God, and assemblies, in order that throughout all these days, being thoroughly cleansed by prayers, almsgiving, fasting, vigils, tears, confession, and all else, we might approach (unto the Mysteries) with a pure conscience according unto our ability," [^1295] "so that, *(Orig. p. 250)* being purified from the sins cleaving unto us in every manner throughout the year, we might with spiritual vigilance become partakers of this unbloody sacrifice." [^1296] Blessed Jerome saith: "The Lord Himself, the true Jonah sent to preach unto the world, fasteth forty days, and leaving us the heritage of fasting, prepareth by this ascetic struggle our souls for the eating of His Body." [^1297]

### Civil Legislation concerning the Forty-Day Fast

Secular legislation for its part met the Church halfway in the sanctification of the Forty-Day Fast. Emperor Theodosius the Great issued two edicts concerning the Forty-Day Fast. According unto one of them, "during the forty days which through the performance of diverse rites prepare for the season of Pascha, all examination of criminal cases is suspended" (doubtless because it was accompanied by judicial torture), and according unto the other, "in the consecrated days of the Forty-Day Fast there shall be no corporal punishments that might end in death." [^1298] A similar ordinance on a particular occasion was made by Emperor Valentinian, according unto the words of St. Ambrose. [^1299] An exception unto the aforementioned laws of Theodosius the Great was made by Theodosius the Younger for cases involving the Isaurian brigands. [^1300]

### Holy Week

The final, pre-Paschal week of the fast was everywhere named the "Great Week" (*Hebdomas Magna*). [^1302] The fast during this week was intensified. The *Apostolic Constitutions* prescribe for it the strictest fast upon bread, salt, and vegetables, advising total abstinence from food on Friday and Saturday. [^1303] According unto the testimony of St. Epiphanius of Cyprus, "these six days of Pascha all the people (*πάντες οἱ λαοί*) customarily spend in xerophagy (*ξηροφαγία*), that is to say, they partake towards evening only of bread with salt and water; while certain ones zealously extend their abstinence (*ὑπεрτίθενται*, literally postpone, that is to say, the partaking of food) unto two, three, and four days; and others even spend the entire week without food until cockcrow on Sunday." [^1304] *(Orig. p. 251)*

### Lazarus Saturday

Unto the Great Week there were reckoned in the Jerusalem Church the penultimate Saturday, whereon was commemorated the resurrection of Lazarus, and the final Sunday, whereon was commemorated the Entry of the Lord into Jerusalem. The divine worship of these days is described in detail by the Aquitanian pilgrim lady:

> "In the morning, at the daybreak of Saturday, the bishop officiates (*offert*) and celebrateth the Liturgy (*oblationem*) so that the dismissal may take place earlier. The archdeacon proclaims, saying: 'Let us all be ready today at the seventh hour (1:00 PM) at the tomb of Lazarus.' And then, upon the arrival of the seventh hour, all assemble at the tomb of Lazarus (in Bethany). On the way from Jerusalem unto the tomb of Lazarus, approximately five hundred paces from that place, there is a church upon the road at the place where Mary, the sister of Lazarus, met the Lord. And when the bishop arrives thither, all the monks go forth to meet him there, and the people enter thither: a hymn is sung there and one antiphon, and there is read the corresponding passage from the Gospel where the sister of Lazarus met the Lord (John 11). Then, after the saying of the prayer and the blessing of all, they proceed thence unto the tomb of Lazarus with hymns. Hymns are sung, as also antiphons adapted unto the day and place; readings likewise are read suitable unto the day. And then, when the dismissal takes place, Pascha is proclaimed: that is to say, a presbyter ascendeth unto a high place and reads that passage where

=== LEAF p238 ===
> it is written in the Gospel: **'Jesus therefore six days before the passover came to Bethany'** (*Iisus zhe prezhde shesti dney Paskhi priide v Vifaniyu*) [^1305] and the rest (John 12). Thus, upon the reading of this passage and the proclamation of Pascha, the dismissal takes place. And this is performed on this day because, as it is written in the Gospel, this occurred in Bethany six days before the passover: and from Saturday until Thursday, when after the Supper the Lord was taken, six days are reckoned. Thus all return into the city, directly into the Anastasis, and there Vespers takes place according unto custom." [^1306]

### Palm Sunday

> "On the following day, that is to say, on Sunday, which formeth the entrance into the Paschal Week, called here the Great Week (*septimana major*), at cockcrow the customary service is celebrated (Matins until morning in the Anastasis or at the Cross). In the morning on the Sunday day there takes place the customary procession (*proceditur*—litany) in the great church, which is called the Martyrium. Upon the conclusion of the service in the great church, before the dismissal, the archdeacon proclaims, saying first: 'Throughout the entire week, that is to say, beginning from tomorrow, at the ninth hour let us all gather in the Martyrium, that is to say, in the great church.' Then he proclaims a second time, saying: 'Today at the seventh hour let us all be ready on the Mount of Olives.' Thus, upon the dismissal of the Liturgy in the great church, that is to say, in the Martyrium, the bishop is escorted with hymns into the Anastasis. Upon the completion of all that is customarily performed on Sundays in the Anastasis after the dismissal in the Martyrium (the lity), [^1307] each person, returning unto his own home, *(Orig. p. 252)* hasteneth to eat, so that at the beginning of the seventh hour (1:00 PM) all may be ready in the church situated upon the Mount of Olives, that is to say, on the Mount of Olives, where is that cave wherein the Lord taught." [^1308]

> "At the seventh hour all the people ascend the Mount of Olives, that is to say, the Eleon, unto the church, together with the bishop; hymns and antiphons suitable unto this day and place are sung, and readings likewise are read (the Ninth Hour). And when the ninth hour arriveth, they proceed with hymns unto the Imbomon, that is to say, unto that place (of the Mount of Olives) from which the Lord ascended into heaven, and where all sit down (for the readings), for all the people, in the presence of the bishop, receive an invitation to sit, and only the deacons ever stand. There also hymns and antiphons suitable unto the

=== LEAF p239 ===
> place and day are sung, and readings and prayers are likewise inserted. And when the eleventh hour arriveth, there is read that passage from the Gospel where the children with branches and palms met the Lord, saying: **'Blessed is He that cometh in the name of the Lord'** (*Blagosloven gryadyy vo imya Gospodne*) (Vespers). And immediately the bishop and all the people arise, and then from the summit of the Mount of Olives all proceed (a procession / lity) on foot. And all the people go before him with hymnody and antiphons, continually responding: **'Blessed is He that cometh in the name of the Lord.'** And all the children, as many as there be in these places, even those that cannot walk because they are very tender, and whom their parents carry in their arms, all hold branches—some of palms, others of olives; and thus they escort the bishop, singing refrains, and they go slowly that the people may not grow weary; therefore already in the evening they arrive at the Anastasis. And when they arrive thither, even though it be quite late, Vespers is celebrated; then a prayer (litany) is said at the Cross, and the people are dismissed." [^1309]

Thus the ancient custom of the Procession on the Foal of an Ass on Palm Sunday and our present universal custom of using (and blessing) palms and willow branches on this Sunday received their beginning in the Jerusalem Church. [^1310]

### The First Days of Holy Week

The first three days of Holy Week in the Jerusalem Church were distinguished by an exceptionally solemn service of the Ninth Hour. It was celebrated in the Martyrium (and not in the Church of the Anastasis), where in general more solemn services were celebrated (for example, the Liturgy on Sundays), and continued from the 9th hour until the 1st hour of the night (= from 3:00 PM unto 8:00 PM). Besides hymns and antiphons there were readings at it suitable unto the day and place, alternating continually with prayers. After the Ninth Hour, Vespers was celebrated, which concluded already at night and ended with a litany (consisting, as customarily, of hymnody, prayer, and the blessing of the catechumens and faithful) of the bishop into *(Orig. p. 253)* the Church of the Anastasis. [^1311] On Great Tuesday and Wednesday this solemn evening service received yet a new addition: another litany (of the same structure)—on Tuesday unto the Mount of Olives, unto the aforementioned cave (where the Lord taught the disciples), and on Wednesday unto the cave of the Church of the Anastasis; at this litany the Gospel was read: on Tuesday from St. Matthew, the discourse of the Saviour concerning the signs of the Second Coming (**"Take heed that no man deceive you"** [*Blyudite, da nikto zhe vas prel'stit*]), doubtless delivered on Tuesday from the Mount of Olives; and on Wednesday the Gospel concerning the betrayal of Judas, which, the pilgrim lady remarketh, was listened to with

=== LEAF p240 ===
affecting cries and groans. [^1312] These Gospel readings are assigned unto those same days of Holy Week by our *Typikon* as well.

### Great and Holy Thursday

Great and Holy Thursday was regarded as a festal day of Holy Week. The canon of the Council of Laodicea: "during the Forty-Day Fast, in the final week, one ought not to dissolve (*λύειν*, that is to say, from the fast) Thursday (*τὴν πέμπτην*), and thereby dishonor the entire Forty-Day Fast, but one ought to fast throughout the entire Forty-Day Fast and be content with xerophagy," [^1313] demonstrateth that there was an opposite custom, and at least from xerophagy they relaxed on Great Thursday. In the Calendar of Polemius Silvius, Bishop of Sion in northern Italy (435–455 AD), [^1314] on March 24 there is indicated *Natalis calicis*—the commemoration of the Chalice (the day of Christ's death was assigned unto March 25, and of the Resurrection unto March 27). In monuments of the VI and VII centuries this day is directly styled a feast (*solemnitas*, *festum*). [^1315] On this day the Liturgy was to bear an especially solemn character; it was celebrated late in the evening (in imitation of the Saviour), and in certain places in addition also in the morning. Blessed Augustine, in reply unto the question of Januarius: "whether to celebrate the offering (the Eucharist) on Great Thursday in the morning, and then again after dinner? To fast and after the Eucharist to dine, as we customarily do?" writeth:

> "Unto certain ones there is exceedingly pleasing a certain plausible reason for this: that on one determinate day of the year, whereon the Lord delivered the Supper, it is permissible as if by a special commemoration to celebrate the Eucharist and receive the Body and Blood of the Lord after the taking of food. Yet I account it more fitting to celebrate it at such an hour that those who have fasted may approach unto the offering, but after the refection which takes place at the ninth hour. By this we compel no one on the day of the Lord's Supper to breakfast, yet neither do we dispute with any; in general I do not criticize this practice, declaring only that the majority or almost all in most places are accustomed on this *(Orig. p. 254)* day (only) to dine (*coenare*); and since certain ones observe also the fast, in the morning the offering of Christ is celebrated on account of those who breakfast, since they cannot endure at once both fasting and the bath, and in the evening on account of those who fast." [^1316]

Blessed Augustine, thus, noteth a twofold practice: on Great Thursday some celebrated the Liturgy in the morning as well, so that there might be possible
