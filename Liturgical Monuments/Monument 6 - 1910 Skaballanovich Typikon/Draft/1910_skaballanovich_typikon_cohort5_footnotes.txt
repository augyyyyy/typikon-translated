[^231]: 1 Corinthians 14:16. "He that occupieth the room of the unlearned" [literally "simple man / commoner"], Slavonic *nevezhdy*, Greek *idiōtou*. *Idiōtēs*—a person who does not hold an official or leading position, or a non-specialist, for example ordinary citizens as distinguished from the king (Herodotus II, 81, et al.), the general public as distinguished from the scholars of a school (Plutarch, *Quaestiones Convivales* I, 1); non-ecstatics and non-mantics as distinguished from the visionary (Pausanias, *Corinthiaca* 13). In the LXX in Proverbs 6:8, as in Herodotus. In the Talmud—an ordinary man as distinguished from a rabbi, craftsman, magistrate, and the like. In agreement with all these meanings, the word here clearly signifies each member of the liturgical assembly as distinguished from him who cometh forward with some revelation, in this particular case with speaking in tongues. Consequently, the word stands not in the sense of a casual visitor to the assembly, for example an unbeliever, as in verse 24, where the very same word is used, nor in the general sense of an ignorant or uneducated person, still less in the modern sense of this word, which some also admitted, rendering it, for example, in German as *Idiot* (A. J. Binterim, *Die vorzüglichsten Denkwürdigkeiten der Christ-Katholischen Kirche*, IV, 1, Mainz, 1827, p. 319). "He that occupieth the room" indicates that the status of the *idiōtēs* is not permanent, but occasional: all, except him who actively ministereth during divine worship, are *idiōtai*, and each becometh such at a given moment when he hath no revelation (Th. Zahn & Ph. Bachmann, *Der erste Brief des Paulus an die Korinther*, Leipzig, 1904, pp. 420–421). Here, consequently, is already given a rather sharp (more so than in the synagogue) division of worshippers into clergy [those who minister sacred rites] and laity, although this demarcation is not yet rigid, but fluid.
