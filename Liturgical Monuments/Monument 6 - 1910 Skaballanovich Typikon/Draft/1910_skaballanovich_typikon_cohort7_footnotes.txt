[^339]: Recently in Russian scholarship the opinion was advanced that the ancient veneration of martyrs in the 2nd–4th centuries also included religious dancing (D. Konovalov, "Religiozny ekstaz v russkom misticheskom sektantstve" [*Religious Ecstasy in Russian Mystical Sectarianism*], in *Bogoslovskiy Vestnik*, 1907, no. 10, p. 351; 1908, no. 4, pp. 707–728). In the acts of the martyrs, which frequently conclude with descriptions of liturgical assemblies in honor of the martyrs, there is no mention of dancing in their honor: to interpret the phrase in the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* concerning celebrating his commemoration "with jubilation and joy" (*agalliasei kai charai* [ἀγαλλιάσει καὶ χαρᾷ]) as referring unto dancing (Konovalov, p. 707) is arbitrary. Only a few monuments of the 3rd and 4th centuries speak of such dances as a local and tolerated phenomenon. Thus, according to the testimony of St. Gregory of Nyssa, St. Gregory the Wonderworker (3rd cent.), yielding unto the inclinations of his flock in order to divert them from pagan festivals, permitted a little leaping/dancing (*ti mikron hypokirtan* [τὶ μικρὸν ὑποκιρτᾷν]) in honor of the martyrs (*Hom. in Eccl.* 6). A 4th-century homily on the commemoration of the martyr Polyeuktos († 259 in Armenia) invites: "according to the established custom let us exult/dance (*choreusōmen* [χορεύσωμεν]) in honor of the martyr" (B. Aubé, *Polyeucte dans l'histoire*, Paris, 1882, p. 79). There is also cited an expression of St. Basil the Great: "we dance with sacred dances (*entheois choreiais* [ἐνθέοις χορείαις]) at the tombs of the saints" (cited in F. C. Conybeare, *Philo: About the Contemplative Life*, Oxford, 1895, p. 154: *Bas.*, t. 1, p. 512 B). In Abyssinian worship to this day dancing has been preserved at molebens and processions of the cross, in which the priests also take some part (B. Turayev, "Abissiniya," in *Pravoslavnaya bogoslovskaya entsiklopediya*, vol. I, St. Petersburg, 1899, col. 71). Orthodox Arabs in Jerusalem also permit it around holy shrines (A. N. Muravyov, *Puteshestviye ko svyatym mestam* [*Journey to the Holy Places*], part 2, St. Petersburg, 1849, p. 124). Thus all these were religious excesses, occurring only in such enthusiastic localities as Asia Minor and Armenia, and never approved by the Church. St. John Chrysostom regards dancing as a demonic enterprise (*Hom. in Matt.* 48). St. Gregory the Theologian advises dancing as David did, understanding David's dance in an allegorical sense (*Oration* 5). Bl. Augustine expresses indignation at the former dances in the martyrium of St. Cyprian and directly before the basilicas of the saints (*Sermon on the Feast of St. Cyprian*; *Sermon* 215 [265]). The Council of Carthage (397), Canon 71, prohibited "abominable dances (*orcheseis mysaras* [ὀρχήσεις μυσαράς]) performed in sacred places, even on the days of commemoration of the blessed martyrs." The Council of Laodicea (late 4th cent.), Canon 53, forbids even at weddings to leap or dance (*ballizein ē orchesthai* [βαλλίζειν ἢ ὀρχεῖσθαι]). In view of such an attitude toward religious dancing in the Church and such rarity of it among ancient Christians, one can hardly see a ritual survival of it in the circular procession during Baptism, Marriage, and Chirotony (Konovalov, p. 723).
