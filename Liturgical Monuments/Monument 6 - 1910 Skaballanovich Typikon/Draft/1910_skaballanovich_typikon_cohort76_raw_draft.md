### Their History (*Istoriya ikh*)

The morning prayers, like the evening prayers, were once distributed throughout the entire service. Yet the most ancient among the surviving Euchologia—such as, for example, the Barberini MS of the 8th–9th century, MS No. 226 of the Imperial Public Library (from the collection of Bishop Porphyrius) of the 8th–10th century, and the Cryptensis MS of Bessarion (*Bessarionis*) of ca. the 9th century, with which the Euchologion of the Paris National Library Coislin MS No. 213 of 1027 coincides regarding the prayers—simply number the morning prayers, indicating a place in the order of Matins only for the last three or four. What is important is that these Euchologia provide all our present-day prayers, excluding, however, the 9th, and in their present order, thereby allowing us to judge concerning the degree of their antiquity. The Cryptensis MS titles the 7th prayer "prayer of the Gospel," the 10th (there the 9th) "prayer of the 50th" (that is, of the psalm), the 11th (there the 10th) "prayer at the Praises" (*Ainoi*), the 12th (there unnumbered) "prayer or dismissal," and then the prayer of the bowing of heads; but before the last two, the Cryptensis and Coislin MSS have a prayer of the catechumens and two prayers of the faithful, with a note that they are read only on occasion (concerning these prayers and this rubric, see Introductory Chapter, p. 348). This latter circumstance, as well as the fact that in certain Euchologia—for example, MS No. 962 of the Sinai Library of the 11th–12th century—the first five prayers are titled "prayer of the first antiphon," "prayer of the 2nd antiphon," and so forth (the Euchologion of the same library No. 973 of 1153 and Patmos Library MS No. 105 of the 13th century title only the first three prayers thus; the Sinai Euchologion No. 958 of the 10th century titles only the 4th prayer thus; and Sinai MS No. 982 of the 13th century notes only concerning our present *(Orig. p. 623)* 10th prayer, there the 9th, "prayer of the antiphon, that is, of Ps. 50"),—these circumstances demonstrate that the morning prayers were originally adapted unto the Chanted Office (*asmatikos orthros*). When they were transferred unto Matins of the Studite-Jerusalem type, which had no antiphons at the beginning, it became necessary to distribute them among the constituent parts of this Matins; and the various Euchologia accomplish this each in its own way, such that one may say there are not even two that coincide in all respects.

Thus, the Georgian Euchologia—No. 208 of the Imperial Public Library and No. 450 of the Tiflis Church Museum, both of the 16th century, but bearing traces of very ancient practice—indicate after the Six Psalms, or at its last psalm (in the second Euchologion), our present 1st prayer (the prayers are not numbered): at the "kathisma" after the first "psalm" (perhaps an entire kathisma is called a "psalm") the 2nd prayer; after the second psalm the 3rd prayer; after the third psalm the 4th prayer, with the conclusions of the prayers being proclaimed as...

=== LEAF p752 ===
...exclamations after the litanies immediately following them (at the kathisma—small litanies); before the Gospel the 9th prayer; at Psalm 50—the 10th; after Ode 3 of the Canon—the 5th prayer; after Ode 6—the 6th; after Ode 7—the 7th; after Ode 8—the 8th, the prayer of fervent supplication; and after the Supplicatory Ektenia the "prayer of egress," that is, our present 12th prayer.

In Sinai Euchologion No. 973 of 1153: at the Great Ektenia the 1st prayer; after it the 2nd and 3rd; prayers 4–7 are cited with the rubric: "other prayers for (Gr. *διά*) the Polyeleos of Sunday in Holy Lent, when there is stichology"; after Psalm 50—the 10th prayer (there the 9th); after Ode 9—the 11th prayer; the "prayer of fervent supplication" at the Augmented Ektenia; after it the 12th prayer; and in its present place the prayer of the bowing of heads.

In the Euchologion of Patmos Library MS No. 105 of the 13th century: the 1st prayer at **"God is the Lord"**; after it the 2nd and 3rd; before the Gospel the 9th; at Psalm 50 the 10th; after (Gr. *ἀπό*) Ode 3 of the Canon the 4th; after Ode 6—the 5th; after Ode 9—the 11th (the conclusions of the prayers serve as the exclamations of the litanies); at **"Glory to God in the highest"**—the 12th; after the Supplicatory Ektenia the prayer of the bowing of heads; after the aposticha the "prayer of dismissal": **"O Lord, Creator** (Gr. *Δημιουργέ*) **of all..."** (with supplication for hearing, non-imputation of sins, pacification of life, and being counted worthy of the Kingdom of Heaven).

In the Sluzhebnik of the Sophia Library (at the St. Petersburg Theological Academy) MS No. 523 of the 14th century: at the Six Psalms the 1st prayer; at the kathismata the 2nd and 3rd; after Ode 3 of the Canon the 4th, 5th, and 6th; after Ode 6 the 7th and 8th; "when they chant *Have mercy on me, O God*"—the 10th; at the Praises (*Ainoi*) the 11th; after them and the Small Ektenia—the 12th (before the Gospel and the Augmented Ektenia).

Other monuments, although they do not provide a distribution of the prayers throughout the service, are nonetheless characterized by departures from the present and simultaneously most ancient order of the prayers, witnessing unto similar attempts to adapt the prayers of the Chanted Matins unto ordinary Matins: thus, MS No. 310 of the Moscow Synodal Library of the 16th century gives the following order: 1–5, 8, 10, 6, 7, 11; the Sluzhebnik of the same library No. 952/347 of the 14th century: 1–4, 10, 5, 6, 11; the *Taktikon* of Emperor John Cantacuzene of the 14th century (Greek MS of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 279), the Euchologia of the same library Nos. 280 and 281 of the 15th century, and Rumiantsev Museum MS No. 486 of the 16th century: 1–6, 10, 7, 8, 11, 12; Sinai MS No. 958 of the 10th century places 10 before 8. Other Euchologia achieve such an adaptation by a simpler route—by omitting certain prayers, predominantly those whose content and position made their assignment unto a specific moment of Matins difficult (as the above-named Euchologia likewise do); most often *(Orig. p. 624)* of all, in the vast majority of manuscripts, the 9th prayer is omitted, or placed outside the reckoning, or put last (an echo of the practice wherein the Gospel was read after the Great Doxology); Paris Euchologion No. 213 remarks concerning it that it "is not said in the Great Church, but in...

=== LEAF p753 ===
...the others, in which the Gospel is proclaimed after Ode 6 on great feasts"; at times prayers 7 are also omitted (see above and Sinai Euchologia Nos. 956 and 958 of the 10th century, No. 962 of the 11th–12th century, and the Eiliton-roll of the Athonite Xenophontos Library No. 161 of the 14th century), 8 (the same, excluding No. 958), 12 (see above and a series of Sophia Sluzhebniki of the 15th century, Nos. 530, 533, et al.), 11 (see above, an Eiliton-roll of the 9th century described by Bishop Porphyrius [^2398], and Athos Esphigmenou MS No. 208 of the 16th century), and 6 (Sinai Euchologion No. 962 of the 11th–12th century). In some manuscripts these omissions reach, therefore, up to four (in the last-mentioned Euchologion) and five prayers: thus, in Sophia Sluzhebniki No. 518 of the 13th century and No. 522 of the 14th century, prayers 4, 6, 7, 9, and 10 are lacking. And the Euchologia of Sinai No. 984 of the 15th century and Athens University No. 94 of 1542 contain only the first three prayers, prescribing that they be read at the Six Psalms.

Gradually and in various locales the recitation of the remaining prayers was also assigned unto the Six Psalms, and the number of such simultaneously read prayers increased from eight—since from the 9th onward the prayers bear the aforementioned titles assigning them unto other parts of the service ("at the Gospel," "at the 50th," "at the Praises," "at Glory to God in the highest"—thus in Rumiantsev Museum Euchologion No. 474 of the 16th century; in Sinai MS No. 961 of the 11th–12th century the 8th is also marked "before Ps. 50")—up to ten (thus in a series of Sophia Sluzhebniki of the 15th century, Nos. 538, 539, 540, 542, and Moscow Synodal Typography Library MS No. 130, where only the 11th and 12th prayers bear such titles: "at the Praises," "at Glory to God in the highest") and up to eleven (Metropolitan Cyprian: "at Matins 11 prayers, and the 12th prayer after *Praise the Lord from the heavens* at the bowing of heads" [^2399]). From this there was but one step unto our present-day practice with its 12 prayers at the Six Psalms (what was lacking, presumably, was the 9th prayer), represented in manuscripts from the 11th century: Euchologion of the Moscow Rumiantsev Museum Sevastianov Collection No. 474 of the 11th century, Athos Vatopedi No. 133/174 of the 14th century, Sinai No. 988 of the 15th century, Moscow Synodal Library No. 616/360 of the 16th century, etc. [^2400] and cf. below). As for printed Sluzhebniki, all Greek editions possess our present composition; of the Slavic, the Moscow editions of 1602 and 1647 lack the 9th, having consequently only 11 at the Six Psalms; the Sluzhebnik of Petro Mohyla of 1629 remarks concerning it that it is prescribed among the Greeks and leaves its recitation unto personal preference; from the Moscow edition of 1658 onward it is inserted, and the composition receives its present form.

### Their Recitation (*Chtenie ikh*)

The morning prayers are read by the priest in the same setting as the evening prayers, that is, before the Royal Doors, bareheaded, in the epitrachelion; "in some places also in the phelonion," adds the Typikon here, having in view, of course, the custom—canonized as early as the Stoglav Council—for secular priests *(Orig. p. 625)* to celebrate the whole of Matins, as of Vespers, in the phelonion. Such a setting of recitation, instilling the thought of repentance and our unworthiness,...

...recalls also the former practice of beginning Matins in the narthex. The reading of the prayers begins at the 4th psalm of the Six Psalms, **"O Lord God of my salvation"** (Ps. 87 [LXX; Masoretic 88]), evidently in order to afford the priest the possibility of hearing at least half of the Six Psalms.

Greek manuscripts and the printed Greek Typikon (Jerusalem): "When the superior begins Psalm 102: **Bless the Lord, O my soul**, the priest himself says the prayers of Matins, standing uncovered before the Holy Doors"; so likewise the oldest Slavic; yet later Greek and Slavic manuscripts (Moscow Synodal Library Greek No. 381 and Slavic No. 332/385 of the end of the 14th century) prescribe beginning the prayers at the psalm **"O Lord God of my salvation,"** as at present. # THE GREAT LITANY AT MATINS (*Velikaya ekteniya na utrene*)

The Six Psalms is not so much a prayer as an outpouring of the feelings of the believer; the Great Ektenia following it, which stands in general at the beginning of all the most important services, constitutes already a defined, precise prayer, embracing the primary needs of a Christian. The Six Psalms at Matins, like the Introductory Psalm at Vespers, attunes the soul unto such prayer, awakening within it indefinite, yet all the more profound religious feelings; they prepare the way for the supplications of the Great Ektenia; at the Divine Liturgy there is no such preparation, because the faithful approach it having been sufficiently prepared by the whole cycle of the daily services, and especially by the Hours, and because in general they approach it with a greater elevation of spirit, which arises spontaneously from the consciousness of the importance of the impending Mystery.

=== LEAF p755 ===
The Typikon prescribes that the Great Ektenia at Matins, as at Vespers, be proclaimed by the priest, since the deacon appears only at the Polyeleos. The priest proclaims it before the Holy Doors, from that place where he read the morning prayers; and after its exclamation, the priest "having departed, stands in his own place," that is, together with the brethren, outside the altar, as at Vespers (see above, p. 533).

### According to Ancient Typika (*Po starym ustavam*)

The Georgian manuscript does not mention the ektenia.

Greek manuscripts and printed editions: "Then (the priest) says the ektenia and proclaims the exclamation."

So likewise the ancient Slavic manuscripts; later manuscripts and the Old Believer Typikon: "The priest or deacon performs the great diaconal service" [^2402].

According unto the *Hieratikon*, *(Orig. p. 626)* the choirs chant **"Lord, have mercy"** at this ektenia (which is proclaimed, like all things there, by the deacon) alternately [^2403].

# "GOD IS THE LORD" (*Bog Gospod'*)

Being a more joyful service than Vespers, being celebrated during the hours of Christ's Nativity and Resurrection, Matins, in place of Psalm 140 and the specifically evening psalms following it, possesses in their stead a general festive psalm in a far more solemn execution than those psalms, namely excerpts from Psalm 117 [LXX; Masoretic 118] under the title **"God is the Lord"** (Gr. *Θεὸς Κύριος*). Among the Hebrews, this psalm was designated for chanting on a certain great feast ("order the feast," v. 27), on the occasion of the manifestation unto the people of a great mercy from God, for which all Israel was bound to give thanks unto God, as for salvation from death and annihilation ("I shall not die, but live," v. 17). The well-known Paschal verse **"This is the day..."** is found in this psalm. Among the psalms, this is almost the most joyful, yielding in this regard perhaps only unto Psalm 67 [LXX; Masoretic 68]. Out of such a psalm is the hymn **"God is the Lord"** composed.

Moreover, from it the most powerful and characteristic verses were selected for this hymn, presenting in brief the content of the entire psalm. The principal among these five "selected" verses—**"God is the Lord and hath appeared unto us; blessed is He that cometh in the name of the Lord"**—is composed of two passages of the psalm arranged in reverse order (vv. 27a and 26), of which the first directly bears the meaning that the Lord (Yahweh) is the true God and that He has now revealed Himself unto us with special tangibility ("hath appeared," from the Heb. "hath illuminated," "hath shone forth"). With this is linked the fact that he who comes now, under such circumstances, in the name of the Lord is blessed ("in the name of the Lord" may also be referred unto "blessed"). The fact that these latter words were applied unto the Savior at His solemn Entry into Jerusalem, and that, according unto His words, with them they shall meet Him also at the Second Coming [^2404], all this points unto Him precisely as that "Coming One," Whose coming is equivalent unto the manifestation of God Himself and reveals God unto all.

Now the coming unto us of the Son of God, the manifestation of God in His flesh, according unto the teaching of the Apostles, possessed as it were three stages: the Incarnation, the Resurrection, and the future Coming [^2405]. The night inspires thought concerning each of these; each of them is commemorated in the night service as having been assigned unto the night (**"Glory to God in the highest,"** **"Behold, the Bridegroom"**). Yet at Sunday Matins the thought of the second, central "manifestation"—in the Resurrection—is most natural. Symeon of Thessalonica refers the verse unto the first and the last: "Well is it said in this hymn: **'God is the Lord and hath appeared unto us'**—the Word of God incarnate; **'blessed is He that cometh'**—He Himself as God in the flesh; **'in the name of the Lord'**—in the name of the Father and His own. And it is exceedingly fitting that by night...

=== LEAF p757 ===
...there is represented *(Orig. p. 627)* the Nativity—the manifestation of the Savior in the flesh, even as this occurred by night, and unto us who sit in darkness and the shadow of ignorance 'a great light hath shined,' according unto the words of Isaiah. Therefore do we also in this life, as in the night, await at midnight the coming of the Bridegroom of our souls, beloved by us and loving us" [^2406]. Unto the Resurrection the verses of the psalm accompanying **"God is the Lord"** are likewise most suited—excluding the verse **"O give thanks unto the Lord"** [Ps. 117:1], taken as the opening verse of the psalm: all of them depict the life of the Savior, full of persecutions (v. 11: "they compassed me about..."), accompanied by the consciousness that the death threatening from enemies would prove powerless over Him (v. 17: "I shall not die, but live...") and would entail the founding of the Church (vv. 22 and 23: "the stone which the builders rejected..."; cf. the verses to the evening prokeimenon).

### Ancient Use of Psalm 117 and "God is the Lord" (*Drevnee upotreblenie ps. 117 i "Bog Gospod'"*)

Psalm 117 [LXX; Masoretic 118] is an alleluiatic psalm (in the LXX) and was included among the Hebrews in the composition of the so-called "Hallel" (Pss. 112–117, Heb. 113–118; Introductory Chapter, p. 12), chanted at Passover, Pentecost, Tabernacles (Sukkot), and the Feast of Dedication (Hanukkah), but originally intended for Passover; occupying the final place in the Hallel, Psalm 117 entered into it later than the other psalms [^2407] and, apparently, was designated more for Tabernacles [^2408] than for Passover (moreover, in the Hebrew Bible it is not an alleluiatic psalm). Having presumably been sung by the Savior and the Apostles at the institution of the Eucharist, the psalm was suited for the Liturgy; and already the Liturgy of the *Apostolic Constitutions* takes from it the verse chanted today (vv. 26, 27a) as a preparatory verse for Communion (together with **"Glory to God in the highest"** and Hosanna) [^2409], as does likewise the Liturgy of Basil and Chrysostom (both without the transposition of the psalm's words); the Liturgy of St. James places the verse **"Blessed is He that cometh"** after the **"Holy, Holy,"** inserting before "that cometh" the words "Who came and" [^2410]. At Matins it is mentioned by the well-known 7th-century description of Sinai Matins, the *Hypotyposis*, and the *Diatyposis* (Introductory Chapter, pp. 295, 359). Yet the Chanted Matins (*asmatikos orthros*), even in the time of Symeon of Thessalonica, did not have **"God is the Lord."**

Judging from the fact that Psalm 117 in the LXX is an alleluiatic psalm—that in the Western Church the Alleluia is considered so specifically a Paschal and festive chant that it is not sung during fasts—and that among us the Alleluia constitutes a feature of the Lenten divine service, which has remained most faithful unto antiquity, one may think that **"God is the Lord"** was also once sung with Alleluia or replaced by it (cf. the content of the 2nd and 3rd morning prayers).

### Manner of Execution (*Obraz ispolneniya*)

In accordance with the greater significance of Matins in comparison with Vespers and the character of the hymn **"God is the Lord,"** it is chanted to the richest melody—the melody *(Orig. p. 628)* of the troparion, which serves as it were as its final refrain. More specifically concerning the chanting of **"God is the Lord,"** the Typikon in the present place states:

"And the appointed canonarch, who pronounces the prokeimena at Vespers, having made a bow unto the superior, proclaims: **God is the Lord** with its verses in the current Tone, and we chant the Sunday troparion."

This incomplete instruction is supplemented in other places. Chapter 9 of the Typikon, concerning the singing of the Alleluia that replaces **"God is the Lord"** and is executed according unto the same rubric, states:

"The canonarch, standing directly before the Holy Doors, pronounces **Alleluia** in the Tone of the Octoechos, reciting also these verses; and we chant the Triadica (*Troichny*); but if it be **God is the Lord**, we chant **God is the Lord** in the Tone of the troparion of the Saint with its verses, and we recite the troparion of the Saint." In the order of Matins for the First Monday of the Great Fast: "The appointed monk or priest recites **Alleluia** in the current Tone of the Octoechos with its verses. And after each verse the clerics chant **Alleluia** thrice."

The Sluzhebnik: "They begin to chant **God is the Lord** according unto the Tone of the troparion of the day; the verse... and **God is the Lord** is sung four times; then the troparion is recited... And while **God is the Lord** is being sung, the canonarch departs and stands in the midst of the temple bareheaded, and recites quietly (slowly?) the above-written verses; and he stands there until they begin the Theotokion; then he makes a bow in the place where he stands (toward the Holy Doors) and, having turned (unto the west), bows unto the choirs and departs. Thus does he do also when there is **Alleluia**."

All these rubrics presume, apparently, that the canonarch first chants **"God is the Lord"** to the Tone, and then chants its verses, whereupon unto each verse the choirs (alternately, which is why there are four verses) respond by chanting **"God is the Lord."** Therefore no proclamation of the Tone is required before **"God is the Lord"**: the canonarch demonstrates how to chant it. In practice, for the most part the verses with the initial **"God is the Lord"** are recited by the priest or deacon who proclaimed the ektenia, while **"God is the Lord"** is chanted four times [^2411].

### "God is the Lord" According to Ancient Typika (*"Bog Gospod'" po drevnim ustavam*)

According unto the well-known 7th-century description of Sinai Matins, **"God is the Lord"** was chanted by skilled chanters, and the presbyter began it (Introductory Chapter, pp. 295–296). The Studite-Alexian Typikon, contrasting **"God is the Lord"** as a joyful hymn with the Six Psalms with its penitential content, requires a corresponding distinction also in the chanting of the one and the other: whereas the Six Psalms is chanted "subdued" (*v'ly*, see above), "straightway on bright and festive days (when alone **God is the Lord** was sung according unto the Studite Typikon) we cry **God is the Lord** brightly and clearly, as having truly perceived the divine manifestation of light suddenly illuminating us, driving far away every cloud of mourning and dejection, and wondrously and brightly bestowing upon us serene joy, rejoicing in this and being unable to contain within ourselves the greatness of this ineffable gladness"; a similar manner of chanting is required by the Studite Typikon also for the **Alleluia** [^2412].

One of the Western Studite Typika (the Typikon of San Nicola di Casole, ch. 1) requires that the brethren stand during the chanting of **"God is the Lord,"** *(Orig. p. 629)* as at the Epistle and Gospel [^2413].

The Georgian manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon does not indicate the manner of execution of **"God is the Lord"** in the order of the Vigil, but makes a note concerning this in the rubrics of weekday Matins (see there).

Greek manuscripts and printed editions: "The monk appointed from Vespers for the prokeimenon, having made the customary bow unto the superior, chants **God is the Lord**, and we chant the troparion..."

## Troparia at "God is the Lord" (*Tropari na Bog Gospod'*)

Having transitioned from a sorrowful and penitential disposition unto a festive and joyful one, and from Old Testament sentiments unto that vivid premonition of salvation that is provided by the selection of passages from Psalm 117 [LXX; Masoretic 118], the hymns of Matins now pass over into direct Christian language. This is done through the appending of the troparion unto the psalm. At this moment the troparion receives its most solemn execution: even at weekday Matins it is sung twice, which is done at no other service, not even the Divine Liturgy; even if there are two troparia for commemorations equal in rank, here the first troparion is sung twice; this is primarily in view of the distribution of the singing between the two choirs. Since the troparion is here properly a refrain unto the psalm (as it was also according unto its original designation), and for a psalm or section of psalms the Lesser Doxology is considered an indispensable conclusion, another troparion (if there be one) and a Theotokion, or a Theotokion alone, is linked with the troparion by means of the latter—which Theotokion always...
