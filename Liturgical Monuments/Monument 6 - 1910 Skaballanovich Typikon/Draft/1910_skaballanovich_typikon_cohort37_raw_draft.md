### Compline

Subject unto no fewer modifications than the Hours was the order of Compline (*apodeipnon* / повечерие), and in like manner the particularities of the diverse Typika exercised no influence upon the development of this office: in both the Studite and Jerusalem Typika it shared a common history. The earliest notices concerning this office lead us to understand that in both branches of the Typikon it possessed a very similar, if not identical, composition. Thus the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis*, distinguishing two forms of Compline (*ta apodeipna* / τὰ ἀπόδειπνα)—complete (*holoklēra* / ὁλόκληρα) and abbreviated for festal days—direct that the second be commenced from **“God is with us”** (*Hypotyposis*) or from **“He that dwelleth in the help of the Most High”**, that is, Psalm 90 [91] (*Diatyposis*) [^1701]. If one compares with these rubrics the order of Compline in the most ancient Horologion according unto the rule of the Lavra of St. Sabas, Sinai Library *(Orig. p. 382)* MS No. 863 of the 8th–9th centuries, one cannot fail to notice that in both instances approximately one and the same office is intended. And in this Horologion, Compline, as we have seen (see above, p. 298), not yet possessing even this title, but designated “Prayer at the 1st Hour of the Night,” has a structure very close unto our present Great Compline in its first part, namely: Psalms 4, 12 [13], 26 [27], **“Glory,”** Psalms 30 [31], 90 [91], 6 (at present: 4, 6, 12 [13], 24 [25], 30 [31], 90 [91]), **“God is with us,”** Alleluia with the verses **“Look down and hear me, O Lord...”** and **“In peace in the selfsame...”** (the present verses unto the troparia **“Mine unseen enemies”**), then the troparia **“Mine unseen enemies...”** or others according unto the day, **“Lord, have mercy”** thrice, **“Deliver not unto beasts the soul that confesseth Thee...”** (a verse or

=== LEAF p362 ===
a prayer?)—thereafter the manuscript breaks off. A similar composition, yet somewhat more complex and closer unto the present one, is exhibited by Compline in the Studite-Alexis Typikon:
“Having chanted the 6 psalms, then **‘God is with us’** and **‘The Bodiless Nature’** and the Creed (**‘I believe’**) and **‘All-Holy Sovereign Lady’** and **‘Our Father’** and **‘Lord, have mercy’** 30 times, then they make 3 prostrations... and likewise again at the 3rd prostration to say: **‘Come, let us worship,’** then Psalm 7, **‘O Lord, rebuke me not in Thine anger,’** and the Trisagion and **‘Our Father’** and **‘Lord, have mercy’** 12 times, and a prayer having been said by the priest, they depart unto their cells” [^1702].

Such a Compline (*pavechernitsa*) is termed *ispoln* (complete, full) and, among other times, is chanted from the Monday of Thomas Week (Antipascha) until Ascension. Yet it is remarkable that even for the Apostles’ (St. Peter’s) Fast a shorter Compline is indicated: “The 6 psalms and **‘God is with us’** and **‘The Bodiless Nature’** and the Creed (**‘I believe’**) and **‘All-Holy Sovereign Lady’** and the Trisagion and **‘Our Father’** and **‘Lord, have mercy’** 12 times, and they finish” [^1703]. In the first Compline there are clearly two parts; the second is not divided into parts. Since it is improbable that during a fast Compline would be shorter than during the semi-festal period of Pentecost, especially in view of the fact that the Studite-Alexis Typikon requires an abbreviated Compline not only for Sundays and feast days, but also, for instance, for the afterfeast of Ascension, it must be that in both places one and the same order of Compline is intended, and its second form is a representative of its most ancient type, accidentally left uncorrected on the pages of the Typikon after its complication with new insertions. This daily-furious/lenten order of Compline according unto the Studite-Alexis Typikon was abbreviated for feasts and other alleviated days by means of curtailing its initial psalms; namely: “on Saturday evening and on a Feast of the Lord, Compline from **‘God is with us’** and the rest and **‘Lord, have mercy’** 15 times; and from the Ascension of the Lord until All Saints, from **‘He that dwelleth in the help’** and the rest.” In the first case, consequently, all initial psalms were omitted and their place was taken by the canticle of Isaiah; in the second case, a series of psalms up unto Psalm 90 [91] was omitted, concerning which it is unknown what place it occupied among them—whether the last, as at present, or penultimate, as in the Sinai Horologion of the 8th–9th centuries. The first kind, that is, the more abbreviated Compline, was appointed, besides Sunday and feast days, on Great Friday, on Ascension evening, and in all probability in other analogous cases; the second, besides the afterfeast of Ascension, on the eve of the 1st Saturday of Lent “for the sake of the feast of St. Theodore,” on the eve of Lazarus Saturday (Palm Saturday), on the first three days of Mid-Pentecost, and in all probability on similar semi-festal *(Orig. p. 383)* days [^1704]. It is unknown whether, besides these three forms of Compline, there existed also a special form of it for Great Lent; the data for resolving this question consist solely in the following remark concerning the service of Great Monday: “and the great *methimon* [great compline / μεθέορτον/меθύμνον] is chanted even as in the fast” [^1705]; judging

### The Nocturnal Hours and the Rule of the 12 Psalms

The further development of Compline, wherein it received—in addition unto the two parts already delineated here—a third part as well, and enriched its concluding section, was conditioned on the one hand by its fusion with the Sung Office rite of the Great Pannychis, but especially by the inclusion into it of the office of the so-called “Nocturnal Hours” [^1707], an order which stood in turn in connection with the ancient “Rule of the 12 Psalms” mentioned already by St. John Cassian (see above, p. 216) and included in Horologia and Followed Psalters (*Sledovannye Psaltiri*) as an independent service. A reference unto both rites (the Nocturnal Hours and the 12 Psalms) is found also in the *Diatyposis*:
“It is needful to know that after the feast of Thomas Week (Antipascha), immediately after Compline, we begin the *pannychis*; and until All Saints we perform only the canon (such a composition was possessed by one form of the sung pannychis) and after it the Blameless (the *Amomos* / Psalm 118 [119]); from that time until Ascension we append 6 psalms, at which there are 3 prostrations and standings (*staseis* / στάσεις); after the Exaltation (of the Holy Cross), however, we perform 12 psalms and the other offices until the Great Forty Days; for then at every Trisagion we perform bows (*metanoiai*) with standings” [^1708].

### The Three-Part Compline

Thus the “Rule of the 12 Nocturnal Psalms” increased the number of Compline psalms from the original 6 unto 12, and introduced into it the morning hymn **“Glory to God in the highest,”** since, by virtue of the fusion of this rule with Compline, the latter was in principle intended to continue until morning; while the influence of the *pannychis* inserted into Compline its middle part with Psalm 50 [51] and a canon. In such a complicated, three-part, and already almost entirely modern form do we find Compline in the Western Studite Typika. Furthermore, in the Calabrian recension of these Typika it is simpler in composition. In its full composition (for fasts), according unto its fragmentary description in the Typikon of the Monastery of San Nicola di Casole (ch. 25), in its first part it contained the Six Psalms and **“God is with us”**; in the 2nd, a canon or triodion; in the 3rd, the Blameless (the *Amomos* / Psalm 118 [119]), **“Glory to God in the highest,”** and **“O Lord Almighty”** (prayer); on non-fasting days this Compline was celebrated only up unto **“Glory to God in the highest”**; in Pentecost, up unto the Blameless; on Sundays, feasts, and the Twelve Days (Dodekaēmeron), the first two parts were celebrated, with **“Glory to God in the highest”** and the Trisagion [^1716]. In the Western Studite Typika of the Sicilian recension, Compline is longer. According unto the Typikon of San Salvatore di Messina (the Melite/Mili Typikon), in the Forty Days it has likewise a three-part composition: the 1st part *(Orig. p. 386)* coincides entirely with the 1st part of our Compline (only the concluding prayer **“O Lord, Lord, Who hast delivered us”** is lacking); the 2nd part

### Little Compline

It now remained for Compline to develop its second part into its modern form and, upon its foundation, to form a special kind of Little Compline. This is observed in the Horologia of the Sinai Library MS No. 868 of the 13th–14th centuries and of the Athos-Vatopaidi Library MS No. 350 of the 12th century. In the first, Compline, in the composition of the 1st part of our present Great Compline, possesses the following supplementary part: Psalms 50 [51], 66 [67]

### Great and Daily Compline

The Typikon of the Shio-Mgvime Monastery of the 13th century provides an order for Great Compline (Little Compline is not mentioned) almost literally identical with the present one; the difference in the 1st part being: **“Lord, have mercy”** 40 times, and the priest says: **“O Lord Jesus Christ, our God, have mercy on us.”** Before the troparia **“Mine unseen enemies”** it is remarked: “there are, however, also other troparia of Compline”; before the second part it is remarked: “and we begin the nocturnal service of Great Compline” [^1720]. ### The Midnight Office

The most ancient purely Studite Typika did not acknowledge the Midnight Office (*mesonyktikon* / полунощница), judging by the fact that it is not mentioned by the *Hypotyposis* and the Studite-Alexis Typikon [^1722]. The latter possesses an office corresponding unto the Midnight Office solely for Great Lent. The Studite-Alexis Typikon during Great Lent appended unto Great Compline an unnamed office, in its composition recalling partly our present Midnight Office and partly the 3rd part of Great Compline:
“Concerning Compline in Great Lent. It is to be known that throughout the entire fast, when Great Compline is chanted, upon its completion and after 6 (?) psalms and after the Holy God, they immediately begin the Blameless (*Amomos* / Psalm 118 [119]), not dividing them by tone nor into verses, but all reciting them with one accord; thereafter is chanted the Holy God, then **‘Glory to God in the highest,’** and the troparion **‘With a merciful eye,’** and the other troparion: **‘My life is drawing to a close,’** and the Theotokion: **‘Thou Who wast born for our sake’**; thereafter he reciteth the Prayer of Manasseh, whose beginning is: **‘O Lord Almighty,’** and this other prayer: **‘Grant unto us, O Master,’** the prayer of St. Eustratius: **‘Magnifying, I magnify Thee,’** thereafter **‘Lord, have mercy’** thrice, and **‘Defiled by passions,’** and **‘Lord, have mercy’** 50 times, and a prayer having been said by the priest, the dismissal taketh place” [^1723].

In the representative of the Western ktetorial Typika of the Sicilian recension, in the Typikon of San Salvatore di Messina (the Mili Typikon), the Midnight Office both receives already its proper title and is liberated from elements of Compline. This Typikon likewise speaketh solely of the Great Lenten Midnight Office and provideth for it an order cognate unto the Studite-Alexis: after the customary beginning (from the Trisagion), “the Blameless (*Amomos*) with 2 **‘Glories,’** the Trisagion, and troparia: **‘Have mercy on us...,’** **‘Lord, have mercy...,’** **‘Open unto us the door of compassion,’** **‘A multitude of my transgressions...,’** and nothing more, **‘Lord, have mercy’** 50 times, the prayer from the priest **‘Thou Who at all times,’** and the dismissal” [^1724]. In both places we find the simplest type of the Midnight Office. The Athos-Iviron Synaxarion in the manuscript of the Tiflis Museum MS No. 222 of the 12th century, while providing only the order of the Great Lenten Midnight Office, likewise merges it with Compline and the Nocturnal Hours, precisely in such a way that both are inserted between the first and second parts of the Midnight Office, which possesses a structure very close unto the present one:
“At the beginning of the 10th hour they ring for Compline and celebrate first the Midnight Office without the Blameless (*Amomos*); after the Holy God, **‘Our Father,’** **‘The unseen, unsleeping...’** **‘Glory,’**—**‘How fearful...’** **‘Both now,’**—**‘Unashamed hope,’** **‘Lord, have mercy’** 40 times. The priest: **‘O Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on us,’** the people: **‘Amen’** (Midnight Office); **‘Come, let us worship,’** **‘Have mercy on me, O God...’** (Psalm 50 [51]) and the canon of the Mother of God and of the saint from the Menaion, the Holy God, **‘Our Father,’** **‘Lord, have mercy’** 40 times (the *pannychis* or Compline); **‘Come, let us worship’** and they read through 12 psalms, wherein after every 2 psalms: Holy God, **‘Our Father,’** **‘Lord, have mercy’** 40 times (Nocturnal Hours); the Beatitudes, **‘Glory to God in the highest,’** Holy God, **‘O Lord of Hosts, be with us,’** **‘Lord, have mercy’** 40 times, **‘O Holy Lord Almighty,’** **‘Lord, have mercy’** 40 times (Compline); **‘Come, let us worship...’** **‘I have lifted up mine eyes...’** (Psalm 120 [121]) **‘Behold now, bless ye...’** (Psalm 133 [134]), Holy God, **‘Lord, have mercy’** 12 times” (the second part of the Midnight Office).
This office is designated “the Nocturnal Hours” [^1725].

A still greater development is attained by the Midnight Office in the Jerusalem Typika. And so ancient a Horologion of the Jerusalem type as that of the Sinai Library MS No. 864 of the 9th century (in Sinai MS No. 863 of the 8th–9th centuries there is no Midnight Office, perhaps torn out) possesses 2 Midnight Offices. After the aforementioned 12 nocturnal psalms, appended here unto the Ninth Hour, and 2 Theotokia following them, there follow here two nocturnal canons with corresponding troparia and refrains, and after them similar stichera concluding with a supplication for tranquil sleep. Then follows a special Midnight Office; it begins with the prayers of Kings Hezekiah and Manasseh and with Psalm 90 [91] (at present in the order of Compline); then: Alleluia with our present morning verses from Isaiah (26:9, 11, 15 [printed: 29, 9, 11, 15]), and the “triadic hymn” **“Behold, the Bridegroom cometh”**; then Psalm 118 [119] (the 17th Kathisma), and after it special sessional hymns and Theotokia, with troparia of penitential content. The Sinai Horologion MS No. 865 of the 12th century contains already two Midnight Offices: a daily one with the 17th Kathisma, and a Saturday-Sunday one with the 9th. Both are of identical structure and fall into four parts, with three troparia and two prayers in each part for the daily, and an equal number of troparia and one prayer for the Saturday-Sunday; besides this, at the beginning of the service are three introductory

troparia. The greater part of these troparia serve at present as penitential sessional hymns and dismissal Theotokia at daily services. The prayers are likewise all of a penitential character, wherein among them are found all 4 prayers of our present daily and Saturday Midnight Office. Thus the Midnight Office according unto this monument does not alter for Saturday and Sunday its *(Orig. p. 390)* penitential character, but merely receives additional troparia for the departed (further heightening its sorrowful character). Besides the Midnight Office, the monument possesses also an “inter-hour of the night, chanted at the 3rd hour of the night,” consisting of Psalms 74 [75], 26 [27], and 55 [56] with penitential troparia **“Have mercy on me, O God”** and a prayer. Finally, the Horologion of the Novgorod-Sophia Library at the St. Petersburg Theological Academy MS No. 1052 of the 13th–14th centuries (unto which the Slavonic Sinai MS No. 12 is very close) provides already a Midnight Office of a completely modern type, differing solely in its troparia and prayers. The Horologion has, like the present one, three forms of the Midnight Office: daily, Saturday, and Sunday; yet the latter is of an identical type with the first two. In the daily, the 1st part has the present troparia, while the 2nd part has a different first troparion: **“I am enfeebled by the sleep of despondency...,”** **“With profundity of wisdom...,”** and **“All generations call thee blessed...”** (the troparion **“With the saints give rest”** is absent). The Saturday Midnight Office—troparia: I: **“With vigilant mind let us look up unto God,”** **“Let us shake off slothful sleep, the Bridegroom draweth nigh,”** **“Present our prayers unto the Mother of God”**; II: **“Since the Judge is come, take heed, O my soul,”** **“With profundity of wisdom,”** **“Thee, a city and haven”** (= wall and haven). The Sunday Midnight Office—I: **“The Life of all hath risen from the dead,”** **“That fearful day,”** **“Thee, an impregnable city”** (= an indestructible wall); II: **“The tomb being opened and hades lamenting,”** **“Grant me the vigilance of the wise virgins,”** **“Our prayers, O Mother of God.”** The prayers common unto all three Midnight Offices are: 1) “The prayer of St. John Chrysostom unto the Holy Lady Theotokos”: **“Having enjoyed many and great gifts from God Who created me”**; 2) **“Thou Who at all times”**; and 3) the concluding prayer: **“Remember, O Lord, the whole world; have mercy on all, defend all; sail with them that sail upon the sea.”** In addition, at each of the three Midnight Offices, after **“Thou Who at all times,”** a single proper prayer is appointed: at the daily—**“O Master, Lord Jesus Christ, Father of compassions and God of all consolation, have mercy also upon me, Thine unworthy servant”**; at the Saturday—**“O Almighty God, Creator of creation above and beneath, Who hast appointed the sun for the enlightenment of the day, and the moon and the stars for the illumination of the night, Who didst command, O Lord, light to shine forth out of darkness”**; at the Sunday—**“O Lord our God, True Radiance of the Father”** [^1726]. In comparison with the preceding monument, there is here a significant differentiation, especially in relation unto the Sunday order of the Midnight Office. Yet in comparison with our present orders, in the 2nd part of the Midnight Office its mortuary character is not so strictly maintained, while the Sunday office has many penitential motifs.
