150. 1 Cor 14:16. "He that occupieth the room of the unlearned," Slavonic: *nevezhdy*, Greek: *ἰδιώτου*. An *ἰδιώτης* is a person not holding an official or leading position, or a layman / non-specialist: for example, ordinary citizens in distinction from the king (Herodotus II, 81, and elsewhere); the general public in distinction from the scholars of the philosophical school (Plutarch, *Symposiacs* I, 1); non-ecstatics and non-diviners in distinction from a visionary (Pausanias, *Description of Greece: Corinth* 13). In agreement with all these meanings, the word here clearly signifies every member of the liturgical assembly in distinction from the one coming forward with a revelation, in this particular case with speaking in tongues. Consequently, the word does not stand in the sense of a casual visitor unto the assembly—for example, an unbeliever, as in verse 24, where the identical word appears—nor in the generic sense of an ignorant or uneducated person, still less in the modern colloquial sense of this word, which certain scholars likewise allowed, rendering it, for example, in German as *Idiot* (Binterim, A., *Die vorzüglichsten Denkwürdigkeiten der Christ-Katholischen Kirche*, IV, 1, Mainz, 1827, p. 319). "He that occupieth the room" indicates that the status of an *ἰδιώτης* is not permanent, but situational: all, except the one actively ministering during divine worship, are *ἰδιῶται*, and each becomes one at a given moment when he does not have a revelation (Zahn-Bachmann, pp. 420–421). Here, therefore, is already given a rather sharp division (more so than in the synagogue) of the worshipers into celebrants and laity, although this demarcation is not yet rigid, but fluid.

Recently in Russian literature the opinion was expressed that into the composition of the ancient veneration of martyrs in the 2nd–4th centuries there entered likewise religious dances (Konovalov, D., "Religiozny ekstaz v russkom misticheskom sektantstve" [Religious Ecstasy in Russian Mystical Sectarianism], *Bogoslovsky Vestnik*, 1907, issue 10, p. 351; 1908, issue 4, pp. 707–728). In the acts of the martyrs, which often conclude with a description of liturgical assemblies in honor of the martyrs, there is no mention of dances in their honor: to interpret the expression in the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* concerning the celebration of his memory "with gladness and joy — *ἀγαλλιάσει καὶ χαρᾷ*" as referring unto dance (Konovalov, p. 707) is arbitrary. Only a few monuments of the 3rd and 4th centuries speak of these dances as a local and tolerated phenomenon. Thus, according unto the testimony of St. Gregory of Nyssa, St. Gregory the Wonderworker (3rd century), yielding unto the inclinations of his flock and in order to divert them from pagan festivals, permitted modest dances (*τὶ μικρὸν ὑποκιρτᾷν*) in honor of the martyrs (*Homily on Ecclesiastes* 6).
