[^613]: The monument was discovered by the Italian archaeologist Gian Francesco Gamurrini in 1884 in a Latin manuscript of c. the 12th century of the Confraternity of St. Mary in Arezzo. The opening leaves of the work are lost, for which reason neither the title of the work nor the name of the author has been preserved. In the margins of the manuscript in two places in the corner stand the letters *PE*, which are considered an abbreviation of the title. Indirect evidence in the work points unto the end of the 4th century as the time of the origin of the *Peregrinatio*: the Jerusalem edifices are described in such a manner that Constantine's structures can be recognized in them; mention is made of a recently built church in Edessa, which occurred in 372 under the Emperor Valens; of the Persian possession of the left bank of the Tigris, which was ceded unto them by the Emperor Jovian in 363; of the Thebaid ascetics (among them St. Anthony the Great, † 356, and St. Pachomius, † 340); on the other hand, the traveler is unacquainted with Jerome's translation of the Holy Scriptures, or the decree of Pope Gelasius of 494 against the reading of apocrypha—the Letter of Abgar, the Acts of the Holy Apostle Thomas, and of the Holy Martyr Thecla; Justinian's buildings in Sinai and Constantinople are not mentioned. The pilgrim came unto the Holy Land *de extremis porro terris* (from the distant ends of the earth); judging by the fact that she compares the Euphrates with the Rhône (*Rhodanus*), she was presumably a native of Gaul; she was an educated and well-read woman; she travels as a noble personage—with a retinue; she is provided with a military escort; bishops receive and accompany her. In Palestine she remained three years, dwelling in Jerusalem and making excursions from thence. Having returned unto Constantinople, she described her pilgrimage unto the "sisters" (fleshly sisters or nuns?). The identification of the pilgrim by some with Galla Placidia, daughter of Theodosius the Great, and by others with Sylvia, sister of the Prefect of Constantinople Rufinus (an Aquitanian), is rather arbitrary. The monument is, of course, of invaluable liturgical importance, yet one cannot but regret that it belongs unto a foreigner and a woman: the pilgrim observes only ceremonies, litanies, how they approach the bishop's hand, and the like, while concerning the content of divine worship she restricts herself unto the remarks: "psalms and hymns were sung," "prayers were read." Nevertheless the order of divine worship is presented rather distinctly. The monument was published in the *Pravoslavny Palestinsky Sbornik*, fascicle 20, 1889, from a transcript made directly from the manuscript itself, and provided with a Russian translation and preface (by I. V. Pomialovsky), whence the present data concerning it are also drawn (pp. I–IX).

[^619]: It is unknown whether *missa* signifies the dismissal or the Liturgy, which is designated by the pilgrim at times as *oblatio*, *offertur*, and rarely *sacramentum*. The name *missa* was adopted for the Liturgy in the West either from the solemn formula of dismissal therein: "*Ite, missa est*", or from the fact that it is celebrated after the dismissal of the catechumens and constitutes the conclusion of lengthy church services. If in the pilgrim *missa* signifies the Liturgy, then daily in the Church of Jerusalem two Liturgies were celebrated—one after Matins, and another after Vespers (on Great Thursday and Holy Saturday two Liturgies—*oblatio*—morning and evening, are clearly indicated by the pilgrim, yet in different churches: *Peregrinatio*, §§ 35, 38), inasmuch as the description of Vespers in the pilgrim also concludes with the words *missa fit* (the opinion concerning two Liturgies was expressed by G. F. Gamurrini, *S. Hilarii tractatus de mysteriis et hymni et S. Silviae Aquitanae peregrinatio*, Rome, 1887, pp. 77–78).
