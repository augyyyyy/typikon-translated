### Inter-Ode Breaks [Mesodia]

However diverse the melodies of the kanons at Sunday Matins may be, nevertheless such a vast stream of canticles (15 × 8 = 120) would exceed the capacity of even the most untiring attention were this stream continuous. In view of this, two large breaks are made within it, by which the kanon is divided into 3 (the sacred number) parts. Since the kanon in principle ought to have 9 odes, for these "inter-ode breaks" [*mezhdopesniya*]

=== LEAF p832 ===
the place chosen is "after" (*apo tes* [Gr. ἀπὸ τῆς]—from, off, after) the 3rd and 6th odes. The inter-ode break (*mesodion* [Gr. μεσῴδιον]), in order to afford rest from the chanting of the kanon and to revive the attention of the listeners, must provide something completely opposite in character unto the kanon. The kanon is a hymn of praise with an especially intricate melody. The inter-ode break accordingly gives prayer instead of praise, reading instead of song, a simpler chant instead of an intricate melody. Thus the inter-ode break at the kanon consists of 3 parts: prayer, reading, and chanting. Since the parts into which the kanon is divided by the inter-ode breaks are analogous unto kathismata, more specifically, unto this composition of the inter-ode breaks was given the identical form possessed by the intervals between the kathismata, that is, they consist of the little litany, sessional hymns or the kontakia with ikoi replacing them, and a reading.

### Antiquity of the Mesodion

The well-known description of Sinai Matins of the 6th–7th century already mentions the inter-ode breaks after the 3rd and 6th odes of the kanon, wherein regarding them it is given to understand that they consisted of certain artistic hymns begun by the priest (Introductory Chapters, 295, 296). The *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* mention only 2 readings at the kanon [^2565]; the Studite-Alexian Typikon, besides the readings after the 3rd and 6th odes, speaks of a sessional hymn after the 3rd ode and a kontakion after the 6th [^2566].

### Litanies after Ode 3 and Ode 6

The little litanies after the 3rd and 6th odes of the kanon can mark the exceptional position they occupy at Matins only by their exclamations. The latter, naturally, are new in comparison with the preceding ones. Yet at the identical time the exclamations here do not continue, as one might have expected, the development of thought of the preceding ones, but—in view of the fact that the kanon is regarded as a completely independent part of Matins, or as a service within a service—they begin a new train of thoughts. Specifically, the exclamation of the litany after the 3rd ode glorifies God simply only as God: "For Thou art our God…", while the exclamation after the 6th ode gives an exhaustive conception of Him as King and Savior: "For Thou art the King of peace *(Orig. p. 688)* (*eirenes* [Gr. εἰρήνης]) and the Savior…" The exclamations conclude the 5th and 6th morning prayers, and the 1st and 6th evening prayers.

Of these two litanies, the Typikon directs the deacon to pronounce the first, and the priest the second. This is because up to the 3rd ode the deacon still participates in the serving, having begun this participation with the Polyeleos or with the Blameless [*Amomoi*]; from the 3rd ode until the 9th, however, as a portion of the kanon less

=== LEAF p833 ===
festive and filled with readings, the priest alone ministers. The pronunciation by the deacon of the litany after the 3rd ode with his removal from the litany after the 6th ode is consequently analogous unto according him, out of the 3 litanies at the evening kathisma, only the last. The litanies after the 3rd and 6th odes of the kanon are pronounced before the holy doors: the first because it is pronounced by the deacon; the second because after the litany of the 3rd ode (for the sessional hymn and the reading) the priest and deacon "are stripped of the sacred vestments, and having gone forth (from the altar), they stand in the customary places." For the identical reason it is noted in the Typikon only regarding the 1st litany that its exclamation is "within the altar."

### Variants of the Exclamations and Ancient Typika concerning the Litanies

The present exclamations unto these litanies are given by all manuscript Sluzhebniki, but the Greek for the 2nd litany has: "For Thou art the King of peace, and unto Thee the glory…"; so also the printed *Euchologion*, whereas the *Hieratikon* is as with us; up to the Moscow *Sluzhebnik* of 1658 inclusively: "… King unto the world [or: unto peace] and Savior of souls…"; only in the *Sluzhebnik* of Petro Mohyla: "unto peace" [*mirovi*]. The Georgian and Greek manuscripts do not speak of litanies; Slavic manuscripts of the 14th century indicate the exclamations as well, but from the 15th century onward they agree with the Greek; concerning the actions of the priest and deacon only the "Order of Patriarch Philotheos" speaks, and that with an addition concerning the priest's pronunciation of the litany: "uncovered" [^2567].

### Sessional Hymn and Reading after Ode 3

Since the kanon, whose foundation is constituted by the Old Testament canticles, is regarded as something analogous unto kathismata, at its inter-ode breaks after the little litany there follow after the 3rd ode a sessional hymn and a reading, and after the 6th ode a kontakion with an ikos, and likewise a reading. From this normal order at Sunday Matins a departure is made because, in addition to the Sunday kontakion and ikos, it is necessary to chant at the kanon also the kontakia and ikoi of the Menaion. Since after the 6th ode of the kanon the Typikon does not permit chanting more than one kontakion and ikos, the Menaion kontakion—and by implication also the ikos (of which the General Menaion speaks directly)—is transferred unto the 3rd ode, and after them is chanted the sessional hymn of the Menaion, "Glory… now and ever…" Theotokion, with which in the Menaion the sessional hymn is always supplied. Neither the Typikon nor even the General Menaion, which gives the rule for combining with the Sunday service two saints, indicates what is to be chanted after the 3rd ode when two saints coincide on Sunday, perhaps because it is taken for granted that one must chant for both the kontakia, ikoi, and sessional hymns after the 3rd

=== LEAF p834 ===
ode. Thus it is directed to do in the Typikon under September 8 (if the afterfeast falls on Sunday). As regards the reading after the 3rd ode, its content is not more closely defined in *(Orig. p. 689)* the Typikon, but judging by the fact that in Chapter 10 of the Typikon a reading at Matins is required, besides the commentary on the appointed Epistle and Gospel of the day, also from the *Margarita* of Chrysostom and Symeon Metaphrastes, while for the commentaries the three preceding readings suffice, after the 3rd ode there must be a reading from these latter books (on Sunday—from the *Margarita*, as the most important book).

### According unto Ancient and Other Typika

Manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon all speak only of a sessional hymn after the 3rd ode, but this is because the kontakion of the Menaion according unto them was chanted after the 6th ode (see below). According unto the *Hieratikon*, if it is the commemoration of a celebrated saint, the reader reads (after the 3rd ode) the Sunday kontakion and ikos, then the 2nd choir chants the sessional hymn of the saint, and the 1st choir the Theotokion; if, however, a saint is not celebrated, the Sunday kontakion and ikos are read after the 6th ode, (and after the 3rd ode) the choir chants the sessional hymn of the saint and the Theotokion; if the kontakion and ikos of the saint are read after the 6th ode, the choirs chant their conclusions [^2568].

### Kontakion and Ikos after Ode 6

Like the kanon itself, the inter-ode breaks steadily increase in solemnity. In view of this, after the 6th ode there is appointed not a sessional hymn, but a kontakion and ikos—hymns close in melody unto sessional hymns, specifically of troparic melody, which is more solemn than the sessional melody. By this there is introduced into Matins a new genre of church hymn, hitherto unused at the Vigil, occupying in the hierarchy of hymnography the second rank—after the troparion; like the latter, depicting the very essence of the celebrated event, but usually from a different aspect than the troparion; if the troparion presents a picture of the external side of the event, the kontakion presents the internal, or vice versa (cf. the troparion and kontakion of Theophany, or of the Ascension, or of the Transfiguration). Moreover, the kontakion usually depicts the very essence of the celebrated event more fully than the troparion (cf. the troparion and kontakion of Pascha, of the Nativity of Christ), for which reason in those cases when at a church service room can be given unto only a single hymn of the feast, it is accorded unto the kontakion; so it happens always at the Typika [*Izobrazitel'nye*], at Small Compline on feasts, and at the Lenten Hours in Passion Week.

### History

*Kontakion* [Gr. Κοντάκιον] is derived from *kontos* [Gr. κοντός]—the rod upon which a scroll of parchment was rolled; the diminutive [Gr. κονδάκιον] is a scroll, a book in the form of a scroll [^2569], otherwise termed an *iletarion* [Gr. ἰλητάριον] [^2570] (*kondwaon* being a corrupted pronunciation); of such scrolls not a few have been preserved in the libraries of the East dating from the 10th–11th centuries, the epoch when books had already assumed their present form; perhaps upon such scrolls the most important books were transcribed, or else they were more convenient for liturgical chant use. *Ikos* [Gr. οἶκος], house; yet the Syriac *beth*, house, can also signify a stanza (as the Italian *stanza* means both room and stanza), and the ikoi are of specifically Syriac origin, and for *(Orig. p. 690)* each feast there was an entire sequence of them—up to 24, so that they precisely constituted a sequence of strophes. Concerning Romanos the Melodist (6th c.), the founder of this genre of church hymnody, and concerning the other composers of ikoi, as well as regarding their primordial form—see the Introductory Chapters, 329–331. According unto the Studite Typikon these were the most solemn hymns of festal divine services; in the most ancient manuscript of this Typikon of the 11th–12th century they alone are placed in the menologion section of the Typikon, exhausting it; for the most important days of the year in this manuscript there is given one kontakion and ikos each with an obligatory prosomoion [*podoben*] for each, while for great feasts the kontakion is set to musical notation; and before the conclusion of the ikos, which is always identical with the conclusion of the kontakion, stands the rubric in cinnabar: "the people" [Slav. *lyudie*] (that is, its ending is chanted by the entire congregation) [^2571].

### Sunday Kontakia and Ikoi

Sunday kontakia depict primarily the fruits of Christ's resurrection and its internal aspect, whereas the ikoi at times also provide a depiction of the historical event (Tones 5, 7, 8). Both serve as a complement unto the troparia, illuminating the event from those aspects from which it is not touched upon by the corresponding troparion.

For certain kontakia model melodies (prosomoia) are likewise indicated in the Octoechos: for the kontakion of Tone 1—**"When Thou shalt come"** (*Egda priideshi*—the kontakion of Meatfare Sunday); Tone 3 **"Today the Virgin"** (*Deva dnes'*); Tone 4 **"Thou hast appeared"** (*Yavilsya esi*—Theophany); Tone 5 **"The Word Co-beginningless"** (*Sobeznachal'noe Slovo*); Tone 8 **"As the first-fruits"** (*Yako nachatki*—the kontakion of All Saints).

### The Prolog

Like the hymn after the 6th ode of the kanon, the reading at this place is likewise exceptional, not a continuation of the preceding readings, but a new one, specifically the Life of the saint celebrated on that day, drawn from a special collection termed the *Prolog* (*prologos* [Gr. πρόλογος], properly a foreword, since in all probability the title of the foreword was erroneously taken for the title of the book) or *Synaxarion* [^2576] (*synaxarion* [Gr. συναξάριον], collection). Dedicated unto the memory of the saint, replacing the entire service unto him if for any reason it is omitted on Sunday, this reading is regarded by the Typikon as especially important, not being omitted even on Sundays with special commemorations [^2577].

### History

=== LEAF p837 ===
The reading of Acts of the Martyrs on the commemoration day of the martyrs was already in custom in the 2nd–3rd centuries, and in the 4th century it attained universal dissemination (Introductory Chapters, 68, 174), constituting, of course, a prominent and significant part of the divine service of that period, which was modest in liturgical material for such days. Even the Studite-Alexian Typikon restricts the service unto certain saints unto the reading of their Life, directing on certain days that only the Octoechos be chanted. By an instruction unto such a reading alone the entire Typikon is exhausted here for certain days (neither stichera, nor kanon, nor troparion being mentioned), for instance October 13: "Holy Martyrs Carpus and Papylus. Their martyrdom is read"; October 20: "Holy Great-Martyr Artemius. His martyrdom is read" [^2578].

It is no wonder, therefore, that already in the 4th century collections of such "martyrdoms" appear, and later of Lives as well (Introductory Chapters, 174)—martyrologies (275ff.). When the number of saints multiplied, it became increasingly difficult to compile complete collections of their Lives or even to have at hand all their Lives; consequently, subsequent complete menologia (6th–8th c.) restrict themselves unto indicating the names of the saints, the place and time of their demise for their commemoration at the Liturgy (ibid., 320ff.), presuming, evidently, the reading of their Lives according unto availability. But from the 9th century onward attempts begin at collections of brief Lives of saints in several lines; such biographical notices are given among other things *(Orig. p. 692)* in the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople of the 9th century under each day of the month; these notices perhaps served as the basis for the *Menologion* [Gr. Μηνολόγιον] *of Emperor Basil* (11th c.), wherein the accounts are already more extensive; from the 11th century also there appear similar collections under the name of *Prologs*, the most ancient of which is the verse Prolog of Christopher of Mytilene (Introductory Chapters, 400–401).

The Slavic *Prolog* is close unto the later recensions of the Greek; consequently, it likewise has as its basis the aforementioned menologion of the Great Church, and especially that of Basil; it is more extensive than the latter (or makes use of a more expansive recension of it), but much briefer than the Lives of Symeon Metaphrastes and the *Chet'i-Minei* of St. Demetrius, omitting many data from the Lives, but also providing abundant details, in places important for the characterization of the saint and engagingly edifying. The Slavic *Prolog* preserved in manuscripts of the 12th–14th centuries had several printed editions, combining the verse Prolog with Basil's Prolog: 1st ed. 1641–1643, last 1792. Greek Synaxaria (likewise verse and non-verse) were printed and continue to be printed in the monthly Menaia (after the 6th ode of the kanon), the most complete being in the Menaia of the 1843 Venice edition (not only for the saints who have services, but also for others whose memory falls on that day); as a separate publication in

The Studite Typikon attached special significance unto the reading of the Lives of the saints: at weekday Matins this was the sole material for reading; long Lives were read in succession across several Matins services, for example the Life of Antony the Great on January 13–17, and of Theodore the Studite on November 8–11 [^2580]. ### The Eighth Ode

From among the other odes the Typikon singles out first of all, naturally, the 8th ode, as the concluding one among the Old Testament canticles, the closest unto the New Testament both in time of composition and in spirit. Its distinction from the others is effected by the fact that the Lesser Doxology concluding each ode (as also each psalm) is here formulated differently, specifically so that it may be close in manner of expression unto the text of the canticle, so that it may pour forth, as it were, from the lips of the confessing youths themselves. Since each verse of the canticle begins with the word "bless", instead of "Glory to the Father" the canticle hath at the end "Bless we the Father…" Moreover, the doxology in such an exceptional form, *(Orig. p. 693)* used nowhere else in divine worship, is placed not at the very end of the canticle, but before its last verse, whereby it is organically interwoven into the canticle. Thanks unto this, the last verse of the canticle, **"We praise, we bless, we worship the Lord…"** (*Khvalim, blagoslovim, poklanyayemsya Gospodevi*), acquires a new meaning, or more precisely, reveals its hidden meaning, that its three principal verbs can relate unto the three Persons of the Most Holy Trinity. This verse serves as the refrain unto the katavasia of the 8th ode, thanks unto which this katavasia likewise is singled out from among the others. Since the verse invites also unto worship, "always at the end of the 8th ode, when we wish to begin the 9th ode (consequently, as much on account of the impending 9th ode, which is even more important than the 8th), we all make a metania (a bow, on Sunday, of course, a small one, such as are further on explicitly required at the Canticle of the Theotokos) equally [all simultaneously], saying: **We praise, we bless, we worship the Lord**" [^2583].

### The Ninth Ode. Vesting for It

However distinguished from the series of the others the 8th ode may be, as the concluding word in the kanon from the Old Testament, yet by how much the New Testament is higher than the Old, by so much doth the Typikon exalt the 9th ode above the other odes. This is expressed first of all by the fact that for this ode "the priest and the deacon vest customarily," as they vest in the course of the Vigil only 2 other times: for the Entrance and for the Polyeleos or Gospel. In vestments they now remain until the conclusion of the service.

### "More Honorable than the Cherubim"

The Biblical canticles in the kanon, arranged approximately in chronological order, lead our thoughts ever closer unto New Testament times, whither the last, 9th ode definitively ushers us. Composed of the canticles of the Most Holy Theotokos and the Prophet Zechariah, it in its first part thus represents the greatest and holiest work in its author of the most exalted and inspired content (concerning the content, see above, p. 668). It is no wonder that the Typikon appoints for this canticle an exceptional execution and surrounds it with special reverence: whereas the other Biblical canticles serve as refrains unto the heirmoi and troparia of the kanon, the Canticle of the Theotokos occupies a completely independent position in relation unto the latter, and in general in the kanon, and possesses its own refrain, identical for all 6 verses into which it is divided for chanting. As such a refrain there serves the hymn **"More Honorable"** (*Čestnējšuyu*), in which with special expressiveness the Most Holy Virgin is confessed as the real, true (*ten ontos* [Gr. τὴν ὄντως]) Theotokos and with great boldness of faith is placed above the highest Angelic ranks. According unto tradition (in Nikephoros Kallistos, 14th c.), the Mother of God, appearing unto the composer of the hymn (St. Cosmas), with a joyful countenance said: "Pleasing unto Me are thy hymns, yet this one is more pleasing than all the others; pleasing unto Me are those who sing spiritual songs, yet never am I so near unto them as when they chant this new song of thine"; and those capable of mystical vision repeatedly saw thereafter the Mother of God blessing those who chanted this hymn [^2586].

### History

The Studite and Evergetis Typika do not indicate the manner of execution for the Canticle of the Theotokos, thereby giving reason to think that here there was no difference from the other Biblical canticles, that is, that the canticle was chanted as the others. The ancient manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon—Georgian and Greek—likewise say nothing concerning the manner of execution of the canticle in the order of Sunday Matins; only later Greek under September 8 note: "We do not chant the verses of the **More Honorable**" [^2587].

At Sunday Matins, concerning the "chanting of the verses of the **More Honorable**" for the first time
