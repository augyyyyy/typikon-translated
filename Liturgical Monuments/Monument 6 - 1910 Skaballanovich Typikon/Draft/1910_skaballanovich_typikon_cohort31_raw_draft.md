### Matins (Laudes) in Western Monasteries

Closely connected with the night office, although appearing as an independent office, was Matins (*matutinum*), called after its psalms **"Praise the Lord from the heavens"** and the following: *Laudes* or *ad Laudes* (= Praises / Lauds). According unto the Rule of St. Benedict, at Matins first of all there is recited (*dicatur*) Ps. 66 [LXX 66 / MT 67] without antiphons, slowly, so that all may arrive in time, and Ps. 40 [LXX 40 / MT 41], which is recited with antiphons, and on Sunday with Alleluia; then 2 psalms changing by the days of the week: on Monday Pss. 5 and 35 [LXX 35 / MT 36], on Tuesday Pss. 42 and 56 [LXX 56 / MT 57], on Wednesday Pss. 63 and 64 [LXX 64 / MT 65], on Thursday Pss. 87 and 89 [LXX 89 / MT 90], on Friday Pss. 75 and 81 [LXX 81 / MT 82], on Saturday Ps. 142 and the Canticle of Deuteronomy (cf. among us on Meatfare, Cheesefare, and Lazarus Saturdays), which is divided into two 'Glories'; on Sunday Ps. 117 [LXX 117 / MT 118] (cf. among us **"God is the Lord"**) and Ps. 62 [LXX 62 / MT 63]. Then a canticle from the Prophets proper unto each day, 'as the Roman Church sings' (see below), and on Sunday—*Benedictiones*, that is, the Canticle of the Three Holy Children and the *Laudes* (Praises psalms [Pss. 148–150]), a reading from the Apostle (on Sunday from the Apocalypse), a responsory from memory, an Ambrosian hymn (today: *Aeterne rerum conditor* ['Eternal Maker of all things']), a canticle from the Gospel (today the Canticle of Zechariah [*Benedictus*]; on Sunday, therefore, 5 biblical canticles: 3 at the vigil and 2 at Matins, the latter in the order of our canon), a litany, and the conclusion (chs. 12, 13). According unto Aurelian, on Matins: 'first a canticle (*canticum*) unto an antiphon, then in direct mode (*directaneum*) **"Judge me, O God"** (*"Sudi mi, Bozhe"* / Ps. 42 [LXX 42 / MT 43]); **"O God, my God, unto Thee I rise early"** (*"Bozhe, Bozhe moi, k Tebe utreniuio"* / Ps. 62 [LXX 62 / MT 63]); **"Praise the Lord, O my soul"** (*"Khvali, dushe moia, Gospoda"* / Ps. 145 [LXX 145 / MT 146]) *(Orig. p. 313)*; **"Praise ye the Lord, for a psalm is good"** (*"Khvalite Gospoda, iako blag psalom"* / Ps. 146 [LXX 146 / MT 147:1–11]); **"Praise the Lord, O Jerusalem"** (*"Pokhvali, Ierusalime, Gospoda"* / Ps. 147 [LXX 147 / MT 147:12–20]). Then: **"Praise the Lord from the heavens"** (*"Khvalite Gospoda s nebes"* / Ps. 148); **"Sing unto the Lord a new song"** (*"Vospoite Gospodevi pesn' novu"* / Ps. 149); **"Praise the Lord in His saints"** (*"Khvalite Gospoda vo sviatykh Ego"* / Ps. 150). ### The Hours

For the Hours the majority of abbots appoint 3 psalms, rarely 6 (Caesarius, *Rule for Virgins* § 69) and 12. The order of the office varies among different abbots, and does not resemble the present Roman order, but departs from the latter in the direction of the Eastern rites. According unto St. Benedict, the office of the Hour begins with the verse: **"O God, come to my assistance"** (*"Bozhe, v pomoshch' moiu vonmi"* / *Deus in adjutorium meum intende*), a hymn, 3 psalms separately (*sigillatim*) and not under one 'Glory', a reading, a verse, **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Kyrie eleison*), and the dismissal. On Sunday at the Hours are sections of Ps. 118 (chs. 17 and 18). According unto Aurelian, at each Hour for monks there are 12 psalms, and for nuns 6; after the psalms a hymn (not before the psalms as in Benedict and today, but after them, like our troparion), 2 readings from the Old and New Testaments, a verse (*versiculum*; cf. among us **"Order my steps"**, etc.). *(Orig. p. 314)* According unto Columbanus, 'at each Hour there are appointed by the elders 3 psalms with

=== LEAF p303 ===
the addition of intercalary verses: first for our sins, then for all the Christian people, for priests, for the other degrees of the clergy consecrated unto God (*plebis*), finally for those who give alms, for the peace of the churches, and in the end for enemies' (cf. among us at the end of the Midnight Office and Compline) (ch. 7).
According unto Isidore: 3 psalms, a responsory, readings from the Old and New Testaments, *Laudes* (?), a hymn, and a prayer (ch. 7). The 1st unknown author indicates at the Hours 'proper psalms, 2 antiphons, and 2 readings with their prayers.' Fructuosus and the 2nd unknown author speak only of the number of psalms: 3. According unto the Master, at each Hour there are 3 psalms, separate responsories, a reading of the Apostle, a reading of the Gospel by the abbot or provost, and *Rogus Dei* (ch. 35).

### Vespers

At Vespers (*vespera*, *lucernarium*) a varying quantity of psalms is appointed, but it is remarkable that some appoint for it alone, as among us, only one psalm; the general structure of the office for the most part resembles that of an Hour, at times completely coinciding with it. According unto St. Benedict: 4 psalms with antiphons, a reading with a responsory (cf. the Old Testament paremias), a hymn (cf. **"O Gladsome Light"**), a verse, a canticle from the Gospel (**"My soul doth magnify the Lord"** / *Magnificat*), a litany (cf. among us), the Lord's Prayer (cf. among us), and the dismissal; the psalms at Vespers are in course from 109 to 147 [LXX 109–147 / MT 110–147], with the exception of those which are employed at other offices (chs. 17, 18). According unto Aurelian, first a *directaneus* (one psalm chanted by the choir simply), then two antiphons, and a third with Alleluia, and a hymn. According unto the 1st unknown author: 1 psalm, 1 responsory, 3 antiphons, and 3 readings. In Columbanus Vespers apparently has the composition of a three-psalm Hour. According unto Isidore, first a *lucernarium* (an evening psalm?), 2 psalms, 1 responsory, *Laudes*, a hymn, and a prayer (ch. 7). In Fructuosus, in winter 6, and in summer 5 psalms with antiphons, the last with Alleluia, 1 responsory, a verse, a reading of the Apostle and the Gospel (the latter by the abbot, on Sunday with Alleluia), and *Rogus Dei* (chs. 36, 41).

### Compline

Compline (*completorium*) is not indicated by all authors. According unto St. Benedict: 3 psalms simply without antiphons, namely 4, 90 [LXX 90 / MT 91] (cf. among us), and 133 [LXX 133 / MT 134], a hymn, one reading, a verse, *Kyrie eleison*, the Blessing, and thereafter occur *missae* (night readings?) (chs. 17, 18). Aurelian gives two complines: the first under the name of the '12th Hour', consisting of 18 pss., an antiphon, a hymn, a reading, and a *capitellum*; the second under the name *completa*—consisting of a *directaneus*, Ps. 90, and 'the customary *caputella*'. Columbanus at the beginning of the night prescribes the chanting of 12 psalms. According unto Fructuosus, at the 1st hour of the night 6 prayers must be performed,

=== LEAF p304 ===
then 10 psalms in common singing, and in conclusion *Laus* and *Benedictiones*; then they mutually forgive and ask each other for reconciliation (ch. 2; cf. among us). According unto the Master, at Compline are recited 3 psalms, a responsory, a reading of the Apostle and the Gospel, the abbot recites *Rogus Dei* and the concluding verse: **"Set, O Lord, a watch before my mouth"** (*"Polozhi, Gospodi, khranenie ustom moim"* / Ps. 140:3; cf. among us **"Let Thy mercy, O Lord, be upon us..."**), followed by mutual reconciliation and forgiveness (ch. 30). At all the daily offices in monasteries, as in cathedral and parish churches, genuflection (*oratio*) was observed, during which prayers were uttered silently (*silentio*) and only the concluding exclamation of the officiant was made aloud. On Sundays, however, and throughout the entire Pentecostarion genuflection was abolished: they prayed standing upright or bowing down (*inclinati*) (cf. among us) [St. Benedict, chs. 50, 20; Master, ch. 48]. In the Master, moreover, genuflection is abolished also from the Nativity of Christ until the Theophany.

### The Roman Rite

The monastic divine worship described above, established in its principal features by St. Benedict and propagated by his disciples, by virtue of the high excellence of his Rule and the influence of the Benedictine popes (St. Gregory I the Great and Gregory II), formed the foundation of the divine worship of the entire Roman Church, supplanting from it the earlier cathedral and parochial worship. In the present Roman Book of Hours or Breviary (*Breviarium*), the division of the daily offices established by St. Benedict is retained, namely the sevenfold division (according unto Ps. 118:164: **"Seven times a day have I praised Thee"**): Matins with nocturns (*matutinum*), Lauds (*Laudes*), Prime, Terce, Sext, None [the 1st, 3rd, 6th, and 9th Hours], Vespers (*vesperae*), and Compline (*completorium*); after the model of the Benedictine Rule the entire Psalter is chanted in a week, being distributed among the offices thus: at nocturns 12 psalms on weekdays (from Pss. 20 to 108, divided across the 6 days of the week); at the daytime Hours—Ps. 118; at Vespers—psalms from 109 to 147 (with the exception of those employed at other offices); at Compline—Pss. 4, 30 (the first 6 verses), 90, and 133 (the second psalm was not used by Benedict). In its details, however, the present Roman order of daily offices received greater development than in the Rule of St. Benedict, for which reason we describe it as the highest point of development of Western worship in the period under consideration; all the more so since for this period it was ready in its essential features and identical in the Roman secular and monastic breviaries. *(Orig. p. 315)*
The offices customarily begin with Matins. Before it is appointed the customary opening: the Lord's Prayer (**"Our Father"**), the *Ave Maria* (**"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"**), and the Symbol of Faith (**"I believe"**), recited silently (introduced no earlier than the 13th century). Matins itself begins with the invitatory verses: **"O Lord, open Thou my lips"** (once) and **"O God, come to my assistance"** with **"Glory... Both now..."** and Alleluia (in Great Lent: 'Praise be to Thee, O Lord, King of eternal glory'). Then Ps. 94 with the refrain (*invitatorium*) and a morning hymn (differing for different days). After it follow the nocturns. They are of 3 kinds: Sunday, festal, and ferial [weekday]. On Sunday there are 3 nocturns. At the 1st nocturn

are sung 12 psalms with 3 antiphons divided into 3 sections of 4 psalms each; a psalm that doth not conclude with an antiphon has, as always, 'Glory' at the end. After the antiphon of the final psalm follows a verse, the Lord's Prayer, the 'absolution' (*absolutio*—a brief petition unto the Savior for the hearing of prayers and mercy with glorification of Him), the abbot's blessing, and 3 readings: from the Holy Scripture of the Old and New Testaments and the Doctors of the Church, with responsories between them, during which the brethren remain seated, rising only at the 'Glory' of the final responsory. At the 2nd nocturn—3 psalms (cf. the kathismata of our Matins), a verse, the Lord's Prayer, the *absolutio*, the blessing, and 3 readings from the lives of the saints or patristic writings with 3 responsories. At the 3rd nocturn—3 Old Testament canticles (the Canticle of Isaiah 33:2–10; 33:13–17; 35:1–10, or other similar canticles) with one antiphon, a verse, the Lord's Prayer, the *absolutio*, the blessing, 3 readings from the New Testament with interpretations of the holy fathers, and 3 responsories. Then the hymn **"We praise Thee, O God"** (*Te Deum laudamus* / *"Tebe Boga khvalim"*), at the singing of which all arise. On feasts there are likewise 3 nocturns, but at each of them there are not 4, but 3 psalms, and at the 3rd nocturn the readings are not from the New Testament, but from patristic homilies for the feast, and instead of the 3rd responsory the hymn **"We praise Thee, O God"** is sung. On weekdays there is one nocturn: 12 psalms are sung under 6 antiphons (2 psalms to an antiphon), a verse, the Lord's Prayer, the *absolutio*, the blessing, 3 readings from Holy Scripture with responsories, as at the 1st Sunday nocturn; the hymn *(Orig. p. 316)* **"We praise Thee, O God"** at weekday Matins is replaced by a responsory (as also on Sunday in Great Lent from the Sunday of Septuagesima). The tripartite division of Sunday nocturns symbolically points, according unto Bonaventure (Commentary on Ps. 76), unto natural, legal, and evangelical revelation, for which reason at the final nocturn the readings are exclusively from the New Testament. After Matins or the nocturns follows the office *ad Laudes* (Praises): after the verse **"O God, come to my assistance"**, the above-indicated psalms and the proper biblical canticle in course with antiphons, a *capitulum* (short reading) from the New Testament, a morning hymn with a responsory, the Canticle of Zechariah (*Benedictus*) with an antiphon, then the prayer proper unto the day (*propria*), the greeting—*salutatio* and *collecta*, general commemorations (*commemorationes*) of the saints, that is, the prayerful invocation of the Holy Virgin, righteous Joseph, the Apostles, the patron of the church, a supplication for peace and the repose of the departed (*commemorationes* are not always said); in conclusion the antiphon of the Holy Virgin. The 1st Hour begins with **"Our Father"**, the *Ave Maria*, the Creed, **"O God, come to my assistance"**; then a hymn, psalms with antiphons, on Sunday—the Athanasian Creed (*Quicunque vult*), a *capitulum* with a responsory, prayers—*preces* (*Kyrie eleison*, *Christe eleyson*, *Kyrie eleison*, **"Our Father"**, the Creed, and several penitential verses from the psalms; on great feasts these are omitted), confession (*confessio*)—of sins unto all the saints with the promise of forgiveness from God, concluding prayers with *Kyrie eleison* and other verses (collect), and the reading of the Martyrology. The 3rd, 6th, and 9th Hours each begin with **"Our Father"**, the *Ave Maria*, and **"O God, come to my assistance"**, followed by a hymn, 3 sections of Ps. 118, an antiphon, a *capitulum* with a responsory, and a collect. Vespers in its structure resembles *ad Laudes*; the customary opening is as at the Hours; 5 psalms with antiphons, a *capitulum*, a hymn with a responsory, the Canticle of the Theotokos (*Magnificat*) with an antiphon, and the prayer proper unto the day. Compline begins with a brief reading (1 Pet. 5:8) with a preceding blessing (a prayerful wish from God of a peaceful night and a perfect end) and a subsequent confession as at the 1st Hour, followed by the above-indicated psalms with antiphons, a hymn, a *capitulum* with a responsory, the Canticle of Simeon (*Nunc dimittis*) with an antiphon, prayers—*preces* with *Kyrie eleison*, resembling the prayers at the 1st

### Other Western Liturgical Rites

In place of the Roman rite, certain Roman Catholic churches and monasteries maintain their own liturgical rites, which are not inferior unto, and at times even surpass, the Roman in antiquity, having consequently been developed in the 6th–8th centuries. Such in particular are the Milanese (Ambrosian) rite, attributed unto St. Ambrose and used in the churches of Milan; the Mozarabic rite (*mozarabs* or *mixtarabs*—a mixture of Spaniards with Arabs), attributed unto SS. Leander and Isidore, Spanish bishops of the 7th century, and used in certain monasteries *(Orig. p. 317)* of Spain; and the Gallican rite, attributed unto St. Irenaeus of Lyons and used in certain churches of Lyons.

### The Ambrosian Rite

According unto the Ambrosian rite, the Psalter is chanted not once a week, but once in two weeks; from Ps. 109 onward it is sung, as in the Roman rite, at Vespers and the daytime Hours; up to Ps. 108, however, it is divided into 10 decuries, each of which comprises 16–8 psalms, and which are sung in turn at the weekday Matins of two weeks, with the exception of Saturday and Sunday; Matins of these latter days, as well as of feasts, has biblical canticles in place of the Psalter; likewise the weeks before the Nativity of Christ and Pascha have proper psalms at Matins; the Psalter is in a different translation than in the Roman rite—the one in which it is used also among the canons of St. Peter's in the Vatican. The customary opening of the offices never contains the Symbol of Faith, but only **"Our Father"** and the *Ave Maria*. At Matins, before the psalms, in the introductory part there is also the Canticle of the Three Holy Children: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers"** (*"Blagosloven esi, Gospodi Bozhe otets nashikh"*) and *Kyrie eleison*. After the psalms, which are sung continuously, at Matins there are only 3 readings: on Sunday from homilies upon the Gospel, on weekdays from the Scripture, and on feasts from the life of the saint or festal discourses; only at the Nativity of Christ and Theophany are there three nocturns and 9 readings, and on Friday 6 readings, the last 3 of which are the Passions according unto Mark, Luke, and John, with Matthew at the Liturgy. At *Laudes*, after the opening verse **"O God, come to my assistance"**—the Canticle of Zechariah (*Benedictus*), in place of which on the Sundays of Advent, the Nativity of Christ, Circumcision, and Theophany, there is sung the Canticle of Moses: **"Give ear, O ye heavens"** (*"Slyshi, nebo"* / Deut. 32); then on Sundays and feasts of saints an antiphon unto the Holy Cross 5 or 7 times and a secret prayer; the Canticle of Moses **"Let us sing unto the Lord"** (*Cantemus Domino* / *"Poim Gospodevi"*) with an antiphon and *Kyrie eleison* thrice, and another secret prayer; the Canticle of the Three Children **"Bless the Lord, all ye works"** (*Benedicite omnia opera* / *"Blagoslovite, vsia dela"*), in place of which on Saturday is sung Ps. 117, and on weekdays Ps. 50 with an antiphon and *Kyrie eleison* thrice; a prayer aloud; the Praises psalms (Lauds), a *capitulum*, an antiphon, *Kyrie eleison* thrice,

=== LEAF p307 ===
*Dominus vobiscum*; a psalm simply by two choirs together (*directus*) and a hymn according unto the day; *Kyrie eleison* 12 with *Dominus vobiscum*, a *psallenda* (an antiphon repeated with 'Glory' and several verses), and on weekdays—a responsory called *in baptisterio* (in the baptistery; cf. below in the Greek 'Choral/Sung Vespers and Matins'), a *commemoratio* of the saint with an antiphon, *Kyrie eleison* 12, a verse and a prayer aloud, *Kyrie eleison* thrice, and *Dominus vobiscum* with the dismissal (*procedamus in pace*—'let us go forth in peace'). The Hours have 3 psalms each without antiphons; at the 1st Hour: Ps. 53 [LXX 53 / MT 54] and 2 divisions of Ps. 118; at the 3rd, 6th, and 9th Hours: 3 divisions of Ps. 118; a hymn, a *capitulum*, a responsory, *Kyrie eleison* thrice, a prayer, and the dismissal. Vespers has: *Lucernarium* (a verse with a responsory), a hymn, then: on feasts *Responsorium in choro*, on weekdays and lesser feasts—an antiphon and 4 psalms, and on great feasts an antiphon and 2 psalms; thereafter on feasts *Notitia* (a proper commemoration), *Psallenda* or *Responsorium in baptisterio* (on weekdays), *commemorationes* (with an antiphon and *Kyrie eleison* 12), *Kyrie eleison* thrice, a prayer, and the dismissal. Compline: **"Our Father"**, the *Ave Maria*, **"O God, come to my assistance"**, a hymn, Pss. 4, 30, 90, 132, and 133 without antiphons, a *capitulum*, the Canticle of Simeon (**"Now lettest Thou Thy servant depart"** / *Nunc dimittis*), *Kyrie eleison* thrice, an antiphon unto the Theotokos, a prayer, and the dismissal [^1542]. Remarkable is the conjunction on feast days of Vespers with Matins or the Liturgy.

### The Mozarabic Rite

The Mozarabic rite has no continuous chanting of the Psalter in course; the psalms are proper unto the days. At Matins (in Spanish *matutinum*, elsewhere *nocturni*) the customary opening: *Kyrie eleison*, *Christe eleyson*, *Kyrie eleison*; **"Our Father"**; *Dominus sit semper vobiscum* ('The Lord be always with you'); a verse proper unto the feast, an antiphon from a psalm with a responsory (*responsus*), a canticle from the Prophets or New Testament with an antiphon and Alleluia (in the Forty Days [Great Lent] without it), another antiphon with Alleluia, a responsory, and a verse, the Blessing (*Benedictio*—the Canticle of the Three Holy Children), the 'sono' (*sonus*—a solemn chant), the Praises psalms (*Laudes*—Pss. 148–150), *capitula* (brief prayerful invocations), a hymn, and petitions (*preces*), resembling our litany-like petitions. *(Orig. p. 318)*
The Hours have a hymn, 3 psalms with antiphons, a responsory, *capitula*, *preces*, **"Our Father"**, and a dismissal prayer. At Vespers (*vesperum*), after the bringing in of the light, the deacon or cleric says in a low voice: '*Kyrie eleyson, Christe eleyson, Kyrie eleyson*. **Our Father**'; the priest: 'The Lord be always with you', the clergy: 'And with thy spirit'; then loudly and drawn out: 'In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, light with peace', and the response: 'Thanks be to God'. At the conclusion

=== LEAF p308 ===
of the offices—**"Our Father"** with the response 'Amen' unto each petition, and unto the 4th petition concerning bread—'For Thou art God'; then a prayer for the diverse needs of the faithful (longer at *Laudes* and Vespers than at the remaining offices); the deacon: 'Bow down for the blessing' (*Humiliate vos ad benedictionem*); the priest—three petitions with the response 'Amen' unto each, and the verse: 'In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ let us go forth in peace' (*In nomine Domini Jesu Christi procedamus in pace*), response: 'Thanks be to God' (*Deo gratias*) [^1543]. In general, Mozarabic divine worship is distinguished by great originality, a wealth of hymns and poetic compositions of Christian authorship, and an abundance of ceremonial actions which the Roman rite doth not possess, while some of them recall Eastern practices. Thus at the daily offices, besides the bringing in of the light: a) the chanting of an 'Alleluiarium' before the readings (cf. among us the Alleluia before the Gospel); b) the washing of hands by the priest before the blessing at the end of the office; the priest washes his hands with the words: 'With a pure heart and pure body may we be accounted worthy to offer unto Thee, O Lord, this blessing'; c) the *laus*, during the singing of which the church is censed, sung with a verse in memory of the saint at nocturns and Vespers, very strongly recall the Eastern choral office of three-antiphon structure.

### The Gallican or Lyonese Rite

The Gallican or Lyonese rite (*Lugdunensis*) follows the Roman in the use of the Psalter, with minor departures at Prime and *Laudes*; the most important differences from the Roman: the non-use of hymns except at Compline, the customary opening consisting of the Lord's Prayer alone (also at Compline), and at Matins on weekdays 1 nocturn with 3 readings. *(Orig. p. 319)*

### The Church Year

The condition of the Church Year in the 6th–8th centuries is sufficiently characterized by: a) the menologia and martyrologies that have come down unto us from this era; b) the activity of ecclesiastical hymnographers.
 
#### Martyrologies: a) The Pseudo-Hieronymian

The most ancient of such martyrologies is the so-called Hieronymian Martyrology (*Martyrologium Hieronymianum*), attributed unto Blessed Jerome, preserved in 8th-century manuscripts, and dating back no later than unto the second half of the 7th century [^1545]. The martyrology received its definitive redaction in Gaul and, in all likelihood, in Auxerre (this town is named 30 times, and all its bishops are included with the exception of one, whereas those of Lyons, for example, are far from all included, etc.). The martyrology contains within itself 6,000 names [^1546]. The majority of days have more than 10 commemorations, frequently 30–40, sometimes more than *(Orig. p. 321)* 1,000 commemorations, and once (June 2) 240; yet there are also days with only 3–4 names (Feb. 3, Oct. 26). The martyrology manifestly strives, in contrast unto the most ancient calendars, to embrace the entire universal Church, whereas sacramentaries even of the 9th–15th centuries intended for Gaul and Germany contain for the most part only Roman saints; in this regard all subsequent

Western martyrologies follow the Hieronymian Martyrology (those of Ado, Usuard, Notker, and Rabanus Maurus). The commemorations of Eastern saints are in the same scope as in the 4th-century Syriac martyrology, and either the author utilized it or they shared a common primary source; the Roman martyrs, apparently, are taken from the *Depositio martyrum* (see above, p. 307); of Roman popes there are 4 more than in Philocalus (see *ibid.*), beginning with Cornelius (251) and ending with Leo I († 461); for some of them the day of consecration is also indicated; the author also used the Carthaginian calendar; there are many Gallic saints; beside the names the hierarchical rank is frequently indicated; the city is indicated for the most part, sometimes only the country; very often the designation: 'in Africa' (without specifying a city); sometimes—*alibi* ('elsewhere'). Of Old Testament saints: Haggai—Jan. 4, Habakkuk—Jan. 15, Job—May 9, the Three Holy Children—Apr. 24, Aaron—July 1, Elisha—Aug. 29, the Sacrifice of Isaac—March 25 (likewise the day of Christ's death), and the exit of Noah from the Ark—April 28. There are Symeon the God-Receiver on January 6, Martha and Mary on Jan. 7, the Conception of John the Baptist on Sept. 24, and his Nativity and Repose as among us. Of the Apostles, only Matthew is missing; James, the Brother of the Lord, on March 25, because he was slain at Pascha; on Jan. 25 'the Translation of Paul' (today his 'Conversion'). Of the venerable fathers: Anthony the Great on Jan. 17, Symeon the Stylite on Jan. 5. All the Twelve Great Feasts are present, except the Transfiguration and the Entry of the Theotokos; furthermore—the Departure of Jesus from Egypt on Jan. 7, and the Beginning of the Lord's Preaching on May 1.
 
#### b) The Lesser Roman and c) Silos Martyrologies

The further development of the martyrology in the West proceeded (in contrast unto the East) by way of its abbreviation; the reason was the absence of all information concerning the majority of the saints in the Hieronymian Martyrology, which excited doubt as unto their existence, and furthermore—its inconvenience for liturgical use. Thus considerably briefer than the Hieronymian Martyrology is the so-called Lesser Roman Martyrology (*Martyrologium Romanum Parvum*), first cited by Ado in the 9th century (see below) and attributed by him unto an unknown Roman pope. Judging by the fact that it shows the Feast of All Saints on November 1, but still as a local Roman feast—a feast established by Pope Gregory III (731–741)—the martyrology is no earlier than this period. It exhibits, on the one hand, traits of greater antiquity in comparison with the Hieronymian, and on the other—marks of significant development. Thus the majority of names do not possess the title 'St.' or 'Bl.'; 7 days have no commemorations; daily there are 2–3 commemorations; vigils occur only for the Nativity of Christ and the Martyr Laurence; there are Apostles of the Seventy *(Orig. p. 322)* and Apostolic Fathers; more Old Testament saints; and the Exaltation of the Holy Cross on Sept. 14 is present [^1547]. This must be a very ancient local martyrology (Spanish? on account of the vigil for Laurence), later supplemented in places.
