...in correspondence with **"saith the Lord."**

### Their History (*Istoriya ikh*)

In the Holy Sepulchre *Order* (*Akolouthia*) of the 9th–12th centuries, from among the present prokeimena under the title *epakouston* (Gr. ἐπακουστόν; while the vesperal and liturgical prokeimena are given under the name 'prokeimenon'), there are assigned Tones 3 and 5 (or 7) at Paschal Matins, Tone 8 at Matins of Bright Tuesday, Tone 4 on Wednesday, and Tone 7 on Thursday [^2463].

In the earliest manuscript copy of the Studite-Alexis Typikon of the 11th–12th centuries, there is a section: "Sunday Matins prokeimena before 'Let every breath,'" wherein the present prokeimena are cited, ending with Tone 5 (the section is unfinished, as are others in this manuscript [^2464]).

# THE GOSPEL (*Evangelie*)

### "For Holy Art Thou" (*Yako Svyat*)

For the hearing of the Gospel our Typikon prepares not only through chants, but also through prayers. The second mode of preparation is more essential than the first, and therefore follows after it. The prayerful preparation, like the chant preparation (the prokeimenon and "Let every breath"), has two stages, and at this first stage it is limited unto the invitation **"Let us pray to the Lord"** (*Gospodu pomolimsya*), the response **"Lord, have mercy"** (*Gospodi pomiluy*) (an as yet indefinite prayer), and the exclamation indirectly indicating our need for sanctification and to this end glorifying God Who is Holy in His saints: **"For holy art Thou, O our God, and Thou restest in the saints, and unto Thee do we send up glory, to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit..."** (*Yako Svyat esi Bozhe nash...*).

### Origin (*Vozniknovenie*)

Without doubt, here is a remnant of an earlier prayer for the worthy hearing of the Gospel; in a certain Georgian Euchologion this prayer is appointed precisely before **"Let every breath"**; it begins like our present one (**"Shine in our hearts"** [*Vozsiyay v serdtsakh nashikh*]), but has the exclamation: **"For Thou art the illumination and the sanctification..."** [^2465]. There is such a prayer also in other manuscript Sluzhebniks at this place.
*(Orig. p. 655)*

### "Let Every Breath" (*Vsyakoe dykhanie*)

Now follows the second and definitive stage of the hymnographic and prayerful preparation for the Gospel. The hymnographic part of this preparation consists in the singing, according unto the order of a prokeimenon (with a canonarch), of the verse **"Let every breath praise the Lord"** (*Vsyakoe dykhanie da khvalit Gospoda*), the final (6th) verse of the final (150th) Psalm, with its 1st verse: **"Praise God in His saints..."** (*Khvalite Boga vo svyatykh Ego...*). Serving as just as direct a preparation for the Gospel as the liturgical Alleluiarion (the prokeimenon at the Divine Liturgy serves as preparation for the Epistle, for which reason at Matins likewise it cannot be the immediate preparation for the Gospel), this verse summons all living beings unto the glorification of the Lord—a glorification that became possible and was realized only after the proclamation of the Gospel unto "every creature" (cf. the 1st Matins Gospel; cf. the verse unto the prokeimenon for the Apostles: **"The heavens declare the glory of God"** and

=== LEAF p793 ===
...the present verse: **"in the firmament of His power"**). Being the final verse of the Psalter, this verse worthily concludes the present 'psalter' division of Matins.

### History (*Istoriya*)

Historically, the precedence of this verse before the Gospel is explained by the fact that formerly the Gospel at Matins (the sung Asmatic Office) was read after the Praises (Lauds) psalms and the Great Doxology.

According unto the Studite Typikon, **"Let every breath"** was sung in the tone of the preceding prokeimenon [^2466].

The Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon are silent; Slavic manuscripts of the 14th–15th centuries specify:

"After the Amen the chanter begins **'Let every breath'** in the current tone, and we sing the same in the same tone. The deacon censes only the altar. Then the chanter the verse: **'Praise God in His saints.'** And we: **'Let every breath.'** Then the chanter: **'Let every breath,'** and we: **'praise the Lord'**"; later ones: "**'Let every breath'** in the same tone."

The Old Believer: "The canonarch: **'Let every breath'** in Tone 6" [^2467].

### "And That We May Be Accounted Worthy" (*I o spodobitisya*)

The prayerful part of the present preparation for the Gospel consists in the invitation from the deacon: **"And that we may be accounted worthy to hear the holy Gospel, let us pray to the Lord"** (*I o spodobitisya nam k slyshaniyu svyatago Evangelia, Gospodu pomolimsya*) (cf. the indefinite invitation at the preceding stage) and the threefold (cf. the single one at the preceding stage) **"Lord, have mercy."** At the Divine Liturgy there is no such special supplication, because the Gospel is read with less solemnity, frequently pertains less unto the commemorated event, and is prepared for by the reading of the Epistle.

### Origin (*Vozniknovenie*)

Already the opening "And" in this exclamation of the deacon indicates that this is a fragment of a formerly more extensive supplication. Indeed, Georgian and ancient Greek manuscripts state: "The deacon (Greek: the priest) says the Little Ektenia, then proclaims: **'And that we may be accounted worthy,'**" which later Greek sources omit; but Slavic manuscripts of the 14th–15th centuries add: "Again *(Orig. p. 656)* and again; the priest the exclamation: **'For unto Thee is proper,'** deacon: **'And that we may be accounted worthy to hear worthily the holy Gospel, in peace to the Lord God let us pray'**"

=== LEAF p794 ===
...let us pray." An exclamation of the same recension is found also in Old Believer editions, but with "worthily" before "to hear," and "the Lord God" (there is no ektenia) [^2468].

### Pre-Gospel Exclamations (*Predugotovitel'nye k Evangeliyu vozglasy*)

Having implored of God help for the worthy hearing of the Gospel through so fervent and prolonged a prayer, the faithful are invited on their own part likewise to take measures toward such hearing. An invitation unto this is made to them several times, by the priest and the deacon alternately. It begins with the warning of the deacon concerning the "wisdom" hidden within the Gospel, which requires most directly a special posture of the body (**"Wisdom! Stand upright, let us hear the holy Gospel"** [*Premudrost', prosti, uslyshim svyatago Evangelia*], the genitive case, according to the Greek, governed by "let us hear"—*orthoi* [Gr. ὀρθοί], *prosti*—"upright," "erect") for the hearing thereof (most immediately *prosti* requires standing upright during the Gospel in contrast unto other readings), and then also a special disposition of soul—peace, concerning which the priest not only reminds them, but also graciously imparts it (**"Peace be unto all"** [*Mir vsem*]), while the faithful (in prayer) desire the same for him (**"And to thy spirit"**). Now the heading unto the Gospel reading is proclaimed, which is uttered as an inseparable part of the Gospel always by the one who reads the latter himself—consequently, at Matins by the priest—in the form: **"The reading from the Holy Gospel according to N"** (Gr. ἐκ τοῦ κατὰ with accusative, literally: "From the Gospel according unto N, the reading"), unto which the choir responds with a joyful glorification of the Lord: **"Glory to Thee, O Lord, glory to Thee"** (*Slava Tebe, Gospodi, slava Tebe*) (this verse is absent from the Greek Euchologion), repeated also after the reading of the Gospel. Just before the reading of the Gospel itself, the faithful are summoned unto attention by the brief **"Let us attend"** (*Vonmem*), which is pronounced by the sacred minister who is not reading the Gospel, in order to separate this exclamation from the holy words of the Gospel.

### Their History (*Istoriya ikh*)

Originally, the preparation for the Gospel consisted, presumably, only of a prayer, similar unto the present **"Shine in our hearts"**; only such a prayer is contained in the earliest manuscripts of all liturgies. But very early the Gospel began to be preceded also by exclamations similar unto the present ones; their antiquity is proved by the agreement in this regard among various recensions of liturgies, even Western ones. Thus, the Alexandrian Liturgy attributed unto the Evangelist Mark, in an 11th-century manuscript, contains such exclamations:

=== LEAF p795 ===
— "Deacon: Bless, Master. Priest: The Lord bless and strengthen [us], and make us hearers of His holy Gospel, Who is blessed God now and ever... Deacon: Stand upright (*stathete*, Gr. σταθῆτε). Let us hear the holy Gospel. Priest: Peace be unto all. People: And to thy spirit" [^2469].

In the Roman Liturgy (Mass), after the prayer *Munda cor meum* ("Cleanse my heart"), the celebrant says:

"Command, Lord, a blessing" (*Jube Domne benedicere*), "The Lord be in my heart," "The Lord be with you" (*Dominus vobiscum*), "And with thy spirit" (*Et cum spiritu tuo*), "The continuation (*Sequentia*)" or "The beginning of the holy Gospel according to (*secundum*) N," "Glory be to Thee, O Lord" (*Gloria tibi Domine*); after *(Orig. p. 657)* the Gospel: "Praise be to Thee, O Christ" (*Laus tibi Christe*), and the prayer "By the words of the Gospel" (*Per evangelica dicta*).

At Matins before the Gospel there is the verse:

"Command, master (*domne*), a blessing. Blessing: May the reading of the Gospel be our salvation and protection (*Evangelica lectio sit nobis salus et protectio*). Response: Amen. The reading of the holy Gospel according to N" [^2470].

In the Milanese (Ambrosian) Liturgy, it is as in the Roman, but before the Gospel there is in addition **"Lord, have mercy"** thrice [^2471].

In the Mozarabic Liturgy:

The celebrant: "The Lord be always with you," the choir: "And with thy spirit," "The reading of the holy Gospel according to N," "Glory be to Thee, O Lord"; after the Gospel: "Amen," "The Lord be...", "And with...", "Praise. Alleluia" [^2472].

In the Liturgy of the Apostle James according unto a 10th-century manuscript:

"Stand upright, let us hear the holy Gospel. Peace be unto all. From N. Let us attend." In the 11th century: "Stand upright, let us hear... Peace be unto all. Let us attend unto the holy reading"; after the Gospel: "Peace be unto thee. Glory to Thee, O Lord." In the 14th century: "Let us attend unto the holy reading. Peace be unto all. Stand upright, let us hear the holy Gospel"; after the Gospel: as in the 11th century [^2473].

In manuscripts of the Liturgies of Basil and Chrysostom prior unto the 16th century, for the most part only a prayer is indicated; from the 16th century, it is as at present,

=== LEAF p796 ===
...yet seldom **"Glory to Thee, O Lord, glory to Thee"** before the Gospel, and still more seldom after it [^2474].

### Typikon for the Reading of the Matins Gospel (*Ustav chteniya utrennego Evangelia*)

The Gospel at Matins is read not by the deacon, as at the Divine Liturgy, but by the priest, in view of the fact that he should "first nourish with the divine word those whom at the Divine Liturgy he will nourish with the mystical Bread," as Christ also did, and as He commanded the Apostles to do ("Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them..."—Matt. 28:19) [^2475]. The priest at the Divine Liturgy has higher functions than the reading even of the Gospel. Moreover, at Sunday *(Orig. p. 658)* Matins the Gospel is more important than the liturgical one, because it directly relates unto the event of the Resurrection (in such a relationship stand the Matins and Liturgical Gospels also of certain other feasts, for example the Nativity of Christ; cf. Pascha). In view of this, the Matins Gospel is read in the altar upon the Holy Table, whereas the liturgical one is read in the center of the temple upon an analogion. This is especially fitting for Sunday Matins, because the Holy Table symbolizes the Tomb of the Savior.

### Historical Note (*Istoricheskaya spravka*)

The Gospel at Sunday Matins in the order of the Asmatic Office as well, at least according unto Symeon of Thessalonica and manuscripts of the 14th–15th centuries, was read by the priest, but upon the ambo; in ancient Western monasteries—by the abbot (Introductory Chapter, pp. 347, 310). According unto certain 16th-century manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon, it was read in the midst of the temple [^2476].

### The Sunday Matins Gospels (*Voskresnye utrennie Evangelia*)

The narrative of all four Evangelists concerning the Resurrection of the Savior is divided for reading at Sunday Matins into 11 pericopes (*zachalos*), presumably because into such a number of parts this narrative naturally falls; perhaps the "eleven" Apostles were kept in view as the closest witnesses of the Resurrection. From the series of Gospels there is omitted only the account of the Evangelist Matthew that most circumstantially describes the very moment of the Resurrection, and is therefore reserved for Pascha (for the Liturgy of its Eve [Holy Saturday]). Thus, all the Gospel readings speak rather of the appearances of the risen Savior than of the Resurrection itself. The cycle of Sunday Gospels, called the 'pillar' (*stolp*), is repeated approximately six times in the course of

=== LEAF p797 ===
...the year: the 1st pillar begins on the Sunday of All Saints, the 2nd after the Feast of Elijah, the 3rd after the Exaltation [of the Holy Cross], the 4th during the Nativity Fast, the 5th after Theophany, and the 6th during the Great Fast (Lent). Tables of these pillars, indicating with which tone each Gospel coincides, are placed in the Liturgical Gospel Book, the Octoechos, and Chapter 54 of the Typikon. For the Sundays of the Pentecostarion (Flowery Triodion), the Matins Gospels follow in a special order.

### History (*Istoriya*)

The Sunday Matins Gospels in their present composition are already contained in manuscripts of the Liturgical Gospel of the 8th century, for example the Greek manuscript of the Moscow Synodal Library No. 42, XLIII, 11, fols. 241–246 [^2477]. Mention of the rotation of the 11 Sunday Gospels exists in the Studite Typikon of the 11th century, the Evergetis, and in Georgian and Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon [^2478]. At Roman Sunday Matins there is a Gospel not relating unto the Resurrection. According unto the Typikon of the Great Church in the Asmatic Office, the Gospel at Sunday Matins was read after the Great Doxology (Introductory Chapter, p. 347), but according unto the Studite *(Orig. p. 659)* and others—at its present place; in the earliest recensions of the Studite Typikon, however, perhaps after Ode 6 of the Canon [^2479]. According unto the present Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople—after Ode 8 of the Canon [^2480].

# FROM THE GOSPEL UNTO THE CANON (*Ot Evangelia do kanona*)

### "Having Beheld the Resurrection of Christ" (*Voskresenie Khristovo*)

With the reading of the Gospel, the festal joy of the worshippers reaches its zenith and pours forth no longer in merely Sunday hymns, but in purely Paschal chants. After the Gospel there are appointed at Sunday Matins two hymnographic compositions from the service of Pascha: **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** (*Voskresenie Khristovo videvshe*) and **"Jesus having risen from the grave"** (*Voskres Iisus ot groba*). True, of the Paschal hymns these are the most restrained in joy: they are of Tone 6, a tone which in its troparic and canonic melodies is the most mournful, and in its sticheraric melody, in which these present hymns are sung, is a tone of quiet joy, barely liberated from beneath grief.

In content as well, the hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ,"** beginning with words highly appropriate unto the moment (during the reading of the Gospel the faithful have spiritually beheld the Risen One with the Apostles), dwells rather upon the counterpart of the Resurrection—the Crucifixion. The hymn does not so much exult over the Resurrection (cf. the Paschal Canon and **"A Pascha sacred and worthy of all honour"**) as it meditates in concentrated fashion upon the significance of the event for Christ (it revealed His Divinity and sinlessness) and for us (it brought joy unto the world, destroying death), being thus of dogmatic content, and invites unto the glorification of the event (having in view the long series of subsequent hymns in its honour: the Canon). At the same time, this is the largest of the Paschal hymns and the most complete and comprehensive, for which reason it replaces throughout Bright Week all introductory psalms. It consists of seven strophes, bound together not so much grammatically as by unity of thought; each of these strophes could be (and perhaps was) an independent chant (cf. **"O Gladsome Radiance,"** above, p. 558), just as one of the strophes serves as such an independent chant (**"Thy Cross do we adore, O Master..."** [*Krestu Tvoemu poklanyaemsya...*]).

### Ancient Use (*Drevnee upotreblenie*)

Of the antiquity of the hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** testifies the presence of a similar text in the Latin Church at the Liturgy of Great (Good) Friday: **"Thy Cross do we adore, O Lord, and Thy holy Resurrection do we praise and glorify: for behold, by the wood of the Cross joy is come into the whole world"** (*Crucem tuam adoramus, Domine, et sanctam resurrectionem tuam laudamus et glorificamus...*) [^2481]. The hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** is mentioned in the well-known 9th-century Jerusalem Paschal *Order* (*Akolouthia*) not only as a Paschal chant, but also as an ordinary

=== LEAF p799 ===
...Sunday *(Orig. p. 660)* chant after the Gospel: on Palm Sunday there is appointed here, in place of **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ,"** a special parodic (that is, patterned upon it) hymn [^2482]. — The hymn **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** bears a distinctly Jerusalem coloration, having in view, presumably, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre (the *Anastasis*—**"let us worship the holy Resurrection of Christ"**) and the Cross behind it (the frequent mention of the Cross). Yet even the earliest manuscripts of the Studite Typikon contain it at Sunday Matins, although immediately after the Gospel there was here a prokeimenon: **"In the nights lift up your hands"** with the verse: **"The Lord bless thee out of Zion"** [^2483] (Psalm 133:2, 3 [LXX; Masoretic 134:2, 3]).

— Under Arseny Sukhanov in the East, this hymn "they recited divided between both choirs line by line," as also Psalm 50 with its concluding refrains (**"Glory... Both now... Through the prayers of the Apostles... Have mercy on me, O God... Jesus having risen from the grave..."**), making six such strophes in the hymn [^2484]. Did not its strophes originally serve as troparia unto the verses of Psalm 50? According unto the present Greek Euchologion, **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** is "recited by the superior or appointed monk"; according unto the Hieratikon—by the hierarch or by a priest standing in the choir [^2485].

### Psalm 50 (*50-y psalom*)

The Psalter portion of Matins is already drawing toward its very end, and it requires here the most powerful and at the same time most expressive chord, most naturally in the form of one of the psalms with which this entire central part of the most important of the daily services is filled. Hardly could a more felicitous choice have been made, therefore, than placing Psalm 50 [LXX; Masoretic 51, *Miserere*] here. No psalm was written under such exceptional and harrowing circumstances as this; none answers more than this unto the fundamental disposition of a Christian—repentance (**"Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand"**) [^2486]—nor expresses it with such power. And the contrast in which it stands unto the preceding prolonged jubilation further heightens this impression: it is, as it were, a skull brought into a festive banquet.

### Manner of Execution (*Sposob ispolneniya*)

In the manner of executing Psalm 50 at Matins, the Typikon, apparently, makes no distinction from **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"**: "Thereafter (after the Gospel) we say **'Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ'** and Psalm 50." Moreover, this hymn, as well as the refrains **"Glory... Through the prayers of the Apostles..."** and so forth, as well as the hymn **"Jesus having risen from the grave,"** having as its refrain the 1st verse of the psalm, stand in such close connection with it that

=== LEAF p800 ===
...it is most natural to chant it as well, and of course, in the tone of these hymns surrounding it, that is, Tone 6, the tone of grief and repentance. The chanting, as for other chanted psalms, ought to be antiphonal, by verses between the two choirs.
*(Orig. p. 661)*

### Its Constant Use at Matins (*Vsegdashnee upotreblenie ego na utrene*)

Already under St. Basil the Great, Psalm 50 constituted an essential, concluding part of Matins (Introductory Chapter, p. 148). In the earliest recensions of the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, this is the most important, and likewise the second, part of Matins (ibid., p. 340). The earliest manuscript copies of the Studite Typikon (the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis*) did not cancel it even for Paschal Matins. Almost all non-Orthodox morning offices also possess it: the Abyssinian, Maronite, Nestorian, and ancient Latin; the Armenian and the present Latin have it only on weekdays (the latter at Lauds—ibid., pp. 299–319), but at the First Hour there is always a series of verses from this psalm [^2487].

According unto the Typikon of the Great Church of Constantinople, it was sung at Matins with the refrain of a troparion unto the verses—either one or several (ibid., p. 340).

Greek manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon express themselves concerning Psalm 50: "it is said"; certain Slavic manuscripts of the 14th century concerning **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** and **"Glory..."** specify "it is recited," "in Tone 6" (concerning the second), concerning Psalm 50 are silent, and concerning **"Jesus having risen"**—"we chant" [^2488].

According unto the *Diataxis* (*Order*) of Patriarch Philotheus: "Psalm 50 with singing" [^2489].

Under Arseny Sukhanov in the East, the psalm, like **"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ,"** "they recite divided between both choirs line by line," making 18 such lines in the psalm (the verses in it being 19), while on weekdays "the psalm-reader recites it" [^2490].

According unto the present Greek Euchologion, the brethren chant it melodically; according unto the Hieratikon—the choirs chant it [^2491].

In the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra it is read.

### Refrains unto Psalm 50 (*Pripevy k 50-mu psalmu*)

The mournful character of this psalm for Sunday Matins is sufficiently softened by the Sunday, and indeed directly Paschal, hymns surrounding it (**"Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ"** and **"Jesus having risen from the grave"**), which according unto the concept ought to serve as refrains unto each of its verses. At the same time, it too, bursting with its penitential tone into the most luminous part of the Vigil, tempers its joy. In order to soften the transition from the grief of this psalm unto the joy of the hymn that immediately follows, **"Jesus having risen from the grave,"** which returns this cycle of hymns unto their beginning
