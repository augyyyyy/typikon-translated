The 'Order' (*Taxis*) of the Athos-St. Andrew 15th-century manuscript and the musical manuscript *Papadike Techne* (*Παπαδικὴ τέχνη* [printed *Παλαδικὴ*]) of the 14th century of the Vatopedi Monastery Library No. 297 (931) depict in greater detail the Entrance, the singing of the Doxology, and the reading of the Gospel. The cantors chant **"Most blessed art thou"** only up unto the words: **"Blessed is Christ our God"**. At the Entrance the domestikos pronounces in an elevated voice these words of the Theotokion (musical MS); at the Entrance between the preceding candlebearers walks the reader in a (short) phelonion with the large cross (aforementioned); upon the entrance of the priests into the altar, he ascends two steps of the ambo and here pronounces in chant the beginning of the Doxology: **"Glory to God in the highest"** ('Order'). 'And the domestikos begins the sweet-sounding hymn (*ᾀσματικὸν ὕμνον*), that is, the Doxology, while the priests pronounce the remainder. (Following the Doxology) the domestikos: **"Having risen from the tomb and burst the bonds…"**, and the priests say the remainder. Then the domestikos: **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son…"**, and again the priests: **"Having risen from the tomb"**; the domestikos with a high voice: **"Having risen. A Psalm of David"**, and immediately the reader says: **"Arise, O Lord"** (musical MS). According unto the 'Order', the reader, following the Great Doxology, 'chants melodically: **"A Psalm of David"** and the remaining verses. The reader departs, and the domestikos or laosynaktes (*λαοσυνάκτης*) ascends unto the 3rd step of the ambo and chants the prokeimenon with the remaining readers; the domestikos likewise chants the musical intonation (*ἤχημα* / *ἤχισμα*) at the endings'. The verses unto the prokeimenon are indicated by the musical manuscript: **"I will confess Thee, O Lord…"**; **"I will be glad…"**; and further the rubric: *Θεσσαλονικαῖον* (according unto the Thessalonian order?): **"O Lord my God, let Thy hand be lifted up"**; *ἡ μεγάλη δοχή* (the great feast / agape feast): **"Arise, O Lord [my God, let Thy hand be lifted up,] forget not Thy poor unto the end"** [LXX Ps. 9:33 / 10:12]. Further according unto the 'Order', the priest in full sacerdotal vestments carries the Gospel, preceded by readers with two candlesticks and two deacons, ascends the ambo and sits with the deacons until the conclusion of the prokeimenon, after which is sung: **"Prolong the holy empire of our sovereigns"** and **"The Master…"** (*Τὸν δεσπότην*); at these hymns the sacred ministers stand up; upon the reading of the Gospel upon the ambo by the presbyter, it is borne by the deacon unto the hierarch to be kissed, and is carried into the altar by the deacon, preceded by the reader with the cross and accompanied by the presbyter (Dmitrievsky, *Τυπικά*, p. 164).

A., *Opisanie liturgicheskikh rukopisei*, Vol. II: *Εὐχολόγια*, p. 1110; Vol. I: *Τυπικά*, p. 152. — Similar litanies existed and still exist in the Western Church: such are the *litania major* on April 25 on the feast of the Apostle Mark, and the *litania minor* or *rogationes* (petitions) on the three days before Ascension. The designations 'major' and 'minor' do not correspond unto the comparative solemnity of these litanies, but are explained historically: the former was instituted earlier (in all probability by Pope Liberius in the 4th century) in place of the very solemn pagan procession *ambarvale* (from *arva*, field—a procession into the fields, in contrast unto *amburbale*, a city procession, to propitiate the goddess Robigo, upon whom depended mildew [*robigo*] upon crops; for which reason April 25 was termed *Robigalia*); Pope Gregory the Great regulated the Christian litany that replaced this pagan procession, appointing seven distinct corporations of Rome (clergy, men, monks, handmaids, married women, widows, and the poor with children) to go forth with the litany from different churches (whence the name *litania septiformis*; *Epistolae*, II, 2). The *litania minor* was first instituted circa 470 by Bishop Mamertus of Vienne on account of earthquakes and other calamities; it was legalized for Gaul by the Council of Orléans in 511 (Canon 27); in the 6th century it received wide dissemination, yet in Rome it was adopted only under Pope Leo III (795–816). On the days of these litanies, especially the second, services were exceptionally lengthy; in the 9th century in certain churches heads were sprinkled with blessed ashes, sprinkled with holy water (as is still done at present according unto the Ambrosian rite), and all walked barefoot (according unto Canon 33 of the Council of Mainz [*Concilium Moguntinum*] of 813). A number of orders have been preserved for the minor litany: according unto them, following a series of antiphons and psalms there was a supplication very similar unto our litany **"Let us all say"** (it begins indeed: *Dicamus omnes, Domine miserere*); following a series of other antiphons reminiscent in general content of our penitential troparia, there followed a litany in the proper sense, termed *statio* (station, since it was performed at the stopping-places of the procession) or *oratio* (from the frequent singing of *ora*—'pray'), consisting of a long series of petitions (at times exceeding 100), addressed for the most part in order unto all the most renowned saints and executed antiphonally (by clergy and choir), for example: *Sancta Maria, ora pro nobis*; *Sancte Michael, ora*; *Sancte Gabriel, ora* or *intercede* ('intercede'); for the more venerated and intimate saints 'pray for us' (*ora pro nobis*), and for the others simply 'pray' (*ora*) (Martène, *De antiquis ecclesiae ritibus*, Vol. II, pp. 514 ff.; Kellner, *Heortologie*, p. 127).
