Later, the word *typos* (τύπος) was assigned the meaning of an edict, a law; the word already possesses such a meaning in Philostratus († 240) and the 5th-century ecclesiastical historian Socrates (Sophocles, E., *Greek Lexicon of the Roman and Byzantine Periods*, New York–Leipzig, 1888, p. 1099); yet *typos* with this meaning does not form the adjective *typikos* (τυπικός) in any author; among ancient Christian writers (Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Basil the Great) this adjective signifies "symbolic," "prefigurative" (see *ibid.*). The root meaning of the word cannot entirely vanish in all instances of its usage; and if *typos* sometimes denotes a law or rule, it is of a special character; thus, in Gregory Nazianzen it is used in relation to the Symbol of Faith [the Creed]; in Justinian it signifies the Divine Law (for citations see Sophocles); Emperor Constans designated his well-known confessional edict by this term [*Typos*]. But a religious law or rule differs substantially from every other kind, and its distinctiveness is well expressed by the basic meaning of the word *typos*. More precisely than "rule" [*ustav*], *typikon* (τυπικόν) could be translated as "ordinal" [*chinovnik*], Lat. *ordinarium* (thus translated by Toscani, *Ad typica graecorum*, Romae, 1864, p. 5).
