This custom evoked also disputes in the Russian Church. At the *Stoglav* Council [1551], Tsar Ivan the Terrible posed, among others, the following question: "For what reason in our realm in Moscow and in all the borders of Moscow, in cathedral and parish churches, except monasteries, at Sunday Vespers and at feast-day Vespers and for great saints, when an Entrance taketh place, do they not sing 'Holy Glory' (that is, **'O Gladsome Light'**), but speak it with a speaking voice, and at Matins on Sunday and on feasts do they not sing the Doxology, but just as on other simple days speak it with a speaking voice?" # THE VESPERAL PROKEIMENON

## Concept of the Prokeimenon

After the hymn **"O Gladsome Light"** there followeth at Vespers, not only festal but also weekday (excluding Lenten and sometimes memorial [requiem] Vespers), the prokeimenon. With the prokeimenon there enters into Vespers a new form of ecclesiastical hymnody, differing from others first of all in its brevity. Whereas ecclesiastical chant customarily consisteth in the execution of more or less lengthy hymns, at times whole psalms, yet more frequently hymns of 5–6 lines (stichera, troparia), the prokeimenon represents a chant consisting of only a few words, composed of a single psalm verse alone, set unto an especially melodious and melismatic tune, and distinguished by repeated chanting: each of these repetitions is preceded by a "verse" in a less solemn execution. Breaking into the series of lengthy hymns of Vespers and Matins, the prokeimenon attracts attention unto itself by the distinctiveness of its form. Moreover, as the prokeimenon there serveth always a verse of Holy Scripture that is especially powerful, *(Orig. p. 562)* expressive, and suited unto the occasion; on which account prokeimena are borrowed almost exclusively from the most poetic book of Holy Scripture—the Psalter (among the prokeimena only a few are not from the Psalms: thus, from the Old Testament Canticles—**"Blessed art Thou, O Lord, God of our fathers"**; from the Gospel—**"My soul doth magnify [the Lord]"**; from the Epistles—**"The grace [of God] hath appeared..."**; and of special composition: **"The Body of Christ..."**, **"Blessed is the way..."**). As an example of an especially expressive prokeimenon may be cited the prokeimenon of Theophany Matins: **"The sea beheld and fled, Jordan was turned back"** [LXX Ps. 113:3]; verse: **"What aileth thee, O sea, that thou didst flee?"** [LXX Ps. 113:5]. Its name "prokeimenon" (*prokeimenos* [Gr. προκείμενος], that which lieth before) was received from the fact that it customarily precedeth, and formerly always preceded, a reading from Holy Scripture. The prokeimenon consisteth of a verse, in the proper sense termed the "prokeimenon", and of one or three "verses", which precede the repetition of the prokeimenon; in this manner, the prokeimenon is chanted the first time without a verse, the second time and subsequent times (if there be several verses) with a verse, and the final time again without a verse, being replaced by its first half.

## History of the Prokeimenon

The prokeimenon is the remnant of the singing of an entire psalm with a refrain unto each verse thereof of some most suitable or

=== LEAF p673 ===
familiar verse unto the people. Already in the 4th–5th centuries whole psalms were sung in this manner, and such singing was known under the name of hypophonic or responsorial, in distinction from antiphonal singing (Introductory Chapter, pp. 155–158). Inasmuch as singing of the first kind was easier and more convenient for execution by the entire congregation, it received, apparently, a wider dissemination. The Council of Laodicea (c. 360) requireth "that in prayer assemblies psalms not be sung continuously one after another, but that after each psalm in the interval (*dia mesou kath' hekaston psalmon* [Gr. διὰ μέσου καθ' ἕκαστον ψαλμόν]) a reading should follow" [^2230]. The prokeimena and alleluiaria are precisely the remnants of these psalms intervening between readings. In the Syriac Liturgy of St. James before the Epistle reading there is still appointed an entire psalm with a preparatory prayer for the cleansing of the heart for the singing thereof (cf. **"Wisdom"**, etc.) [^2231]. Already in the 4th century, from among the psalms sung in church, one verse began to be singled out in the capacity of a refrain, as it were eclipsing the entire psalm: "The fathers ordained," saith St. John Chrysostom, "that the people should chant in response from the psalm a powerful verse, containing sublime doctrine" [^2232]. In the 6th century, in the account of John Moschos and Sophronius concerning the visit unto Nilus of Sinai, the term prokeimenon ("that which lieth before" [*predlezhashchaya*]) hath, apparently, a meaning already close unto the present one (Introductory Chapter, p. 295). Yet even in monuments of the 8th–15th centuries, besides that which is presently designated by this word, the troparia at the Hours, and sometimes antiphons, are termed prokeimena (see above, pp. 341, 470). On the other hand, in an 11th-century manuscript (the Rossano MS) of the Liturgy of St. James, the prokeimenon is termed *propsalma* (*propsalma* [Gr. πρόψαλμα]) [^2233]. The *psallendo* (= *(Orig. p. 563)* for singing) of the Mozarabic Liturgy hath literally the form of our prokeimenon: it consisteth of a psalm verse termed *psallendo*, of another termed *versus* (verse), and of the repetition of the end of the first (likewise *psallendo*) [^2234]. In other rites of the Latin liturgy, unto the prokeimenon correspondeth the responsory, which until the 9th century was sung entirely like our prokeimenon, with a verse and with division into two parts (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 307–308). At present sometimes in the Roman Catholic rite the antiphon likewise occupieth the position of our prokeimenon (see Introductory Chapter, p. 156, n. 8; p. 157, n. 7).

## Acclamations Before the Prokeimenon

Inasmuch as the prokeimenon in antiquity always preceded a reading, and at present for the most part precedeth it, there are pronounced before it the very same acclamations wherewith from antiquity the reading of Holy Scripture was prefaced. At ordinary Sunday Vespers, at which there are no readings (paroemias), such acclamations before the prokeimenon are likewise not out of place: they single it out from among the other hymns of Vespers, concentrating

=== LEAF p674 ===
upon it the special attention of the worshippers. By these acclamations the deacon first of all inviteth unto special attentiveness: **"Let us attend"** (*proschōmen* [Gr. πρόσχωμεν]); the priest, as it were in the capacity of a condition for such attentiveness, for the first time from the sanctuary, into which he hath solemnly entered, imparteth peace. **"Peace be unto all"** is a greeting so becoming the Church Militant, unto which by Christ hath been bequeathed the peace that surpasseth all understanding. The deacon arouses unto attentiveness by pointing unto the singular wisdom contained in the impending hymn or reading: **"Wisdom! Let us attend!"**

## History of These Acclamations

The most ancient of these acclamations is **"Peace be unto all"**. **"Peace be unto you"** originally in the ancient bellicose East was not so much a greeting as a declaration that the visitor had come not with customary hostile intents [^2235]. Yet by the time of the Savior, "Peace" had become an ordinary greeting and was beloved of Him Himself [^2236]. Alongside it, and probably more customary than it, among the Hebrews was the greeting unto a person: **"The Lord be with thee"** or **"with you"** [^2237]. In the troubled first centuries of Christianity during liturgical assemblies that threatened the participants with arrest, the former greeting acquired an especially pleasing coloring, which, in conjunction with the fact that it had been consecrated by the Lord's own usage, rendered it more customary than the second alike in the Eastern and Western Church of the 4th–5th centuries (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 169–170). Yet subsequently in the West the second greeting (*Dominus vobiscum*) almost everywhere supplanted the first (surviving only in the Gallican Liturgy), perhaps because the first, as especially sacred and beloved of Christ, *(Orig. p. 564)* was avoided in the presence of catechumens [^2238] (against which even Tertullian cautioned, rebuking heretics for greeting all without distinction with peace) [^2239]. **"Peace be unto you"** in some places in the West was preserved only in the capacity of an episcopal greeting. The Council of Braga of 561 against the Priscillianists (who gravitated generally toward the East) decreed: "It is resolved that bishops should not greet the people otherwise than presbyters, but alike, saying: 'The Lord be with you', as is said in the Book of Ruth, and the people should answer: 'And with thy spirit', as this tradition of the Apostles themselves is maintained by the whole East (in relation unto Spain), and not as the Priscillianist error hath altered it" [^2240]. In the Liturgy of the Apostle Peter (the Alexandrian), which in general is close unto Western liturgies, in an 11th-century manuscript the greeting is always **"The Lord be with you"**, while in a 17th-century manuscript it is sometimes also **"Peace be unto all"**, wherein the former is pronounced, as also in the Roman Church, sometimes by the deacon [^2241]. Of the Eastern liturgies,

=== LEAF p675 ===
the Liturgy of the Apostle James is replete, especially in its later manuscripts, with the greeting **"Peace be unto all"**, sometimes **"Peace be unto thee"**; in particular, it hath this greeting also before the prokeimenon; moreover, in its most ancient manuscript in this latter place there is this single acclamation alone [^2242]. The acclamation **"Let us attend"** is likewise found already in the 4th century in liturgical usage (see Introductory Chapter, p. 170). Unto it correspondeth in the Mozarabic Liturgy **"Keep silence"** (*Silentium facite*) before the Epistle [^2243]. Yet before the prokeimenon it appeareth only in later manuscripts of the Liturgy of St. James; on the other hand, in the Liturgies of Basil the Great and John Chrysostom, beginning with the most ancient (8th–10th c.) manuscripts, it appeareth in twofold form, although it is not present in all manuscripts, and by no means in all in twofold form [^2244]. As regards the acclamation **"Wisdom"**, the Alexandrian Liturgy, which hath **"Peace be unto all"** and **"Let us attend"**, doth not have it, nor doth the most ancient manuscript of the Liturgy of St. James (yet before the Symbol of Faith [Creed] it hath: **"In the wisdom of God let us attend"**); before the prokeimenon only a later manuscript of the Liturgy of St. James hath it [^2245], whereas all manuscripts of the Liturgy of Basil the Great, beginning from the 8th–10th centuries, contain it. It is remarkable that concerning these acclamations before the prokeimenon at Vespers the manuscripts of the Typikon and the present *Sluzhebnik*, as also the Old Believer Typikon, make no mention whatever. They are indicated only in the *Diataxis* of Patriarch Philotheus in the very same form as in the present Typikon—consequently, they were borrowed thence; St. Symeon of Thessalonica at Chanted Vespers doth not mention these acclamations, yet at ordinary Vespers he indicateth: "The deacon saith: **'Wisdom'**, and the hierarch, having sat down, imparteth peace" [^2246]. Hence it is evident that the prokeimenon was appointed to be heard seated, as also the reading following it, yet not by all, but only by the priests at the high place [synthronon]. The Greek Euchologion of the Imperial Public Library, *(Orig. p. 565)* Bishop Porphyrius Collection No. 226 of the 8th–10th centuries, in the order of the Liturgy of Basil the Great, saith concerning the chanting of the prokeimenon: "And turning, [the priest] saith: **'Let us attend. Peace be unto all'**; and immediately he sitteth down and saith: **'Wisdom'**; and the chanter: **'A Psalm of David. Let us attend'**, and the prokeimenon"; a slightly later manuscript (in Goar) instead of "immediately he sitteth down" hath: "And the hierarch sitteth down with the priests, while the deacons and ministers, and the clergy and the people stand" [^2247]. Such sitting of the priests during the prokeimenon and reading (of the Epistle) is termed in ancient Typika the "high sitting" (*hē kathedra anō* [Gr. ἡ καθέδρα ἄνω]) or "great sitting", in distinction from the sitting of the entire congregation, which is termed the "low sitting" (*kathedra katō* [Gr. καθέδρα κάτω]) or "little sitting", and which was permitted at all readings except the paroemias and the Epistle (and naturally, the Gospel) [^2248]. Although after the prokeimenon at Sunday Vespers there was no reading in antiquity either, just as now, yet, as we have just seen, in the time of St. Symeon of Thessalonica (15th c.) the sacred ministers sat at the high place for the duration thereof;

=== LEAF p676 ===
whence cometh the present (unwritten) custom of their standing at this time at the high place.

## Manner of Execution of the Prokeimenon

Besides the introductory acclamations, the prokeimenon is distinguished from other hymnographic compositions by the external ceremonial setting of its chanting. In the execution of the prokeimenon the most conspicuous part is taken by the canonarch, who thus steps forward in divine worship at especially solemn moments: at **"The Lord is God"** or Alleluia (see Chapter 27 of the Typikon). After the acclamations before the prokeimenon, "the canonarch, having made a bow unto the superior, intoneth the prokeimenon and the verses, while the clergy [singers in the choir] chant the prokeimenon; the reader (= canonarch), having his arms folded upon his breast, standing in the midst of the temple, awaiteth the conclusion of the prokeimenon; and having made a bow, departeth unto his place."

## According Unto Ancient Typika

The stepping forward of the canonarch at the prokeimenon is the remnant of the former chanting of it and of its verses by a single singer prior unto the singing by the people (whence also the name—canonarch); for this reason in the above-cited passage of the Euchologion of the 8th–10th centuries, in place of the canonarch there is the chanter; see Introductory Chapter, p. 295; cf. at present at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts **"Let my prayer arise"**. Concerning the canonarch at the prokeimenon, manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon speak in the following manner: the 13th-century Georgian MS—instead of "canonarch"—"he who must recite the prokeimenon"; Greek MSS: "the appointed (*tachtheis* [Gr. ταχθείς]) monk"; ancient Slavic MSS: "another monk" (that is, in comparison with the one who read **"O Gladsome Light"**). Instead of "intoneth" [saith], ancient Greek MSS have nothing; later ones (from the 14th c.) and printed Greek editions: "singeth" (*psallei* [Gr. ψάλλει]), and so also Slavic MSS. Instead of "while the clergy chant", the Georgian MS hath: "the people the same"; the most ancient Greek MSS: "when we chant"; later Greek, printed Greek, and Slavic MSS have nothing, that is, they speak of the chanting of the prokeimenon and its verses by the canonarch alone. Greek and Slavic MSS before "having folded his arms" have: "having turned toward the west". The Old Believer Typikon hath all as our own, only before "having folded his arms": "having turned toward the west" [^2249].

## The Prokeimenon of Sunday Vespers

The prokeimenon of Saturday-Sunday Vespers is sung more solemnly than at weekday Vespers. It is sung according unto the order of a Great Prokeimenon, although it is not titled "Great"; namely, it is sung

=== LEAF p677 ===
with three verses, and consequently 4 1/2 times. Besides the present Vespers, such an execution is possessed by the prokeimenon at Vespers of the Twelve Great Feasts of the Lord, during Bright Week, and at Vespers of the Sunday days themselves of the Great Fast [Lent]. In this regard Saturday-Sunday Vespers is placed higher than Vespers even of the Twelve Great Feasts of the Theotokos, which hath no Great Prokeimenon, and is equalized with Feasts of the Lord. The difference with the latter is only that the Great Prokeimenon there is sung at Vespers not on the eve of the feast, but on the feast day itself (with the exception of that case when the feast falleth on a Saturday); this is because Vespers is considered as pertaining unto the following day only from **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** (see Typikon, Cheesefare Sunday); at Sunday Vespers, however, it is otherwise, because the portion thereof before **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** falleth upon such a semi-festal or pre-festal day as Saturday; whereas the festal celebration of Sunday itself, having no afterfeast, must cease earlier than on the Twelve Great Feasts. Great Prokeimena are assigned unto Vespers, and not unto Matins, by virtue of its ancient connection with the Divine Liturgy and the agape.

Already the Studite Typikon possessed Great prokeimena, that is, with three verses—yet only for Pascha, the Nativity of Christ, Theophany, and also at Matins of Great Saturday—the first before the paroemia; the second at this Matins with two verses [^2250]. All other prokeimena according unto this Typikon have but a single verse, including **"The Lord is King"** (*Gospod' votsarisya*) at Sunday Vespers [^2251]. So also the 13th-century Georgian manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon; Greek and Slavic manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon, however, as well as printed Greek editions, direct the prokeimenon **"The Lord is King"** to be sung with two verses—the first two of the present ones, and in Greek this prokeimenon is termed *distichon* (*distychon* [Gr. δίστιχον / printed δίστυχον], two-versed), evidently in distinction from the great and ordinary ones [^2252]. Later Slavic manuscripts are silent concerning the verses [^2253]. The Old Believer Typikon is like our own. Greek and Slavic manuscripts say nothing concerning the concluding **"The Lord is King"**; the 13th-century Georgian manuscript, however, after its single verse unto the prokeimenon, hath: "the chanter: **'The Lord is King'**, the people the same". Yet the half-singing of the prokeimenon for the concluding time, without doubt, goeth back unto profound antiquity, as musical tradition and the analogy of the Roman Catholic Church demonstrate (see Introductory Chapter, p. 308).

The prokeimenon of Sunday Vespers is sung in Tone 6, the most mournful of the tones, without doubt for the same reason that in this tone there is sung also the first sticheron of the Paschal service (**"Thy Resurrection, O Christ Savior"**): the joy of the *(Orig. p. 567)* Resurrection breaketh forth for the first time through the grief over the death of Christ. In manuscripts the tone is not indicated.

As regards content, the prokeimenon of Sunday Vespers **"The Lord is King"**, with its verses: **"The Lord is clothed..."**, **"For He hath established the world..."**, **"Holiness becometh Thy house"**, borrowed

=== LEAF p678 ===
from Psalm 92 [LXX 92; MT 93], doth not speak as directly of the Resurrection as the Matins prokeimena, yet on the other hand it speaketh of it from an interior aspect, indicating its profound essence in the royal glorification and, as it were, strengthening of the Lord, in the consolidation of the world that was disintegrating from sin, and in the founding of the Church. The immediate sense of the words of the psalm taken for the prokeimenon and constituting, presumably, the glorification of God for some victory (in the LXX the psalm is inscribed: "A song of praise of David")—is that God, as Creator of the world, is also its King, and His royal vesture is creation itself, revealing both His glory (majesty) and power (He is clothed with strength); as such, God is able to secure also the dignity, that is, the holiness, of His singular dwelling-place upon earth—the temple, preserving it from desecration.

The psalm was used among the Hebrews on Friday, as the joyful day of the completion of creation and the eve of the Sabbath (see Introductory Chapter, p. 11), whence probably cometh its inscription in the LXX: "On the day before the Sabbath, when the earth was founded" [LXX Ps. 92:1 incipit]. In the Syriac Liturgy of St. James it is recited upon the dismissal of the 'hearers' (catechumens) before the Symbol of Faith [Creed] (cf. with us at the Proskomedia) according unto an order similar unto the chanting of the prokeimenon with us—the priest: **"The Lord is King, He is clothed with strength, alleluia"**, followed by a brief glorification of the Son of God as the Bread of Life; the deacon: **"The Lord is clothed with strength and hath girded Himself, for He hath established the world which shall not be moved, alleluia"**, with a glorification of like content; the priest: **"Holiness becometh Thy house, O Lord, unto length of days. Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit"**; the deacon: **"The Lord is King"** [^2254]. In the Roman rite of divine worship this psalm is used at Sunday Lauds (Introductory Chapter, p. 315). At Chanted Vespers it must have constituted the 2nd antiphon, whereof by the 15th century there remained only the refrain **"The world"** (*Vselennuyu*) (Introductory Chapter, p. 341), yet at that time Chanted Vespers already possessed the present Sunday prokeimenon (Introductory Chapter, p. 342). Studite and Jerusalem Sunday Vespers possessed such a prokeimenon from the very beginning.

# PART 2 OF VESPERS

The subsequent order of Vespers, as has been said, is of a more prayerful than laudatory content. This second and principal part of Vespers hath much in common with the Divine Liturgy: the very same litanies (and at festal, most ancient Vespers, in that very *(Orig. p. 568)* same sequence); in ancient Vespers and Matins this resemblance was further augmented by the presence of prayers for the catechumens, those preparing for illumination [the photizomenoi], penitents, and the like. In Vespers this resemblance is even greater than in Matins: the conclusion of Vespers—**"Now lettest Thou"** [Canticle of Simeon], the Trisagion, and the troparion—coincideth fully with the conclusion of the Liturgy, a conclusion which, moreover, is performed by the priest already secretly [inaudibly], after the prayers of thanksgiving after Holy Communion. Thus, the second part of Vespers, like the conclusion of Matins [^2255], is, properly speaking, a Liturgy without the Gifts, yet by no means utterly devoid of the prayerful grace of the true Liturgy, inasmuch as the priest here (after the Entrance) approacheth the very same throne of grace. And it containeth prayers already for the faithful alone.

# THE AUGMENTED LITANY (*EKTENĒS HIKETEIA*)

## Its Content

This part of Vespers (as also of Matins) begins with one of the most fervent supplications known unto the Typikon, termed in common parlance the Augmented Litany (*sugubaya ekteniya*) [^2256], and in the liturgical books "fervent supplication" (*ektenēs deēsis* [Gr. ἐκτενὴς δέησις]) [^2257]. The short prayers offered up hitherto in several litanies are elevated in their intensity across the 12 petitions (reckoning with the exclamation) of the present litany: the faithful are invited to offer them up with all their soul and with all their mind, and begin their supplication with a petition for being heard and for mercy in general. The Augmented Litany is a prayer for the faithful alone, as St. Symeon of Thessalonica likewise defineth its content: "for faithful kings, for the local hierarch, for the superior of the monastery, for the guardians of the temple, for the Orthodox standing present, and for the faithful everywhere" [^2258]. With particular distinctness the litany prayeth for the tsar [sovereign], namely for 10 needs of his: for the strength of his rule ('might' [*derzhava*], *kratous* [Gr. κράτους]), victory (in warfare), longevity ('abiding' [*prebyvanie*], *diamonēs* [Gr. διαμονῆς]), peace, health, salvation (eternal), yet greater ('more abundantly' [*naipache*], *epipleon* [Gr. ἐπιπλέον]) divine assistance ('to help' [*posobiti*], *synergēsai* [Gr. συνεργῆσαι]) unto him, and full success ('to prosper *(Orig. p. 569)* [him]' [*pospeshiti*], *kateuodōsai* [printed *kateuodōsoa* / Gr. κατευοδῶσαι]) [^2259] in all things, and the complete subjection ('under his feet' [*pod noze*]) unto him of enemies domestic (*echthron* [Gr. ἐχθρόν]) and foreign ('adversary' [*supostata*], *polemion* [Gr. πολέμιον]); then a separate petition for the empress [queen] and a separate one for the heir and the reigning house (without uniting the former with the petition for the tsar, and the latter with the petition for the authorities and the military, as in the Great Litany); for the spiritual authority (the Holy Synod and the local bishop) together with all our brethren in Christ (= *adelphotētos* [Gr. ἀδελφότητος], more precisely 'brotherhood' = the 'clergy' [*prichte*] of the Great Litany); for the armed forces; a new petition in comparison with the Great Litany for the departed—and first of all for the founders of the monastery ('builders' [*sozdatelekh*], *ktētorōn* [Gr. κτητόρων], ktitors), who are termed on account of this their work blessed (almost saintly) and ever-memorable (*aeimnēstōn* [Gr. ἀειμνήστων]), and thereafter also for all the departed, yet of them first, naturally, for those buried right here; after this petition as it were for the departed brethren of the monastery—a petition for the present brotherhood and all their needs (which are enumerated with the same distinctness as in the petition for the tsar—8, among them for 'visitation' [*poseshchenie*], *episkepseōs* [Gr. ἐπισκέψεως], special mercies of God, then not only for 'forgiveness' [*proshchenie*], but also for the complete 'remission' [*ostavlenie*] of sins); constantly narrowing the circle of those interceded for, the Augmented Litany in its final petition
