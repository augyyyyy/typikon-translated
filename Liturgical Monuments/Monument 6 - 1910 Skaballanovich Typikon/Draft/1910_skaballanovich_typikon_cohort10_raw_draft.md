=== LEAF p91 ===
Origen holdeth a loftier view concerning the hours of prayer than his teacher Clement of Alexandria: "The entire life of a holy man ought to be one great, unceasing prayer, a part whereof shall be prayer in the proper sense, which ought to be performed not less than three times a day"; having referred unto Daniel, the Apostle Peter, and David in proof of the necessity of praying at three "times of the day," Origen addeth: "yet neither shall we spend the nocturnal time without this manner of prayer, as it is related concerning Paul in the Acts that he with Silas in Philippi prayed at night" [^448].
*(Orig. p. 95)*
*St. Cyprian* already elaborateth carefully the symbolism of the prayer hours of the day. Perceiving together with Tertullian in the threefold number of the hours the mystery of the Holy Trinity, St. Cyprian discerneth the same signification also in the three-hour intervals separating these hours one from another; in particular, St. Cyprian discerneth the significance of the 3rd hour in the descent of the Holy Spirit, of the 6th in the prayer of the Apostle Peter which decided the reception of the Gentiles into the Church, and of the 6th and 9th in that

"the Lord, crucified at the 6th hour, until the 9th washed away our sins with His blood... For us, however, beloved brethren, beyond the hours observed from of old, the times (*spatia*) of praying and the mysteries have now increased. For it is meet to pray also in the morning, that by morning prayer the Resurrection of the Lord may be glorified. At the setting of the sun and the conclusion of the day it is necessary again to pray; since Christ is the true Sun and the true Day, therefore at the setting of the sun and the conclusion of the day, when we pray that light may come upon us, we entreat (*praecamur*) the coming of Christ, Who will impart the grace of eternal light... And if we abide always in Christ, that is, in the light, then even at night we ought not to cease from prayer... Let not the darkness which we have escaped hinder us; let there be no omission of prayer in the nocturnal hours (*nulla sint horis nocturnis precum damna*) from slothfulness and neglect" [^449].

Thus, all the most important ecclesiastical writers of the 3rd century make mention of the hours of prayer, and all alike designate as such hours the 3rd, 6th, and 9th, noting that besides these morning and evening were dedicated unto prayer, and that prayers at night were likewise in custom. Apparently, at these hours private prayer is presupposed, yet the analogy of these hours with the morning and evening assemblies for divine worship, as well as the designation of nocturnal prayers in Tertullian as "assemblies" (*convocationes*), permitteth us to think that in certain places and at certain times small

=== LEAF p92 ===
prayer gatherings were held at these hours. Thus the writers of the first half of the 3rd century know already, not counting Matins with the Liturgy and Vespers, up to 6 prayer hours (and with these services—8): morning prayer, the 3rd, 6th, and 9th [printed: 8th] hours, evening prayer, and nocturnal prayer, perchance even more than one (St. Cyprian: "nocturnal hours"). The prayers of the 3rd, 6th, and 9th [printed: 8th] hours consequently bore approximately the same character as the morning and evening prayers. Hence even now among certain Eastern Christians (for example, the Armenians), who have preserved an exceedingly ancient ecclesiastical practice, morning and evening prayers are accounted an ecclesiastical service on a par with the others (as likewise in certain ancient Greek and Slavonic Horologia). Remarkable is the testimony of St. Cyprian that "now the times of praying and the mysteries have increased": before his very eyes there was taking place, as it were, both the multiplication and the complication of these secondary ecclesiastical services.

#### Prayer Hours according to Canonical Monuments of the 3rd Century

Naturally, the canonical monuments of the 3rd century, which belong rather unto its second half than unto the first (upon which fall the aforementioned *(Orig. p. 96)* testimonies), know already both a somewhat greater number of prayer hours and a more communal, liturgical character attending them. The testimonies of canonical monuments concerning the prayer hours are important for us also in that they speak of the practice of diverse Churches: Syrian, Egyptian, and Roman, and nonetheless indicate a uniform practice. The slight difference in this practice among the diverse Churches may testify that this practice was elaborated independently. In view of the particular importance of this epoch for the elaboration of the ritual of the liturgical hours, the first foundations whereof were now being laid, we shall present in parallel all the prescriptions of this kind in the canonical monuments of the 3rd century. Let us begin with the *Testamentum*, as providing more succinct indications.

The *Testamentum* in its prescriptions concerning the prayer hours makes a distinction between the clergy (more specifically, the bishop and presbyter) and the laity, requiring from the former greater zeal and diligence in observing these hours. Comparing all these testimonies one with another, one cannot fail to

=== LEAF p98 ===
notice first of all that the *Testamentum* imparteth unto the hours of prayer in part that character which they possess now in the Catholic Church, being obligatory only for the clergy (*horae canonicae*), although it requireth the arrangement at these hours of at least small prayer assemblies. The latter two monuments, however—the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*—insist more upon the unfailing observance of the hours of prayer by all the faithful. Characteristic is the indication of these monuments regarding the very frequent, well-nigh daily celebration of the morning divine service, wherein the reading of Holy Scripture is recognized as its most important part. Amid the rarity of copies of the Bible at that time, the church was the sole place where one could study it, the most accessible school of theology. It appeareth incomprehensible that the monuments require attendance at the morning divine service especially from "those who know how to read": perchance the latter more than others neglected these assemblies, deeming the domestic reading of Holy Scripture sufficient. The *Egyptian Canons* note especially the broad development of preaching (not without reason do they share a common homeland with Origen). The *Testamentum* and the *Egyptian Canons* single out from among the other hours of prayer the ninth, indicating for its prayers pre-eminently *(Orig. p. 101)* a joyful and laudatory character. Such a character the service of this hour beareth even now. The *Testamentum*, comparatively with the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*, possesseth new liturgical orders. Thus in the Syriac recension it indicateth in the evening, besides the hour of the lighting of lamps (Vespers) and the "first hour" of the night, corresponding unto the service of retiring unto sleep of the other monuments or our Compline, yet the 12th hour of the day as a prayer hour, placing it before the lighting of lamps. An echo of this practice is that in the present Coptic Horologion "Compline" precedeth Vespers [^458]. The Arabic recension of the *Testamentum*, omitting the service of the 12th hour, possesseth another, superfluous in comparison with the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons*—namely, the *first hour (of the day)*. It is possible that the Syriac *Testamentum* likewise pointeth unto this service by the word "in the morning" (if a comma be placed after it). Characteristic is that insistence wherewith the *Testamentum*, yet only in the Syriac text, requireth the observance of the *nocturnal hours* of prayer: either because these hours were more neglected, or by reason of their special favorableness for a prayerful disposition. Certain ancient Horologia, as we shall see subsequently, possess an entire system of "nocturnal hours," equalizing them in number with the diurnal ones and bringing both quantities separately up to 12.

Thus already in the 3rd century there exist positively all our present ecclesiastical services under the form of prayer at the corresponding hours, while of these services: the Liturgy, Matins, Vespers, and the 3rd, 6th, and 9th Hours trace their origin back unto the apostolic age.

#### The Order of the Hours of the 3rd Century

=== LEAF p99 ===
The monuments of the 3rd century do not indicate for the prayer hours any definite order. Consequently, herein everything was left unto the freedom of the believer. Yet already in the 2nd century, as we have seen, the indispensable accompaniment of the prayer hours then observed was the Lord's Prayer. That this was so in the 3rd century as well is clearly evident from the fact that Tertullian, precisely in his treatise *On Prayer*, speaketh of the prayer hours, as likewise from the fact that well-nigh every significant writer of the 3rd century left a commentary upon this prayer (Tertullian, Origen, St. Cyprian). To what extent the Lord's Prayer was considered basic, all-exhaustive, and necessary at every prayer, and consequently in honoring the sacred hours of the day, is demonstrated by the tone of defense wherein Tertullian speaketh concerning other prayers: "since there exist petitions corresponding unto the circumstances of each, then, having premised the established and ordinary prayer (that is, the Lord's Prayer, as the context demonstrateth) as a foundation, as it were, we have the right to express accidental (*accidentium*) desires, the right, as it were, to pass beyond the bounds of petitions" [^459]. These "supplementary" prayers unto the *Our Father* *(Orig. p. 102)*, presumably, did not yet possess ready models anywhere, judging from the fact that the *Testamentum*, so rich in prayers, provideth no such models; yet the "prayer of the widows" at midnight in that same *Testamentum* may be regarded as the first attempt to formulate prayers also for the liturgical hours.

Tertullian permitteth us to say yet more concerning the prayer ritual of the liturgical hours. Immediately after speaking concerning these hours, remarking that "a brother who hath entered the house must not be sent away without prayer," he saith: "those more zealous in prayer have the custom of subjoining unto prayers the alleluia and psalms in such manner that by their concluding words those present may respond (unto him)." With this testimony of Tertullian one may compare the following prescription of the *Testamentum*: "unto him who singeth in church, let the virgins and boys respond. If also at home two or three sing privately, in like manner alternately let those who sing respond; likewise also the men" [^460]. Both there and here there is clearly question of that semi-private, semi-public prayer which the prayer "hours" represent in the canonical monuments of the 3rd century; and as an accompaniment of this prayer, that is, of the then order of the hours, there are already indicated, as also now, both psalms and the alleluia (now with us concluding the psalms of the Hours), and the singing or other public recitation of the most expressive verses of the psalms (cf. our present prokeimenon, the refrains unto the troparia of the hour, and the "verse" of the hour). — The same Tertullian in the same treatise acquainteth us with a whole series of rites in use at these semi-public prayers, whereof certain he disapproveth, while others he recommendeth, and which in greater or lesser degree have been reflected in our present divine service in general, and in particular in that of the Hours. Of these rites the most

=== LEAF p100 ===
customary and necessary in Tertullian appeareth genuflection: "every day we ought to prostrate ourselves before God, at least at the first prayer wherewith we greet the day; on fasts and stations, however, no prayer can be without genuflection" [^461]. Well-nigh equally necessary at every prayer was the "kiss of peace" [^462] (preserved with us only at the Liturgy). It was customary likewise to lift up and spread forth the hands [^463], and to wash the hands before prayer [^464]. Tertullian expresseth disapproval concerning the customs of taking off the outer garment at prayer and sitting down after it [^465]. Tertullian makes no mention in this enumeration of rites of the sign of the *(Orig. p. 103)* cross, yet in another place he speaketh concerning it: "at every departure (*progressum*) and movement, at every entrance and exit, at putting on clothes and shoes, at washing, at table, at the lighting of lights, at bed, at sitting down, and in every action we trace (*terimus*) upon the forehead the sign (*signaculo*) of the cross" [^466]. But the *Canons of Hippolytus* and the *Egyptian Canons* speak of the sign of the cross immediately after the discourse concerning the liturgical hours and in connection therewith.
