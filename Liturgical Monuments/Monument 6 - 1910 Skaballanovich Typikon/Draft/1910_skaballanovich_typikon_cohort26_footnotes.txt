[^1403]: Otherwise K. A. H. Kellner, *Heortologie*, S. 99. Scholars determine variously that which gave occasion unto the separation of the festival of the Nativity of Christ from Theophany. It is thought that the cessation of martyrdom exerted a partial influence here, enabling Christians to value more highly earthly life and the nativity thereof; perhaps they desired to establish a Christian festival in opposition unto the pagan feasts celebrating the December solstice. In Rome from December 18 to 23 the Saturnalia were celebrated; in correspondence unto the grim character of the god Saturn or Cronus (who devoured his own children), this was a mournful festival with gladiatorial combats (St. Cyril of Alexandria, *Contra Julianum*, lib. IV); following upon it came the joyful feast of the 'Unconquered Sun' (*Sol Invictus*), celebrated with particular splendor by Emperor Julian in honor of the rising sun and on the threshold of the new year, falling at the season when the turning of the sun had already become noticeable unto all (Emperor Julian, *Oratio IV* [*In Solem Regem*]). In the aforementioned Roman Calendar of Philocalus under December 25 there is noted: '*N(atalis) Invicti*'—'the birthday of the Unconquered': 'Unconquered' was a customary epithet of the gods, especially appropriate with respect unto the nascent sun (it is possible, however, that the birthday of the reigning emperor was so styled in this calendar, since in other months the calendar designates the birthdays of the emperors—Bishop Christopher [F. Smirnov], *The Origin and Significance of the Feast of the Nativity of Christ* [*Proiskhozhdeniye i znacheniye prazdnika Rozhdestva Khristova*], Kyiv, 1883 [al. 1893], pp. 92 et seq.). In Alexandria on December 25 there was the pagan festival of Kikellia; there were festivals in such a season in other countries as well. The supposition hath also been voiced that the Jewish feast of the Dedication of the Temple on the 25th day of the month of Kislev [al. Tebeth] (= December), unto which indications are found in the Prophets Haggai (2:15–18) and Zechariah (8:19; compare John 10:22), and that moreover in connection with Messianic predictions, could likewise have exerted an influence upon the establishment of the festival upon December 25 (F. Smirnov, *ibid.*, pp. 59 et seq.). — When the festival of the Nativity of Christ was assigned unto December 25, that day came to be accounted the day of the historical event itself. Prior unto that time the question concerning the day of the Saviour's birth was disputed. We have seen that Clement of Alexandria indicates, apparently, three opinions existing in his day regarding the day of the Saviour's birth; some assigned it unto Pachon 25 (an Egyptian month, = May 20), others unto Tybi 15 or 11 (January 10 or 6). Compare above the opinion of St. Epiphanius of Cyprus. In the III–IV century treatise *De pascha computus*, attributed unto St. Cyprian of Carthage, March 28 is accounted the day of Christ's Nativity, whereas in the inscription upon the statue of St. Hippolytus of Rome it is March 25. In the portion of St. Hippolytus's *Commentary on the Book of the Prophet Daniel* newly discovered by Georgiades (in Chalki) there is the observation: 'The first manifestation of our Lord in the flesh, wherein He was born in Bethlehem, occurred on December 25 in the 42nd year of Augustus, 5500 years from Adam; and He suffered on March 25 on a Friday, in the 18th year of Tiberius, in the consulate of Rufus and Rubellius'; but in other fragments of the same commentary (for example, in the ancient manuscript of the Chigi Library) and in the quotation of this passage by the Armenian Bishop George from 714 AD, the day of the month is not indicated here, nor is it required by the context of the commentary (K. A. H. Kellner, *Heortologie*, S. 98). Only in the IV and V centuries did fathers begin resolutely to defend the date of December 25 for the Nativity of Christ—St. John Chrysostom (see below) and Blessed Augustine (*Sermones* 190, § 1; 192, § 3; 196, § 1).
