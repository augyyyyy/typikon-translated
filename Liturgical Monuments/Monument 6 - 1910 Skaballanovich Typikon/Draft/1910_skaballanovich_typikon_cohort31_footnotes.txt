[^1545]: In Rome already under Pope Gregory I the Great there existed a martyrology with commemorations for each day of the year, as is evident from the reply of this pope in 598 unto the request of Eulogius, Patriarch of Alexandria, to send him the *Acts of All the Martyrs* collected by Eusebius of Caesarea; replying unto the patriarch that no such collection was found in the Roman archives, the Pope writes: 'But we possess a collection in one codex of the names of almost all the martyrs, with an indication of their passions for all days, and daily we celebrate masses in their honor; however, in this volume it is not shown how each suffered, but only the name, place, and day of the passion are indicated... We think that you also possess this, most blessed brother' (Gregory the Great, *Registrum epistolarum* VIII, 29). Blessed Jerome is considered the compiler of the most ancient Roman martyrology that has come down unto us, on the basis of the testimonies of Cassiodorus († 563): 'Read the passions of the martyrs, which you will find, among other places, in the letter of Jerome unto Chromatius and Heliodorus' (*De institutione divinarum litterarum*, c. 32; Migne, *Patrologia Latina*, Vol. 70, col. 1147), and of Bede († 735): '...a martyrology which by name and preface is attributed unto Blessed Jerome, although Jerome was not its author, but its translator, while the author, they say, was Eusebius' (*Liber retractationis in Acta Apostolorum*, c. 1; Migne, *PL*, Vol. 92, col. 997). The correspondence of Blessed Jerome with Bishops Heliodorus and Chromatius is appended unto the copies of the Hieronymian Martyrology; these bishops requested that he send them from Caesarea the calendar (*feriales*) of Eusebius for the greater veneration of the martyrs—in which Gregory, Bishop of Cordova, was especially renowned, as was revealed at a certain Council of Milan, celebrating 'on every non-fasting day morning masses, and during fasts evening masses, and commemorating therein many names of martyrs'; Blessed Jerome, in place of the calendar of Eusebius, sends unto the bishops his own, wherein, according unto his epistle, he 'inscribed the feasts for each month and for each day; but since for each day there occurred more than 800–900 names of martyrs of diverse regions, and for no single day, except January 1, fewer than 500, he records only the more renowned.' The authenticity of this correspondence is doubtful, since the style here is not Jerome's; and apart from it, the compilation of the martyrology by Blessed Jerome is corroborated by nothing else: neither he himself, nor Gennadius of Marseilles († after 495) in his work *De scriptoribus ecclesiasticis*, nor Gregory the Great speaks of such a compilation; furthermore, the martyrology contains commemorations later than Blessed Jerome. It was published according unto a 10th-century MS in Migne, *Patrologia Latina*, Vol. 30, and by the Bollandists, *Acta Sanctorum*, Novembris tomus II, Brussels, 1894, according unto 8th-century MSS (edited by Giovanni Battista de Rossi and Louis Duchesne).
[^1546]: If in the second century mass slaughters of Christians were represented by a figure of no more than 40 (Lyons, 177), then in the third century and especially under Diocletian the martyrs were numbered in the thousands; the magnitude of the figure here therefore excites no suspicion, nor does the frequent repetition of certain names (Felix 118 times, Saturninus 87, etc.), as well as the strangeness of other names (*Manira*, *Jtercola*, *Barbalabia*); one must bear in mind the Roman custom of showing no restraint in assigning nicknames unto slaves.
