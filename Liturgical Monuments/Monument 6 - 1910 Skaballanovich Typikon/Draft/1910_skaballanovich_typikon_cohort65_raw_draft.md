# THE LITTLE LITANY AT THE VESPERAL KATHISMA

A special solemnity is imparted unto the First Kathisma likewise by the fact that after each Stasis (*Slava*) thereof a litany is appointed, whereas at Matins a litany is appointed only after an entire Kathisma, and at weekday Matins after a Kathisma there are no litanies at all. Such solemnity reminds of the origin of Vespers from the Liturgy and the agape meal, and constitutes a survival of the prayers and litanies at the initial antiphons of Chanted Vespers (from which the "little antiphons" at the conclusion of Vespers were distinguished—see Introductory Chapter, pp. 377 ff.). Litanies add prayer unto psalmic praise. For this purpose Little Litanies here suffice, which, being an abridgment of the recently uttered Great Litany, as it were merely resurrect its supplications in memory. Historically, the presence here of Little Litanies is explained by the prayers of the antiphons formerly situated at this place in Vespers, for which the litany served merely as an introduction in its 1st petition and a conclusion in its 2nd and 3rd petitions and exclamation, as is done at present at the Kneeling Prayers of Pentecost. The Little Litany repeated thrice throughout the First Kathisma is varied by its exclamation: at the 1st litany: **"For Thine is the might, and Thine is the kingdom and the power and the glory, of the Father..."**; at the 2nd: **"For Thou art a good God and lovest mankind, and unto Thee do we send up glory, unto the Father..."**; at the 3rd: **"For Thou art our God, a God that showeth mercy and saveth, and unto Thee do we send up glory..."** [^2153]. These exclamations all represent a gradual unfolding of the Divine attributes. If the exclamation of the Great Litany points indeterminately and generally unto the supreme worshipfulness of God (His majesty), *(Orig. p. 537)* then the second points unto His power and universal governance; the third unto His goodness and providence toward mankind; and the fourth unto the complete revelation of the Godhead in redemption.

## History of the Little Litany

The formula **"Again and again"** (*eti kai eti* / *paki i paki*) is found already in the *Apostolic Constitutions* (4th–5th c.) before the litany [^2154]. In the Liturgy of the Apostle James according unto the 11th-century Rossano Codex, before one of the opening prayers of the priest stands the proclamation of the deacon: **"Let us pray unto the Lord"**; before one of the subsequent prayers stands the deacon's exclamation: **"Again (*eti*) let us pray unto the Lord"** (at present among us such a formula is found in the petitions of the Augmented Litany, yet *eti* is there translated: "furthermore"); in the litany for the offered Gifts (after their hallowing) in this Liturgy, the first petition is: **"Again and again (*eti kai eti*) continually let us pray unto the Lord"**; the same occurs in the litany of thanksgiving after communion; in this

=== LEAF p643 ===
manner, the proclamation **"Again and again"** appears only at the conclusion of the Liturgy; so likewise in the 14th-century copies of the same Liturgy, yet there the first diaconal proclamation is likewise: **"Again let us pray unto the Lord"** [^2155]. As we have seen (see above, p. 523), already the Liturgy of the Apostle James provides a litany corresponding unto our Great Litany, not always in one and the same scope, but at times in an abridged form; yet such an extensive abridgment thereof as is provided in the present Little Litany it knoweth not; nevertheless, the final litany in this Liturgy, that of thanksgiving after communion, consists of three petitions, like the present Little Litany. The most ancient formula of the Little Litany, apparently, began with **"Help us"** (*Zastupi* / *Antilabou*) (see Introductory Chapter, p. 377; cf. at the Vespers of Pentecost).

## Actions of the Clergy at the Vesperal Kathisma

Directing the priest to pronounce the Great Litany and the first two Little Litanies at Great Vespers, the Typikon assigns the final Little Litany at the Kathisma, before **"Lord, I have cried"**, to be pronounced by the deacon, perhaps as a token of its connection with **"Lord, I have cried"**, from which begins the participation of the deacon in the celebration of Vespers. "During the singing of the 3rd antiphon the deacon departs, and having made a bow unto the superior (asking thereby his blessing for participation in the service), enters the sanctuary; with him enters also the priest on duty; the deacon, taking his sticharion and orarion, receives a blessing from the priest and, kissing his right hand, vests in the sticharion according unto custom."

All manuscripts of the Jerusalem Typikon assign the Little Litanies, like the Great Litany, unto the deacon. The prescription of the present Typikon is taken from the *Diataxis* of Patriarch Philotheus, where this is treated in greater detail:

"It is necessary to know that if the deacon does not come forth (*phorēsē* [does not vest]) at the beginning of Vespers, he vests during the singing of the 3rd antiphon, if there be a stichology; yet if there be none, during the singing of the Introductory Psalm, as hath been said. And he vests in this manner: he makes a bow unto the superior and enters by the side doors into the holy sanctuary; here he finds the priest standing, for he entered earlier; and having taken his sticharion, the deacon approaches the priest and says: 'Bless, *(Orig. p. 538)* Master, the sticharion with the orarion'; and the priest blesses it, saying: **'Blessed is our God always, now and ever and unto ages of ages.'** And having vested in this manner therein, the deacon, upon the conclusion of the stichology,

goes forth and stands in the customary place and says the Little Litany" [^2156].

## Part One of Great Vespers in Historical Development

Both by its entire structure and by the solemnity of its execution, the beginning of Great Vespers strongly resembles Chanted Vespers, especially in its most ancient form, traces whereof, nevertheless, very few remain in its monuments of the 14th–16th centuries. Judging by these monuments, Vespers began with three antiphons (see Introductory Chapter, p. 377), each consisting of a single psalm sung throughout antiphonally with the refrain **"Alleluia"** or **"Glory to Thee, O God"**; these psalms, in all likelihood, were the 85th, at present the third psalm of the Ninth Hour; thereupon the 92nd Psalm, **"The Lord is King"** (*Gospod' votsarisya*), which hath at present provided the prokeimenon for Vespers; and finally—the 67th, **"Let God arise"** (*Da voskresnet Bog*) (see Introductory Chapter, p. 341), at present assigned only unto Paschal Vespers and Matins. Each antiphon had a priestly prayer and a Little Litany; whereas a lengthy litany, whether Great or Augmented, was, in all likelihood, uttered for the first time after the Entrance (see Introductory Chapter, p. 377). Behold, probably, that heritage which the Holy Sepulchre and Studite Typika received as the opening of Vespers (see Introductory Chapter, p. 357), and which they were obliged to rework in order to adapt it unto monastic divine worship, with its obligatory chanting through of the entire Psalter within a given period (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 189–190, 295). The result of such adaptation was the substitution, in place of the fixed psalms for the opening antiphons of Sunday Vespers, of the first section of the Psalter, while preserving both the former structure for the beginning of Vespers and the manner of chanting the psalms. And in place of the fixed "Sunday" psalms removed from the beginning of Vespers, there was introduced the fixed "evening" 103rd Psalm.

# "LORD, I HAVE CRIED"

After the Kathisma the Old Testament chants of Vespers are replaced by distinctively Christian ones: psalms give way unto stichera, and at the same time the fixed melodies of Vespers—for the Introductory Psalm and **"Blessed is the man"**—are replaced by alternating melodies, the tone of the Octoechos or the Menaion (in the strict sense this takes place when from the First Kathisma only its 1st antiphon is sung; if the entire Kathisma is sung, then already at its 2nd and 3rd antiphons the tone of the Octoechos begins). In view of this, upon the conclusion of the Kathisma with its litanies, the canonarch, whose duty it is to begin the most important chants, loudly announces this tone, namely the tone of the first stichera at **"Lord, I have cried"**; consequently, on Sunday the *(Orig. p. 539)* recurring approaching tone of the Octoechos, and on feast days the tone of the first stichera from the Menaion, announcing it in the form: "The tone is such-and-such."

The 13th-century Georgian manuscript of the Jerusalem Typikon and Slavic manuscripts do not mention such an announcement of the tone by the canonarch; yet according unto Greek manuscripts: "The monk appointed to canonarch, upon the conclusion of the stichology, proclaims the tone" [^2157].

## The Tones

Of the tones, whose application unto the service begins from this moment of the Vigil, there are eight, and each of them possesses its own musical coloring, difficult to define in words, just as in general the definition (interpretation) of this or that character of music is difficult. And this is all the more so in that the melodies themselves for a tone are not something immutably fixed, but have undergone and continue to undergo diverse modifications. Moreover, the melodies of one and the same tone are not identical for stichera, troparia, canons, and prokeimena. Nevertheless, the musical foundation across these diverse variations of a tone is even now one and the same, and hath been preserved more or less firmly throughout the centuries. Therefore a certain characterization of the tones is possible. Such a characterization was compiled by medieval Western theorists of church music for the Roman Catholic modes, and by virtue of the homogeneity of the musical structure of the Octoechos both among us and in the Western Church, founded upon the properties of the ancient Greek tetrachord, it is applicable unto our tones as well.

— The First Tone is simpler, more grave, and more majestic than the others; the Dorian mode, lying at its foundation, Plato and (pseudo-)Aristotle prefer unto others; it was employed for

=== LEAF p646 ===
solemn, resounding hymns directed toward appeasing the anger of the deity, especially for hymns unto Apollo (*paean* / *paian* [Gr. ποαάν]) and at sacrificial libations (*spondē*); this musical mode was considered a guide unto a virtuous life; it was the favorite melody of the Arcadians and Lacedaemonians (according unto Polybius); according unto Athenaeus, it was practiced in the education of youths; Cassiodorus calls it the giver of chastity and producer of purity; the Pythagoreans animated themselves with it in the early morning for labor; Ptolemy, who assigns the eight melodies unto the eight heavenly spheres, compares the Dorian mode unto the sun: just as the sun dries up moisture and dispels darkness, so this mode dispels phlegm, sloth, sluggishness, sleep, sorrow, and confusion; it befitteth renowned men of rich gifts; it is sublime, devoid of luxury and licentiousness.

— At the foundation of the Second Tone lies the Lydian mode, which corresponds unto the sanguine temperament; it engenders meekness, benevolence, and pleasantness; it is delightful, breathes of pleasure, and was employed in dances; according unto Cassiodorus, it drives away superfluous anxieties and despair, and consoles the sorrowful; it is correlated with Jupiter.

— The Phrygian mode, lying at the foundation of the Third Tone, is compared unto the sea; it is stormy, rousing, provoking indignation; men were inspired by it unto battle; it is forceful and sharp, deeply piercing the soul; among poetic meters the anapest corresponds unto it; it contains large leaps in its melodic movement *(Orig. p. 540)* and suits the taste of impetuous, vehement, wrathful cholerics; it seeks harsh, martial words; Plato and Aristotle preferred it together with the Dorian mode above others, yet forbade its use unto youths in view of its passionate character.

— At the foundation of the Fourth Tone lies the Mixolydian mode, so named because it hath something in common with the Lydian mode (Tone 2); others call it Locrian (Julius Pollux); it exerts a twofold effect—now exciting unto joy, now suddenly inspiring sorrow; it was used by the ancients in tragedy because it inspired grief while having at the same time a certain measure of excitement and pleasantness; among the Pythagoreans it designated the harmony of the 7th and final heavenly sphere; by its wholeness and coherence it imparts a singular peace unto the soul; it is superior unto other modes by its gentle and pleasing modulations; unto those weighed down by worldly cares it inspires a longing for the heavenly, awakens the dulled mind, and more than other modes expresses the operation upon us of the grace of the Holy Spirit. — The other four tones are called "indirect" or "plagal" (*plagioi*) of the first four, representing toward them a certain

=== LEAF p647 ===
antithesis (a negative pole).

Thus, the Fifth Tone hath at its foundation the Hypodorian mode, which, in contrast unto the Dorian, induced as it were slumber, for which reason the Pythagoreans employed it in the evening to assuage cares; it answers the taste of the listless, sorrowful, and unfortunate; it calms emotional agitations; Aristotle calls it majestic, steadfast, and grave, and compares its influence with the action upon the Earth of the Moon, the lowest planet, influencing humidity; it is a suppliant, somewhat weeping melody, full of entreaty for deliverance from calamity, disgrace, and bondage; it contains in itself something analogous unto paleness.

— The Sixth Tone hath at its foundation the Hypolydian mode, expressing feelings of piety, devotion, humanity, love, sorrow, and weeping; yet grief here is not suppliant, as in the preceding mode, but flows from love, devotion, and attachment (it was employed in prayers unto Venus); it stirred the soul and served as the language of the emotions; it was distinguished by a flowing and coherent progression of sounds.

— The Seventh Tone hath at its foundation the Hypophrygian mode, gentle, touching, pacifying, suppressing biliousness, soothing; unto it was ascribed even a certain flattery and deceptiveness, and it was suited unto Mercury; with it the Cretans and Lacedaemonians sounded the retreat from battle; it is suited unto words expressing exhortation, caress, and persuasion.

— The Hypomixolydian mode, corresponding unto the Eighth Tone, artificially added in order not to deprive the Mixolydian mode of its plagal counterpart, is likened unto the vault of heaven with its diversity of stars; it doth not express any single emotion, but is indifferent unto them; that which cannot be expressed by the other modes can be conveyed unto this one, in which respect it is akin unto Tone 1; it is leisurely pleasant and represents the immovability of future glory; it suits modest men endowed with a capacity for deep contemplation; it suits words concerning lofty matters, concerning the heavenly realm, and prayers for blessedness [^2158]; yet in general it is a melody worthy of its (final) *(Orig. p. 541)* place.

In the church tones the objectionable peculiarities of the Greek melodies found mitigation and transformation, being purified of everything sinful; only the general coloration remained the same. — As regards the number of the tones, eight could have been selected for it likewise on account of the symbolism of this number. It is regarded, according unto St. Gregory the Theologian and the writer calling himself Dionysius the Areopagite, as a symbol of eternity [^2159]. St. John of Damascus saw in the number seven the entirety of the present age, and in the number eight—the future state after

=== LEAF p648 ===
the resurrection of the dead. "The divine worship of the Church, divided into or subsumed under the number eight, expresses in itself the thought that Christian life in time and in eternity consists in the perpetual glorification of the Lord, in eternal service unto Him" [^2160].

Concerning the adoption by the Church of Greek melodies for divine worship, witnesses exist already from the 3rd century (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 107–108); in the 4th–5th centuries there were no more than four melodies (ibid., pp. 160–161); unto Pope St. Gregory the Great (†604) in the West and St. John of Damascus (8th c.) in the East is attributed the introduction of the Octoechos [^2161] (ibid., pp. 334–335).

## The Psalms of "Lord, I Have Cried"

Under the title **"Lord, I have cried"** (*Kyrie ekekraxa* / *Gospodi vozzvakh*) are understood four psalms—headed by Ps. 140, **"Lord, I have cried"**; Ps. 141, **"With my voice unto the Lord have I cried"**; Ps. 129, **"Out of the depths have I cried unto Thee, O Lord"**; and Ps. 116, **"O praise the Lord, all ye nations"**. Of these psalms Ps. 140 is most suited unto the evening and its service, inasmuch as the singer therein entreats God to accept his prayer as an evening sacrifice; this petition, like the entire content of the psalm, shows that the psalm was sung by David, unto whom it is ascribed also in the Hebrew Bible, far from the Temple during one of his persecutions, for which reason the psalm in general contains a prayer for deliverance from enemies for the sake of the righteousness of the persecuted one. Homogeneous with it in content and circumstances of origin, Ps. 141 ("A contemplation of David; a prayer when he was in the cave") develops and intensifies this prayer (there: "Lord, I have cried unto Thee"—here: "with my voice unto the Lord have I cried", "I will pour out my supplication before Him"). The prayer of Ps. 141, in turn, is intensified in Ps. 129 (there: "with my voice... have I cried", here: "out of the depths have I cried", not simply "hear me", as in Ps. 140, or "attend unto my supplication", as in Ps. 141, but: "let Thine ears be attentive"). Unto Ps. 129 is joined the brief, two-verse Ps. 116, developing the final thought of Ps. 129: "For with the Lord there is mercy...": "For His mercy is confirmed upon us...". All these psalms, not to mention that they contain the thought of the replacement of the Old Testament sacrifice by a spiritual one, are applicable unto the Savior suffering and triumphing over His enemies, especially certain expressions, such as: **"Bring my soul out of prison"** (the grave and Hades)—the verse with which so fittingly begins the singing of the Sunday stichera; **"The righteous shall wait for me, until Thou shalt recompense me"** (the righteous *(Orig. p. 542)* in Hades waited to see how the work of Christ would end); **"I looked upon my right hand, and beheld, and there was none that would know me"** (the scattering of the Apostles during the sufferings of the Savior); **"Like a clod of earth is broken upon the ground"** (the rupture of the earth's crust during

=== LEAF p649 ===
the earthquake).

Concerning the use of Ps. 140 at Vespers St. John Chrysostom speaks already (Introductory Chapter, p. 145). This psalm, in all likelihood, the *Apostolic Constitutions* (4th–5th c.) understand under the name of the "lucernarium" (*epilychnios*) psalm, beginning Vespers and exhausting its Old Testament material (ibid., p. 147). In the "Canon of Antiphons of the Psalms" attributed unto Patriarch Anthimus of Constantinople (6th c.), this psalm and the 85th have the antiphon: *kata lychnēs* ("from the lampstand") and together with the 12th psalm are called *lychnakoi*, as likewise in the 5th-century Alexandrian Codex of the Bible [^2162]. It is mentioned at Vespers by the most ancient Typika of the Great Church of Constantinople, and is present at Vespers in all Eastern rites and in all non-Orthodox rites of divine worship, excepting the Western, whereof the Roman rite hath all these psalms at Vespers once a week, the Ambrosian rite likewise, and on feast days always Ps. 129 (see Introductory Chapter, pp. 300, 301, 303). When there was at Vespers neither the 103rd Psalm nor the chanted antiphons, Ps. 140 was situated at its beginning; hence, perhaps, the assignment unto it of the censing which customarily began the service.

## The Initial Refrain

In view of the exceptional position which the psalms of **"Lord, I have cried"** occupy at Vespers, an especially solemn execution is assigned unto them. They are sung, similarly unto Ps. 103 and certain others, with special refrains, namely—at first with the refrain **"Hear me, O Lord"**, expressing the general thought of the psalm and composed from its words. This refrain is attached unto the verses of the psalm in that distinctive manner in which refrains are attached unto the so-called "antiphons" at the Liturgy. Namely—unto the first verse of the psalm the refrain is attached twice: the first time unto the incomplete verse, the second time unto the entire verse, whereby the opening words of the psalm are sung twice. Unto the second verse the refrain is attached once at the conclusion of the verse. The impression is produced that the chanter from fullness of feeling is as it were unable to utter the entire verse at once, and this is done by the other choir, beginning again the same verse from the start. For example, in the antiphons for Pascha from Ps. 65, instead of singing the 1st verse of this psalm with the refrain **"By the prayers of the Theotokos..."** in the following manner: "Shout with joy unto the Lord, all the earth; chant now unto His name, give glory unto His praise. By the prayers of the Theotokos, O Savior, save us", it is sung thus: "Shout with joy unto the Lord, all the earth. By the prayers of the Theotokos, O Savior, save us. And again (the second choir): Shout with joy unto the Lord, all the earth, (yet now with the addition of the remaining

=== LEAF p650 ===
words of the verse:) chant now unto His name, give glory unto His praise. By the prayers of the Theotokos, O Savior, save us." In the same manner there is attached unto Ps. 140 its refrain **"Hear me, O Lord"**. There is sung: **"Lord, I have cried** *(Orig. p. 543)* **unto Thee, hear me"**, and the refrain: **"Hear me, O Lord"**; thereupon the 1st verse in full with its opening words already sung: **"Lord, I have cried unto Thee, hear me"**, etc., and the refrain: **"Hear me, O Lord"**; thereupon the 2nd verse now in a single delivery: **"Let my prayer be set forth..."**, etc., and the refrain **"Hear me, O Lord"**. The subsequent verses of the psalm, as also the subsequent psalms, are sung without a refrain.

The Studite-Alexian Typikon, certain manuscript copies of the Jerusalem Typikon—for example, Moscow Synodal Library No. 336/388 of the 16th century—and the Old Believer Typikon indicate the following refrains unto the psalms of **"Lord, I have cried"**: unto Ps. 140: **"Hear us, O Lord"** (according unto the MS of the Moscow Synodal Library: **"me"**); unto Ps. 141: **"I cried unto Thee, hear [me]"** (*Vozvakh Ti uslyshi*) (Studite according unto the Typography copy), or (Synodal MS) **"I cried unto Thee, hear me"**, or **"I cried unto Thee, O Lord, save me"** (Jerusalem), or the same without **"O Lord"** (Old Believer); unto Ps. 129: **"O God, Savior, have mercy"** (Studite Typography copy) or **"O Christ Savior, have mercy upon us"** (the rest) [^2163]. The second refrain is from Ps. 140:1 and represents an intensification of the first. The third refrain is a transition unto the stichera and, perhaps, the embryo thereof (so frequently do stichera end).

## Stichera

Upon the psalms of **"Lord, I have cried"** the Old Testament chant material of Vespers gradually passes into Christian: unto the final verses of these psalms church hymns are attached, and this alternate chanting signifies that the Old Testament accords with the New, according unto the word of Christ: "I came not to destroy the law, but to fulfill it" [^2164]. The church hymns attached in this manner unto the verses of the psalms received therefrom the designation of stichera—a genre of church hymns which by the Typikon is ranked below other genres of church hymns, such as the troparion, kontakion, exapostilarion (*svitilen*), hypakoē, and kathisma-hymn (*sidalen*), just as the chant melody of stichera is simpler than the melody of all the latter.

By stichera (*ta stichēra*, understanding *biblia*) among the Holy Fathers are not infrequently designated the poetic and didactic books of the Old Testament on account of their poetic meter [^2165]. In Greek Horologia so are called at times the Old Testament canticles—of Moses, Anna, and others.
