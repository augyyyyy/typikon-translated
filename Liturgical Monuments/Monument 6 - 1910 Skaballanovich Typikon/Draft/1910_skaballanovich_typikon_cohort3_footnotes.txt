The musical term *Selah* is likewise interpreted as indicating antiphonal chanting. It is thought that antiphonal singing was borrowed by the Hebrews from Egypt, where choral songs were divided between priests and people, between men and women, between children and adults. Such singing accompanied the worship of the golden calf: descending the mountain and hearing the tumult, Moses says: "It is not the voice of them that shout for mastery, neither is it the voice of them that cry for being overcome, but the noise of them that sing do I hear" (Exodus 32:18); more literally from the Hebrew: "It is not the alternating voice [*'anot*—answering] of victors, nor the alternating voice of the vanquished, but alternating voices do I hear." Philo perceives the first instance of antiphonal chanting in the Canticle of Moses upon the crossing of the Red Sea. How accurate is this? The execution of this canticle is thus described in Exodus: "Then sang Moses and the children of Israel this song unto the Lord, and spake, saying: I will sing unto the Lord, for He hath triumphed gloriously..."; after the exposition of the canticle it is added: "And Miriam the prophetess, the sister of Aaron, took a timbrel [tambourine, drum, a dance musical instrument] in her hand, and all the women went out after her with timbrels and with dances [Heb. *mecholot*, choral dances, Greek *χορῶν*]; and Miriam answered them: Sing ye to the Lord, for He hath triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider hath He thrown into the sea" (Exodus 14:31; 15:1, 20–21).
