# THE BEGINNING OF MATINS (*Nachalo utreni*)

## Bell-Chime for Matins (*Zvon k utrene*)

Now begins the second and most important part of the Vigil—Matins [*utrenya*]. Its beginning is marked by a *peal of bells* [*zvonom*].

"After the Great Reading of the Acts of the Holy Apostles (or of the Epistles), the paraecclesiarch goes forth and strikes the great bell and the other bells."

It is not specified that he should strike first upon the one great bell and then upon all, as before the beginning of the Vigil, because this is not the blagovest (the initial tolling), which requires a special duration, but merely a "signal" [*znamenie*] of the beginning of Matins. Its duration is therefore completely unspecified.

### According to Ancient Typika (*Po drevnim ustavam*)

According unto the ancient recensions of the Jerusalem Typikon, at the beginning of Matins at the Vigil there was prescribed not only a bell-peal, but also a censing, and according unto certain Slavic copies of the Typikon, an exclamation as well; but the ringing was only on one large bell or semantron [*bilo*].

Georgian and Greek manuscripts and Greek printed editions:
"The candle-lighter [*kandilovzhigatel'*], having prepared the censer, enters and strikes the great [instrument]; and the priest censes."

Slavic manuscripts of the 14th century (Moscow Synodal Library Nos. 328/383 and 329/384):
"The kandilaptis makes a prostration unto the superior or unto his place, and departing, rings. Upon the completion thereof, the priest makes a prostration unto the superior, and having entered into the altar, takes the censer, places incense therein, and says the prayer of the censer; then he marks the Holy Table with the sign of the Cross with the censer, and proclaims: Blessed is Christ (No. 329 without 'Christ') our God always..."

Slavic manuscripts of the 15th and 16th centuries (for example, No. 678/386 and the Kyiv Theological Academy Museum) speak neither of censing nor of an exclamation, but concerning the ringing they add: "on the great wood and on the others, if there be any"; and so likewise...

## Initial Versicles of Matins (*Nachal'nye stikhi utreni*)

Matins of the Vigil begins with the angelic hymn at the Nativity of the Savior: **"Glory to God in the highest"** (Lk. 2:14), just as in a more expanded form it also concludes with it; this is, of course, in view of the fact that with the event *(Orig. p. 616)* that laid the foundation of our salvation—and which, moreover, occurred by night—and with its glorification it is natural to begin the cycle of daily services. In view of the exceptional importance of this brief hymn, which represents not a human composition, it is repeated three times in honor of the Holy Trinity. Having prefixed such a lofty hymn, the worshippers venture to begin their own praise also, and in the feeling of their own inadequacy for it, they ask for Divine assistance—again not in their own words, but in God-inspired words, though having already proceeded from human lips—with the verse of Psalm 50:17 [LXX; Masoretic 51:15]: **"O Lord, Thou shalt open my lips"** (Heb. *śəp̄ātay*, Gr. *χείλη*, lips) **"and my mouth"** (Heb. *pî*, Gr. *στόμα*, mouth) **"shall declare Thy praise."** The prayer here is expressed in the future tense of confidence. The verse was especially suited unto that era when the divine service was improvised. It is repeated twice, as it were for each choir. Constituting the beginning of the service, both versicles, however, must stand in the closest connection with its first section, which in liturgical services is always the psalms. Psalms in this case also begin the service, whereas the indicated versicles are regarded only as their initial refrain. This is expressed in that the versicles in execution are not separated from the Six Psalms that follow them, being recited in the same manner as the Six Psalms, and by the same person (see below).

### Their History (*Istoriya ikh*)

**"Glory to God in the highest"** was included from antiquity in the composition of the Liturgy. The Liturgy of the *Apostolic Constitutions* has this verse immediately before the Communion itself; our Liturgy and the Nestorian Liturgy attributed unto the Apostle Thaddaeus have it at the very beginning; the Roman Liturgy likewise has it in the opening part and in the form of the first section of our Great Doxology. Unto Matins the verse passed, perhaps, under the influence of the transfer of the Liturgy unto the morning and its union with Matins. At Matins it is mentioned in the oldest manuscript copies of the Jerusalem Typikon, but at weekday Matins, and not at Sunday Matins, where the Six Psalms is directly indicated [^2387]. — With the verse **"O Lord, Thou shalt open my lips,"** recited thrice,...

=== LEAF p743 ===
...St. Benedict prescribes beginning Matins; according unto the observation of the commentators on Benedict, this verse lifted the obligatory nighttime silence, for which reason it was customary with it to sign the mouth with the sign of the Cross [^2388]. And the present-day Roman Matins begins with it. With it likewise begins the Armenian Midnight Office (= Matins), with its recitation also thrice (Introductory Chapter, 299). The Lyonese Liturgy has it before the Gospel [^2389]. At Jerusalem Matins it appears in manuscripts simultaneously with the first verse, and both at once in the present manner of execution—that is, with the recitation of the first thrice and of the second twice—and in immediate connection with the Six Psalms, from which they were consequently never separated in manner of execution, that is, they were chanted in the same manner as the Six Psalms (see below).

## The Plan of Matins (*Plan utreni*)

Our Matins, like the Roman Catholic service, visibly divides into four parts corresponding unto the four "watches" of the night, since the prototype of Matins was the ancient all-night vigil (*vigilia*), which on the eve of feasts occupied the entire night. These parts are:
1) The Six Psalms with the Great Ektenia and **"God is the Lord"**;
2) The Kathismata with the Blameless (*Amomoi*) or the Polyeleos and the Gospel;
3) The Canon;
4) The Praises (*Ainoi*) with the Doxology and the litanies.

### Part 1 of Matins (*1-ya chast' utreni*)

The first part of Matins, like the second, gradually elevates the worshippers from a sorrowful and penitential disposition—fundamental for a Christian—unto joy in God. Such an ascent in the first part of Matins has three stages: the Six Psalms, the Ektenia, and **"God is the Lord."** From a certain grief wherewith the first is suffused, the second—the Ektenia—transfers us into prayer of a supplicatory character, the hope aroused by which is already close unto the triumph breathing in **"God is the Lord."**

# THE SIX PSALMS (*Shestopsalmie* — *Hexapsalmos*)

This part opens—and with it, consequently, the whole of Matins—with the system of psalms known under the name of the Six Psalms (Gr. *ἑξάψαλμος*). Thus the introductory psalms at this service, in accordance with its length and its primary place among the daily services, are twice the normally accepted number of them for each service—3. The composition of the Six Psalms comprises:
— Ps. 3 **"Lord, why are they increased that trouble me?"** [*Gospodi, chto sya umnozhisha*],
— Ps. 37 [LXX; Masoretic 38] **"O Lord, rebuke me not in Thine anger"** [*Gospodi da ne yarostiyu*],
— Ps. 62 [LXX; Masoretic 63] **"O God, my God, unto Thee do I rise early"** [*Bozhe, Bozhe moy, k Tebe utrenyuyu*],
— Ps. 87 [LXX; Masoretic 88] **"O Lord God of my salvation"** [*Gospodi Bozhe spaseniya moyego*],
— Ps. 102 [LXX; Masoretic 103] **"Bless the Lord, O my soul"** [*Blagoslovi dushe moya Gospoda*],
— Ps. 142 [LXX; Masoretic 143] **"Lord, hear my prayer"** [*Gospodi uslyshi molitvu moyu*].

The psalms were selected, doubtless not without intention, from different parts of the Psalter evenly; by this they represent it in its entirety, as if to say that, properly speaking, one ought to recite it in its entirety, as the ascetics were wont to do by night. The psalms were chosen of homogeneous content and tone, predominant in the Psalter: namely, all of them depict the persecution of the righteous man by enemies and his steadfast hope in God, which only grows (cf. Ps. 3:8; 62:6 [LXX; Masoretic 3:7; 63:5]) with the increase of persecutions (cf. Ps. 3:2, 3; 37:7, 8 et seq.; 87:4, 5 [LXX; Masoretic 3:1, 2; 38:6, 7; 88:3, 4]) and in the end reaches jubilant resting in God (Ps. 102 [LXX; Masoretic 103]). All these psalms are superscribed with the name of David (in the Hebrew Bible as well), with the exception of 87, which is inscribed "unto the sons of Korah," and were sung by him, of course, during the persecution on the part of Saul (perhaps Ps. 62 [LXX; Masoretic 63]) or Absalom (Ps. 3; 142 [LXX; Masoretic 143]), reflecting in themselves the spiritual growth of the singer amidst these tribulations. Out of the multitude of psalms of similar content, precisely these were selected here also because in certain passages they have in view the night and morning (Ps. 3:6 [LXX; Masoretic 3:5]: **"I laid me down and slept; I awaked"**; Ps. 37:7 [LXX; Masoretic 38:6]: **"I went mourning all the day long,"** v. 13 [Masoretic 38:12]: **"imagined deceits all the day long"**; Ps. 62:1 [LXX; Masoretic 63:1]: **"unto Thee do I rise early,"** v. 7 [Masoretic 63:6]: **"I remembered Thee upon my bed, and meditated on Thee in the morning"**; *(Orig. p. 618)* Ps. 87:2 [LXX; Masoretic 88:1]: **"I have cried day and night before Thee,"** v. 10 [Masoretic 88:9]: **"I have stretched forth my hands unto Thee all the day,"** vv. 13, 14 [Masoretic 88:12, 13]: **"Shall Thy wonders be known in the dark? — But unto Thee have I cried, O Lord, and in the morning shall my prayer prevent Thee"**; Ps. 102:15 [LXX; Masoretic 103:15]: **"his days are as grass, as a flower of the field"**; Ps. 142:8 [LXX; Masoretic 143:8]: **"Cause me to hear Thy lovingkindness in the morning"**). Mournful psalms alternate with joyful ones, wherein for the former even-numbered places were naturally chosen. The psalmody material of the Six Psalms is somewhat multiplied; this was done 1) in that at the end of each psalm there is repeated its most characteristic...

=== LEAF p745 ===
...or most suitable passage for the morning service, to the extent of 1/2 to 2 1/2 verses, namely: Ps. 3:6 [LXX; Masoretic 3:5]; Ps. 37:22–23 [LXX; Masoretic 38:21–22] (the final verses); Ps. 62:7b–9 [LXX; Masoretic 63:6b–8]; Ps. 87:2–3 [LXX; Masoretic 88:1–2] (the opening verses); Ps. 102:22b [LXX; Masoretic 103:22b]; Ps. 142:1b–2a, 10b [LXX; Masoretic 143:1b–2a, 10b]. At the conclusion of the final psalm, the first of its two repeated passages is even pronounced twice (**"Hear me, O Lord, in Thy righteousness..."**). 2) Unto the psalmody material of the Six Psalms is appended the mixed Christian-Old Testament doxology customary in such cases (see above, pp. 468–469): **"Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, both now and ever, and unto ages of ages. Amen. Alleluia, alleluia, alleluia, glory to Thee, O God** *(thrice)*; **Lord, have mercy** *(thrice)*; **Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit, both now and ever, and unto ages of ages. Amen,"** which serves as the conclusion unto each triad of psalms, wherein at the second triad "Lord, have mercy" and the second "Glory... both now and ever..." are not said, in view of the immediately following ektenia, wherein these petitions are provided in an amplified measure.

## Its Origin (*Proiskhozhdenie ego*)

Prior unto the appearance of monastic typika, Matins in respect unto the quantity of psalms did not differ from Vespers: both in the *Apostolic Constitutions* have one psalm each, Matins having the 62nd (Introductory Chapter, pp. 135, 137); the same psalm is indicated for it in the treatise *On Virginity* [*De virginitate*] of Pseudo-Athanasius (4th–5th century) [^2390]. Under the influence of the ascetics who chanted the entire Psalter during the night, monasteries introduced the singing at Matins of 75, 60, 50, 30, 20, and 18 psalms; the Rule of Benedict and other Western rules reduced this number unto 12, basing themselves, doubtless, upon the very widespread ancient custom of chanting 12 psalms at a time, wherein the Rule of Benedict divided them into two groups of six, prefixing unto them Psalms 3 and 94; other rules (Western) reduced this number still further—unto six, five, and four psalms (Introductory Chapter, 189–190, 216, 301–313). The latter found application also in the Armenian Matins, wherein all the psalms of its 4-psalm group coincide with ours: 3, 62, 102, 142 (ibid., 299). And Choral Matins under Symeon of Thessalonica hath at the beginning Psalms 3, 62, and 133 (ibid., 345). The most ancient mentions of our Six Psalms are found in the well-known description of Sinai Matins of the 7th century, and in the *Hypotyposis* and *Diatyposis* (ibid., 295, 359).

## Manner of Execution (*Sposob ispolneniya*)

The Six Psalms with the verses preceding it—**"Glory to God in the highest"** and **"O Lord, Thou shalt open my lips"**—as the most important part of the service, the Typikon appoints to be read by the superior.

=== LEAF p746 ===
"And when the superior and all the brethren have arisen (from the reading), *(Orig. p. 619)* he begins: Amen (the response unto the concluding doxology of the reading?), Glory to God in the highest... (thrice); then he says: O Lord, Thou shalt open my lips... (twice). And he recites the Six Psalms in a quiet and gentle voice."

A certain lack of clarity in the phrasing here as unto who "begins" and "recites" the Six Psalms (perhaps intentional, = impersonal) is resolved in Chapters 7 and 9 of the Typikon and in the order of the first Monday of Great Lent, where it is directly stated: "and the superior says: Glory to God in the highest," etc.; and in the note unto Chapter 7 ("behold") in the enumeration of what is read by the superior, the Six Psalms is named. Thus, after a whole torrent of triumphant hymns, the Vigil now passes into a quiet, prayerful tone, which is so fitting for the beginning of a new service. This is the most minor-key part of the Vigil. Yet it is remarkable that here there are none of those rubrical remarks concerning a penitential disposition during the reading of the Six Psalms that are given in the typikon of weekday Matins (Chapter 9) and still more in the order of Great Lenten Matins (Monday of the First Week of the Forty Days).

## Execution in Antiquity (*Ispolnenie v drevnosti*)

Thanks unto the Six Psalms, as subsequently unto the kathismata, the beginning of our Matins in its tone corresponds fully unto the character of all ancient chanting, which was, like the Jewish synagogal singing, predominantly recitative (Introductory Chapter, 20, 155). In general, "in the early centuries, when the Blood of Christ shed for them still boiled in the veins of the faithful, the psalms were chanted with so moderate an inflection of the voice that the singing was more like reading than singing. A remnant of such chanting in the Western Church is singing *in directum*, which at times replaces antiphonal singing with voice modulations." For an especially long time such a character was retained by the chanting at the nocturnal service: according unto the rules of Isidore of Seville, the Nocturns were read, while Matins (*Laudes*) was sung, "that the delight of diversity might banish weariness" [^2391]. Thus, the Six Psalms in manner of execution is most faithful unto antiquity. However, such chanting for the Six Psalms is presupposed, as we shall see, only by the oldest Studite typika; whereas the Jerusalem typika initially, it appears, desired a more solemn chanting for it. In favor of this speaks the repetition, preserved to this day, of the most important passage from each psalm at its conclusion; this repetition is completely analogous unto the antiphon in Roman Catholic worship, with this difference, however, that the antiphon there not only concludes...

# THE MORNING PRAYERS (*Utrennie molitvy* — *Euchai Orthrinai*)

Not being content with their own prayer in the words of the Six Psalms, the faithful send unto prayer a grace-endowed intercessor for themselves—reminding one of the heavenly Intercessor for us before God—in the person of the priest, who *(Orig. p. 621)* during the Six Psalms, standing before the Royal Doors, offers our prayers in mysterious, silent colloquy with God. These secret prayers of the priest at Matins, in accordance with the greater length and importance of this service in comparison with Vespers, are more numerous than at the latter; yet their number is determined, as at Vespers, by the symbolic number 12, which rivals 7 in sacred significance. Most immediately, this number instills the thought that we would desire to spend in prayer all the hours of the night—a time especially conducive unto prayer and convenient for it—recalling that Matins arose out of the original all-night vigil (*vigilia*) on the eves of feasts.

Embracing all the needs of a Christian entering upon a new day, the morning prayers—more precisely, the prayers of Matins (Gr. *εὐχαὶ τοῦ ὄρθρου*)—are at the same time adapted unto the various parts of Matins, having been originally, like the evening prayers, intended for reading at different points of the service.

The 1st prayer, being introductory, thanking God for our awakening and for the present service, asks for Divine assistance in general and for the worthy celebration of divine worship; it concludes with the exclamation of the Great Ektenia. The 2nd and 3rd prayers, beginning with the words of the Canticle of the Prophet Isaiah, correspond in content unto this canticle, with whose verses "Alleluia" is sung at Lenten Matins (in place of **"God is the Lord"**).

More specifically, the 2nd prayer asks for instruction in working righteousness and holiness in the fear of God, that God may remember and hear all who pray with the priest by name, save them, bless and sanctify them, and grant peace unto the world, unto the Church, unto the priest, unto the sovereign, and unto all people (cf. the Great Ektenia and the Prayer behind the Ambo); the doxology (= exclamation) glorifies the name of God.

The 3rd prayer is for our instruction in God's righteousness, and for inner illumination (following the outer illumination of the preceding prayer) through the preservation within us of the seal of the Holy Spirit, which will bring in its train righteous action as well as a joyous forthcoming day; exclamation of the 1st Small Ektenia.

The 4th prayer asks that He accept our thanksgiving (offered hitherto) for sleep and awakening and vouchsafe us eternal blessings, wherein the prayer steps beyond the boundaries of those present and...

=== LEAF p750 ===
...embraces all the brethren on land, on sea, and everywhere; the exclamation (= at the morning Supplicatory Ektenia) glorifies the mercy of God.

The 5th prayer asks for the acceptance of our morning prayers (as the preceding prayer asked for the acceptance of morning thanksgiving), as incense, and that He bypass not with His help any one of those who keep vigil and sing; the exclamation (= of the ektenia after Ode 3 of the Canon) concludes the general glorification of God and begins a new series of exclamations.

The 6th prayer gives thanks already for the benefits of our whole life, and more specifically again for the past night, and asks that God vouchsafe us to sing and pray unto Him unceasingly, and remember "them that cry unto Him in the night" (consequently, those especially in need of help), crushing their invisible enemies; the exclamation (= after Ode 6 of the Canon) concludes the general glorification of the Kingdom and salvation of God.

The 7th prayer, repeating the former petitions for the acceptance of thanksgiving and instruction in His statutes, asks for instruction in worthy prayer for us and for the forgiveness of every sin; the exclamation "For Thine is the might, and Thine is the kingdom..." develops the first thought of the preceding exclamation and prepares for the next. *(Orig. p. 622)*

The 8th prayer develops the former petition for the acceptance of our prayer, enumerating its forms as well: supplications, confessions (perhaps an indication of the polyeleos psalms), nocturnal services—and asks for the granting of faith, hope, and love (the positive aspect of the negative good requested in the previous prayer—the forgiveness of sins), for the blessing of all our undertakings in the day now drawing nigh and the attainment thereof; the exclamation—with the glorification of the name and Kingdom of God, = at the Polyeleos.

The 9th prayer is for the worthy hearing of the Gospel, with the glorification of God as our illumination (having in view also the approaching dawn); it is identical with the Liturgy prayer.

The 10th prayer—of Psalm 50 [LXX; Masoretic 51]—adapts its petitions unto Christian needs (for example: "vouchsafe that there may be offered unto Thee a sacrifice of righteousness and an oblation upon Thy holy altars"), with the glorification of the mercy of God (= exclamation before the Canon, the development of the second thought in the exclamation of the 5th prayer).

The 11th prayer—of the Psalms of Praise (*Ainoi*)—asks the Creator of the Angels with special tenderness of heart (*milisi deyem*), attained through all the prayers, to accept our doxology (an anticipation of the next prayer), offered together with all the creatures of God; the exclamation (= after Ode 9 of the Canon) unites our prayer unto the angelic praise.
