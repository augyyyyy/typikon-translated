[^1806]: The extent unto which this Typikon was implemented in the Athonite Iviron Lavra even prior unto the appearance there of a complete copy of it, under St. Euthymius, its second hegumen († 1012 AD), is evident from the life of the latter, compiled by his successor and translator of the described *Synaxarion*, Gregory Mtatsmindeli [the Hagiorite]. According unto the biographer, Euthymius stood in church without leaning upon a staff and without leaning against the wall. The brethren standing by the wall were likewise forbidden to lean against it. The younger brethren stood in the middle of the church in two or three rows, reverently, with hands lowered; when appointed, they sat upon their benches, which likewise stood in the middle of the church in two or three rows. Euthymius appointed two God-fearing *epitropoi*-overseers, one senior, and the other junior, subordinate unto the first. The junior *epitropos* was sent through the cells and brought unto church any brother who remained in his cell. If a brother did not arrive for the Six Psalms, Euthymius judged him according unto the rules of the father (Theodore) in the following manner: he did not punish one who labored much by day and had no time to sleep—his daytime labors were imputed unto him as an ascetic struggle of prayer. But those having leisure or performing petty tasks, as well as priests, deacons, and readers, he subjected unto such punishment: he compelled the younger to make 100 prostrations before the altar, while the elderly and infirm he deprived that day of wine and food. During the service, the senior *epitropos* stood at the doors and inquired of any departing brother concerning the reason for his exit; if no essential need was apparent, he did not permit him to leave; but in case of genuine necessity, he let him out only upon condition that he return immediately. In the event that the one who exited was late, he was subjected unto a rebuke; and if he was late a second time, on that day he was not admitted into the refectory, but was kept upon dry bread or sentenced unto 100 prostrations. Unto those laboring untiringly, the holy father permitted leaving the church after the Six Psalms to rest, but with the obligation to return by the second bell ringing. Unto the weak among the brethren, he allowed standing in the narthex and leaning upon a staff, and unto the sick even to sit. Younger brethren conversing, laughing, or taking each other by the hand were immediately reproved; and if caught a second time, they were reported unto the holy father, who judged them strictly. One guilty of quarreling or fighting was brought unto the cell of the holy father, who commanded him to lie upon the ground; the guilty brother, having made a bow, lay down; one of the brethren held him by the head, another by the feet, and a third struck him over his cassock with 30, 40, or 60 blows of a belt. If anyone interceded for the one being punished, he was subjected unto the very same punishment. Those punished, kneeling, bowed before the holy father and with tears in their eyes begged his forgiveness. Thereafter they lay upon the ground and remained in such a posture for two or three weeks (?). From time to time the holy father came unto them and comforted them in word and deed; unto the same he urged other monastic officials. The executor of the punishment likewise comforted the punished brother and presented him with a cassock, or a scapular, or some other necessary item. The punished regarded the punishment they endured as though they had suffered it from the hands of Christ Himself. Above all, the holy father took care that no one should give false testimony or witness in any matter without knowing its circumstances; whoever was convicted of this he expelled from the monastery. The blessed father provided the brethren with everything, down unto needle and thread, and thereby deprived all of an occasion to sell or acquire anything without his command; a brother could not transfer his property unto another without the consent of the holy father. If anyone transgressed the will of the holy father and acquired clothing or anything valuable, he strictly rebuked him. We have seen more than once how he burned in the monastic enclosure a scapular, clothing, or a klobuk (*koukoulion*). Frequently he said unto the brethren: "The saints denied themselves even water. Is it not sufficient for us to take food twice a day, and that in abundance—with wine and various dishes?" Therefore, if anyone bought wine and oil, the entire purchase was poured into the refuse pit. Kekelidze, *Liturgicheskie gruzinskie pamyatniki* [Liturgical Georgian Monuments], pp. 305 ff., 478 ff.

[^1817]: Dmitrievsky, *Τυπικά*, pp. 671, 678. In the Typikon of Empress Irene this order of election develops into a very intricate rite. Upon three identical parchments was written the prayer: "O our Master Jesus Christ, through the intercessions of our all-immaculate Lady the Mother of God Full of Grace, reveal unto us sinners whether Thou deemest worthy of governance over us our sister [Name]"; the parchments, sealed by the patroness of the monastery, were placed upon the holy table for the duration of the Vigil and the Divine Liturgy. Immediately following the Divine Liturgy, the Trisagion is chanted and the troparia: **"Have mercy on us, O Lord..."** (*Pomiluy nas, Gospodi...*); **"Glory..."**, **"O Lord, look down from heaven and behold..."** (*Gospodi, prizri s nebese i vizhd...*); **"Both now..."**, **"O Only-Begotten, Consubstantial..."** (*Yedinorodne, Yedinosushchne...*). The priest (fully vested) intones the Insistent Litany, unto which he appends the petition: "Furthermore we pray that the Lord will show us one worthy of governance over us"; the sisters chant: "Lord, have mercy" thirty times with fifteen prostrations, lifting up their hands with the prayer: "O God Who knowest the heart, reveal unto us sinners one worthy of governance over us." The priest, having made three prostrations before the holy table with the same prayer, takes one of the parchments, and following verification of the seal, the woman who sealed it opens it; the elected nun, after three prostrations, takes from a special place before the sanctuary the Typikon and the staff, stands in the abbess's stall, is greeted by all, glory is sent up unto God, and the customary dismissal is given by the priest (Typikon of Empress Irene, ch. 11; Migne, *Patrologia Graeca*, vol. 127).
