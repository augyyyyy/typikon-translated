## Character and Content of the Introductory Psalm

Psalm 103 [104 MT], with which Vespers begins, "and with it all the services of the day" [^2035], and which is therefore termed introductory (*προοιμιακός* / *prooimiakos*), expresses the feeling inspired by the evening (vv. 19, 20: **"The sun knoweth his going down. Thou appointedst darkness, and it was night"**) with its repose from labours and leisure [^2036]—a feeling of awe at the glory of God in nature, in creation, and in providence over it, which have been contemplated *(Orig. p. 502)* throughout the entire day [^2037]. In this psalm, parallel unto Psalm 102 [103 MT]—which depicts the glory of God not in nature, but in Israel, and His gracious rather than natural providence over it (for which reason it is used at the Liturgy)—in Psalm 103, in the words of St. Symeon of Thessalonica, "the Lord is blessed, narration is made concerning all His creation (*δημιουργίαν*), and thanksgiving is lifted up unto Him, since upon the completion of the day it is fitting to give thanks for all things" [^2038]. Among the series of homogeneous psalms that depict the glory of God in nature (such as Psalms 8 and 18 [19 MT]—the glory of God preeminently in the heavens; Psalm 64 [65 MT], **"A hymn becometh Thee, O God,"**—the divine goodness revealed in the fertility of the earth; Psalm 28 [29 MT],

=== LEAF p602 ===
**"Bring unto the Lord, O ye sons of God,"**—the majesty of God in atmospheric phenomena), the present psalm [^2039] was chosen for Vespers, presumably on account of its singular fullness, as well as for its distinct delineation of the days of creation, the beginning of which, according unto the Book of Genesis, coincides with the evening [^2040]. In view of such exceptionality, sublimity of content, and propriety of the psalm in this place, Vespers—alone among all the daily services—possesses only this single psalm as its introductory psalm (even the Hours have three introductory psalms, and Matins has six).

Following both the order of the days of creation and the comparative hierarchy of the cosmic spheres in depicting the glory of God, the psalm successively depicts the glory of the Creator in the heavens, upon the earth, and in the watery realm, and thereupon His glory as the Provider. Representing God under the image of a mighty King, the psalm calls light His garment (the 1st day of creation), and the tent ("curtain")—the vault of heaven, which, notwithstanding its watery composition, serves as the immovable upper chamber of God; the clouds are the chariot of this King, and His messengers and ministers are the wind and fire (the 2nd day of creation). As regards the earth, what is astonishing first of all is its unshakably firm foundation, a foundation which is properly nothing other than its own gravity [^2041]; equally expressive of the majesty of the Creator is the manner in which it received its present surface: the water (the deep) that originally enveloped it, even covering the mountains (**"upon the mountains shall the waters stand"** [^2042]), fled as it were timidly into its reservoirs before the authoritative word of God (on the 3rd day of creation), and upon the earth the mountains rose up, while between them valleys were laid out; gathered now into their natural boundaries, the waters came fully to serve the purposes of Providence: they not only "give drink" unto the beasts [^2043] upon the earth (of which, as an example, the wild asses, onagers—the most timid of steppe animals—and the birds nesting *(Orig. p. 503)* upon the coastal crags [^2044] are named, as being equally in need of ready drink), but also foster the growth of food upon the earth for its inhabitants, for which reason God sends waters (in the form of rain) even upon the highest mountains. The psalmist enumerates the chief kinds of these divine creations of the second half of the 3rd day, dwelling upon those particularly beneficial unto man—wine, oil, and bread—and unto beasts; yet those beneficial unto the latter, in contrast unto the former, God Himself causes to grow without the cooperation of man (**"which Thou hast planted"**—hence their frequent grandeur, as the cedars) in His care for all His creatures, for each of which He prepared upon the earth—now fully ordered (on the 3rd day)—a dwelling suited unto its instincts: for birds, trees, and for certain of them, such as the heron (Greek *ἐρῳδιός*, in Hebrew: the stork, crane,

=== LEAF p603 ===
etc.), and particularly tall trees ("leadeth them"—rising above all nests) [^2045]; for the wild roes ("harts"), the mountains; for the timid hares [conies], the crags. This convenient habitation for His creatures—the earth—God also illuminated in a corresponding manner (the 4th day of creation), wherein among the luminaries the moon is named first, as regulating the longer and sacred intervals of time; night is appointed preeminently for the activity of wild beasts, and the day for man. Having surveyed the earth in the entire fullness of the creations of God (**"the earth was filled with Thy creation"**), which sufficiently testify unto the majesty of the Creator (**"for—oh, how!—magnified are Thy works, O Lord; in wisdom hast Thou made them all"**), the psalmist descends lower, into the sea, and there finds an almost greater fullness of life, downright innumerable in its creeping things (the 5th day of creation), yet more grandiose (**"this dragon"** [leviathan], Hebrew *leviathan*, sea monsters), which nonetheless is subdued by the power of God (**"which Thou hast made to play therein"**) even in the hands of man, who boldly navigates the waters filled with such inhabitants. Now the psalmist dwells generally upon the living inhabitants of the earth (the 6th day), who exist solely because God feeds them (Providence); their entire essence consists in the spirit that animates them, and this spirit is entirely in the hands of God: He takes it away at the death of a creature and returns it at birth, whereby—through this life in death and after death—the face of the earth is ever renewed (most immediately, the reference must be unto autumn and spring). The psalmist would desire that the glory of the Creator be preserved unto the ages; God indeed, as an artist, rejoices in His work (cf. in Genesis the divine approval of each day of creation); yet at times the earth also provokes the wrathful gaze of God, which shakes and sets it on fire (earthquakes and volcanoes); this wrath would not cloud our joy and song unto the Lord if sinners were to vanish from the earth, with the fervent wish for which, repeating the opening words of the psalm, the psalm concludes. *(Orig. p. 504)*

## Manner of Execution of the Psalm

For such content, the psalm merits that exceptionally solemn chanting, the like of which the Typikon appoints for only a few other psalms (the Polyeleos psalms, Psalm 136 [137 MT], Psalm 118 [119 MT]). The psalm, as indicated above, is begun by the superior or the ecclesiarch, who with the brethren chants the first half of verse 1: **"Bless the Lord, O my soul,"** and the refrain: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord."** "Then the second chanter of the right choir: **'O Lord my God,**

=== LEAF p604 ===
**Thou art magnified exceedingly. Blessed art Thou, O Lord,'** softly, with harmonious voice, the rest of the brethren chanting along with them," that is, during this the rest of the brethren also chant softly along with the chanter (in the Greek this last remark is absent). Thus, the refrain **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord,"** taken from Psalm 118:12 [119:12 MT], is appointed here for the psalm and, it is assumed, ought to be appended unto each verse. But besides this refrain the Typikon presupposes other refrains for the present psalm as well, at least one more, as is shown by the subsequent rubric of the Typikon concerning the reading of the Prayers of Light by the priest, namely that this reading must begin "when they begin to chant: **'In wisdom hast Thou made them all'** and **'Glory to Thee, O Lord, Who hast made all things.'**" The first of these two verses constitutes the middle of verse 24 of the psalm, while the second expression is not found in the psalm; consequently, it is a refrain; yet the Typikon does not specify at which verse of the psalm this refrain replaces the preceding one, unless the latter remark is taken as such an indication, that is, if this refrain is not presupposed from verse 24 of the psalm. If we turn unto the tradition preserved in the practice of singing and in the musical books, we find here yet another refrain unto the psalm: **"Marvelous are Thy works, O Lord,"** which is attached unto verse 6 of the psalm: **"Upon the mountains shall the waters stand"** (entering into the singing of the psalm from this verse). The same tradition speaks of singing not the entire psalm, but only selected verses of it. The singing of the entire psalm is not explicitly stated in the Typikon either, but it is apparently presupposed both by the above-cited remark, "when they begin to chant," and by the fact that the reading of the 7 Prayers of Light, which requires considerable time, is appointed to begin at a specific moment in the singing of the psalm, prior unto which moment the priest is directed only to stand at his place.

Psalm 103 for the beginning of Vespers is not yet indicated in the well-known description of Sinai Vespers given by Abbots John and Sophronius (presumably of the 6th c.) in Nikon of the Black Mountain: here (Sunday) Vespers begins with **"Blessed is the man"** [^2046]. But according unto the Sinai Horologion of the 8th–9th c. and St. Theodore the Studite (†826), Vespers begins with Psalm 103 [^2047]. And a series of subsequent monuments of the 9th–10th cc.—the *Hypotyposis* (*Ὑποτύπωσις*) attributed unto St. Theodore the Studite [^2048] and the *Diatyposis* (*Διατύπωσις*) of St. Athanasius [^2049]—already concordantly indicate this psalm at the beginning of Ves- *(Orig. p. 505)* pers. As regards the manner of chanting it, in the "Canon of Antiphons (Refrains) for the Psalms of the Great Church of God," inscribed with the name of Patriarch Anthimus of Constantinople (6th c.), the refrain appointed for this psalm is: **"Have mercy (*οἰκτείρησον*) on me, O Lord"** [^2050]. The solemn singing of the psalm at the beginning of Vespers at the Vigil was introduced, of course, only by the Jerusalem Typika, the most ancient extant copies of which, such as the Georgian Shiomgvime and the Greek Sevastyanov of the 13th c., and the Greek Synodal of the

14th c., speak of singing the psalm in Tone 8 [^2051], whereas the most ancient Slavic [manuscripts] (14th c.) are silent regarding the tone [^2052]. Neither group indicates refrains for the psalm. Nor do the 16th-century manuscripts of the Typikon indicate them [^2053]. Yet all the most ancient Typika unanimously demand an elevation of the voice, and in general a more solemn singing, beginning from the middle of verse 28 of the psalm:

**"When Thou openest Thy hand"** (the description of the 6th day of creation and of Providence): "and when we arrive (*φθάσωμεν*) (Slav. 14th c.: 'after the verse is spoken by the canonarch, that is') at the verse: **'When Thou openest (*Ἀνοίξαντος*) Thy hand,'** the ecclesiarch makes a bow (*μετάνοιαν*) unto the superior, and the superior, or if he is not present, the ecclesiarch, lifts up the voice higher upon this verse (*ἀναφωνεῖ τὸν στίχον ὑψηλότερον*) (+ Shiomgvime and Synodal)" [^2054].

## Actions of the Priest during the Introductory Psalm

The singing of the introductory psalm, despite all its solemnity, is not accompanied by any ceremonies. Censing, as we have seen, must conclude prior unto it. On the contrary, from the beginning of its singing the priest, as it were, leaves off the performance of the service for a time: together with the paraecclesiarch who took part in the censing, having exited the sanctuary, they make before the Holy Doors—it is presupposed, having closed them beforehand—three bows (*μετάνοια*); then, turning back, they make a bow unto the superior and one unto each of the two choirs, and withdraw unto their places (*στασίδια*) [^2059]. But when they begin to chant **"In wisdom hast Thou made them all"** and **"Glory to Thee, O Lord, Who hast made all things,"** that is, verse 24 of the psalm (of all 35 verses) with its refrain, the priest approaches the Holy Doors again, and here in epitrachelion with uncovered head (*ἀσκεπής*) reads (*λέγει*) the Prayers of Light, which contain a supplication for all things needful for the evening and thereby answer unto the praise of the introductory psalm for the past day. These prayers are read before the Holy Doors because the Entrance into the sanctuary has not yet taken place. For this reason, in comparison with these prayers, the Prayer of the Entrance occupies a higher rank, and a yet higher one—the Prayer at the Bowing of Heads, which is read before the Holy Table.

Ancient Typika exhibit slight differences here from the present practice. The most ancient Greek [manuscripts] and the Shiomgvime speak of one bow by the priest unto the Holy Doors and one unto each choir (3 bows instead of the present 6); *(Orig. p. 507)* the most ancient Slavic has already 3 bows unto the Holy Doors (5 bows); neither mentions a bow unto the superior; 16th-century copies and the Old Believer [Typikon] agree with the present. More significant is the difference that the Greek and Shiomgvime appoint the reading of the Prayers of Light from **"When Thou openest Thy hand,"** that is, 4 verses of the psalm later than the present, from verse 28; but the most ancient Slavic (14th c.) and the Order (*Diataxis*) of Patriarch Philotheus are already like the present. The change was occasioned, perhaps, by the shortening of the chants. Copies of the 16th c. and the Old Believer [edition], instead of the present "in epitrachelion" (which is absent in all ancient [witnesses]), have: "and taking the epitrachelion and signing it with his hand crosswise and kissing it, he places it upon himself" [^2060]. This assumes the practice, observed even now in the East and in Greece, that the priest vests in the epitrachelion each time only for the duration of the prayers and sacred actions, while during the entire remaining time of the service he stands without it in the rank of the rest of the brethren; in view of this, the epitrachelion is hung on the front side of the iconostasis by the Holy Doors [^2061].

# THE PRAYERS OF LIGHT

The prayers read by the priest during the singing of the introductory psalm are termed "Prayers of Light," *εὐχαὶ τοῦ λυχνικοῦ* (*euchai tou lychnikou*), that is, prayers of the evening office (Vespers was called *τὸ λυχνικόν* / *to lychnikon*, "the Lucernarium" or "Office of the Lighting of Lamps," already in the 4th c.), since these are almost all the prayers of the priest at Vespers, and in antiquity they were distributed throughout various moments of this service. They are appointed in the sacred number of 7, which corresponds also unto the 7 days of creation and providence celebrated during their reading; at Matins, corresponding unto the greater length and importance of that service, analogous prayers are appointed likewise in a sacred number, but of 12. In their content, neither each of the Prayers of Light individually, nor, still less, their entire sequence, presents anything systematic. Nevertheless, each succeeding prayer, directing attention unto a new aspect of the divine essence, supplements in some measure the petitions of the preceding ones. The 1st, calling upon the divine mercy and longsuffering, these fundamental attributes of His, as an introductory prayer, asks first of all that He hear our supplication, direct us generally upon the path of God, and impart a joyful sense of the fear of God; the prayer has a concluding doxology identical with the exclamation of the Great Litany. The 2nd and 3rd prayers are of a penitential character, wherein one, moreover, asks for spiritual enlightenment and a sinless remainder of the day, while the other asks for the fulfillment of all our petitions that are full of hope in God, and for the granting unto us not only of fear before God (as in the 1st prayer), but also of love toward Him; the prayers conclude with the exclamations of the 2nd and 3rd litanies (the Little Litanies) of Vespers. The 4th prayer—of a joyful character—commemorating the angelic doxologies unto God, asks to unite with *(Orig. p. 508)* them our praise as well, now and in eternity; as its concluding doxology it has once again the exclamation of the Great Litany [^2062]. The 5th prayer, directing attention unto the Providence of God, which is unshaken by our sins, asks for preservation from them during the remainder of the day and throughout our whole life; it has a doxology nearly identical with the exclamation of the litany before the Canon at Matins and with the Liturgical exclamation before Communion. The 6th prayer, directing attention not only unto the Providence of God (cf. the 5th prayer), which testifieth unto the majesty, unsearchability, and goodness of God, but also unto the Kingdom promised unto us [^2063], once again (cf. the 2nd and 5th prayers) asks for a sinless remainder of the day. Exceptional in its content and length among the other Prayers of Light is the 7th. Directing our gaze toward the majesty, immortality, and light-bearing nature of God, and surveying

=== LEAF p608 ===
the content not only of Vespers (expressions are borrowed from Psalms 103 and 140 [104 and 141 MT]), but also of Compline (there is an expression from Psalm 90 [91 MT] and the final prayer of Compline), it contains only a single petition for a sinless and blessed evening, night, and (already) morning.

The Prayers of Light, read at present during the introductory psalm, are more ancient than the use of this psalm at Vespers, and therefore they were originally attached not unto this psalm, but unto the so-called antiphons of Vespers, that is, unto psalms chanted by verses with special refrains, resembling the refrains unto our present Liturgical antiphons: **"Save us, O Son of God... through the prayers of the Theotokos,"** etc. In certain ancient Euchologia (ca. 13th c.) the Vesper prayers are entitled "Prayer of the 1st Antiphon," "Prayer of the 2nd Antiphon" [^2064]. Such titles are encountered even in copies of the 16th c. [^2065] In the most ancient Euchologia known at present (8th–10th cc.), however, the Vesper prayers are simply numbered [^2066], which perhaps indicates that already by that time they had been removed from their former locations and assigned unto a single moment of Vespers [^2067]. The simplest and, presumably, the most ancient practice for these prayers among known monuments—notwithstanding the comparatively late era of the manuscript (16th c.)—is represented, apparently, by the Georgian Euchologion of the Tiflis Ecclesiastical Museum, which indicates in the section entitled: *(Orig. p. 509)* "Sunday Prayers of the Antiphons and of Vespers, When the Priest Vests and a Vigil Occurs," the following order of Vesper prayers, significantly different from ours. Giving a detailed order of Vespers, this Euchologion does not mention the introductory psalm, but begins with the naming of the first three of our present prayers, calling them the prayers of the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd antiphons and appointing after each of them a little litany with the exclamation that serves as the conclusion of the prayer; thereupon, at various points in Vespers, it indicates 5 more prayers, and among them our present 7th prayer at **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** [^2068]. A Georgian manuscript of the Imperial Public Library No. 208 (16th c.), which gives the order of Vespers with slight differences from ours—although Vespers here has Psalm 103—indicates the same three prayers (the first three of our present ones) not at the psalm, but at the three antiphons of the Vesper kathisma, and besides them at various points of Vespers the same 5 additional prayers, and among them our present 7th prayer at **"Vouchsafe, O Lord"** [^2069]. Close unto this is the practice given by the *Sluzhebnik* in MS No. 518 of the Sophia Library of the St. Petersburg Academy, where of the Vesper prayers the three present ones are indicated at the beginning and the present 7th at **"Now lettest Thou"** [^2070]. Thus, the first 3 Prayers of Light from antiquity occupied their present position in the order of Vespers, namely, they were read at the very beginning of Vespers (at its initial 3 antiphons, before Psalm 103 was introduced here, and subsequently during this psalm).

Such a position these prayers hold immovably in all preserved monuments, whereas all the remaining prayers are arranged in different monuments in the most diverse order, and are not all indicated by all monuments [^2071]. These three prayers, consequently, constitute the most ancient nucleus of the Prayers of Light. Very soon this sacred number of theirs began to increase, mostly by drawing into their group prayers from other parts of Vespers. The *Sluzhebnik* of the 13th c. from the St. Petersburg Sophia Library No. 524 already has 4 prayers at the beginning of Vespers (the 4th being our present 6th) [^2072]. But, of course, uniformity in this regard could not exist in that era: fluctuation in the number of prayers continues down unto the 17th c. Nevertheless, it is characteristic that in the 14th c. the most widely accepted number of prayers, in Russia at least, was 6: Metropolitan Cyprian (1390–1405), in answer unto the question of Abbot Athanasius regarding the number of evening and morning prayers, replies: "at Vespers there are six prayers, the seventh is the entrance prayer, and the eighth is upon the completion of Vespers and after the exclamation" [^2073]. From this it was but one step unto the present practice with its 7 prayers in one group and two prayers at other places of Vespers. A similar practice, with slight differences not only in the arrangement of the prayers, but at times also in their composition, is represented by a series of monuments of the 15th–16th centuries, although the number of Vesper prayers in these monuments is still very diverse and *(Orig. p. 510)* reaches 9, 10, and 11 [^2074], but including herein also the prayers at other places of Vespers: at the Entrance, at the Augmented Litany, and at the Bowing of Heads; while in the group of prayers for the introductory psalm the majority of these monuments indicate either 8 or 7 [^2075]. Those having 8 add unto the present ones a prayer beginning with the words: **"Blessed art Thou, O Lord God Almighty, Who knowest the mind of man"** (*Благословен еси Господи Боже Вседержителю, сведый ум человечь*), which is present in many ancient Greek Euchologia beginning with the Euchologia of the 8th c. [^2076], wherein this prayer occupies mostly the 4th or 5th place and disrupts the agreement in the numbering of subsequent prayers with our present ones. In the number of 8 and with the indicated redundant prayer, the Prayers of Light are contained also in a series of the first printed editions of the *Sluzhebnik*, beginning with the Moscow edition of 1602 and the Striatyn edition of 1604, and ending with the Moscow edition of 1652. For the first time this prayer was omitted, and the Prayers of Light definitively assumed their present composition in the number of 7, in the Moscow *Sluzhebnik* of 1655.
