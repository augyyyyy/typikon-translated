The initials "K D" belong to the renowned Kyivan woodcut master active at the Pechersk Lavra press in the 1620s.
[^4]: The Saturday Great Vespers Dogmatikon in Tone 1 (*Vsemirnuyu slavu* / "The glory of the whole world") is traditionally attributed to St. John of Damascus, celebrating the mystery of the Incarnation and the Virgin as the Ark and gate of heaven.
[^5]: Epigraphic witness: The Kyiv 1629 imprint reads *iz" istlěniya* (изъ истлѣ́нїа / "out of corruption"), reflecting Greek ἐκ φθορᾶς, rather than the variant reading *ot" tleniya*.
[^6]: The Sunday Midnight Office (*Polunoshchnitsa*) features a dedicated Canon to the Most Holy and Undivided Trinity for each of the Eight Tones, chanted in monastic and cathedral practice prior to Sunday Matins.
[^7]: Melodic model (*podoben* / automelon): *Grob" tvoy* (Гро́бъ тво́й / "Thy Tomb, O Savior, the soldiers guarding..."), Tone 1, providing the rhythmic and strophic template for the Sessional Hymn (Kathisma).
[^8]: The Sessional Theotokion highlights the maternal bold intercession (*parrhesia*) of the Theotokos before the Son, urging believers to invoke her day and night to dispel despondency and spiritual lethargy.
[^9]: The Heirmos of Ode 6 draws directly from the biblical prayer of Jonah in the belly of the sea monster (Jonah 2:3–7 [LXX Jonah 2:4–8]), an archetype of descent into Hades and divine deliverance.
[^10]: The phrase *na stado yezhe okradoste* (на ста́до є́же ѡкра́досте) in the Sessional Hymn addresses the Trinity on behalf of the faithful flock that has been raided, scattered, or despoiled by spiritual adversaries (the demons).

[^11]: The Sunday Midnight Office (*Polunoshchnitsa*) dedicated Canon to the Holy and Undivided Trinity in Tone 1 concludes here with the completion of Ode 8 and the entirety of Ode 9, ending with a final Theotokion before the transition to Sunday Matins.

It opens with "God is the Lord" and the Resurrection Troparion of the Tone (*Kameni znamenanu* / "The stone being sealed..."), accompanied by a cross-reference rubric to Saturday Great Vespers where the full text is printed.

[^14]: The Sunday Hypakoe (*hypakoe* / у̀пакоѝ): *Razboinicheye pokayaniye* ("The repentance of the thief opened Paradise..."), chanted after the second Kathisma and Sessional Hymn, reflecting early Jerusalem liturgical practice.
[^15]: The Hymns of Ascent (Anavathmoi / *Stepénna*), Tone 1, consisting of three antiphons based on the Gradual Psalms (Psalms 119–133 [LXX 118–132]), composed by St. Theodore the Studite (759–826). The second antiphon opens with an allusion to Psalm 120:1 [LXX 119:1]: *V hory tvoyikh voznesé mya zakon dobrodětelei* ("Unto the mountains of Thine ordinances did the law of virtues elevate me...").
[^16]: Trinitarian theology in the third antiphon: *yedinstvo bo yest' Tritsa yestestvom", a ne litsy* ("for the Trinity is one in essence, yet not in persons"), an exact Slavonic rendering of the orthodox Cappadocian formula (μία οὐσία, τρεῖς ὑποστάσεις / one essence, three hypostases).
[^17]: Sunday Canon of the Resurrection, Tone 1, traditionally attributed to St. John of Damascus. The Heirmos of Ode 1 (*Tvoyá pobědítelnaya desnítsa* / "Thy victorious right hand...") is based on the First Biblical Canticle, the Song of Moses at the Red Sea (Exodus 15:1–19 [LXX 15:1–19]).
[^18]: The Kyiv 1629 Octoechos includes alongside the Resurrection Canon two complementary Sunday canons: the Canon to the Precious and Life-Giving Cross (*In" Kanon" Krestu*) and the Canon to the Most Holy Theotokos (*In" Kanon" Presvyatěi Bogoroditsě*), chanted conjointly at Sunday Matins.
[^19]: Dogmatic Theotokion of the Canon to the Cross: *Boga dshche zachalá yesi... v" dvoyu sushchestvu, znayemago sna* ("Thou didst conceive God, O daughter... the Only-begotten Son known in two natures"), expressing the Christological dogma of the Council of Chalcedon (451 AD).
[^20]: Epigraphic reading: *u Piláta isprosíste umobrědno* [Slavonic: оумобрѣ́дно / оумнобрѣ́дно], translating Greek ἐкφρόνως ("madly, frantically, out of your mind"), describing the irrational clamor of the crowd demanding Christ's crucifixion before Pontius Pilate.
[^21]: Canon to the Cross, Ode 4: *Kto yesí krasen" iz" Edéma... ot" vinograda vosoríka* ("Who art Thou that comest fair from Edom, and this redness of garments from the vineyard of Bozrah?"), drawing directly upon the messianic and prophetic vision of Isaiah 63:1–3 [LXX 63:1–3].
[^22]: Canon to the Cross, Ode 6: *Um" syi bezstrasten" i neshchestven", priměshayetsya Khs" Bg" umu chlcheskomu* ("Being the impassible and immaterial Mind, Christ God unites Himself to the human mind"), directly echoing St. Gregory the Theologian's soteriological doctrine (Ep. 101): "For that which He has not assumed He has not healed" (τὸ γὰρ ἀπρόσληπτον, ἀθεράпеυτον).

[^23]: Canon of the Cross, Ode 7, Heirmos: *Sotrotsi vo blagochestii vospitani* ("The youths reared in piety..."), rooted in the biblical canticle of the Three Holy Children in the Babylonian furnace (Daniel 3:19–24 [LXX Daniel 3:51–90]). The first troparion explicitly contrasts the earth cursed through the innocent blood of Abel shed by Cain (Genesis 4:10–11) with the earth blessed through sprinkling by Christ's God-flowing Blood (Hebrews 12:24).
[^24]: Canon of the Most Holy Theotokos, Ode 7: *v Adamovu kozhu obleksya* ("clothed Himself in the skin of Adam"). This draws upon the classic patristic interpretation of the "tunics of skins" (Genesis 3:21) bestowed upon the fallen protoplasts, signifying mortal and passible human nature assumed without sin by the Incarnate Logos (cf. St. Gregory the Theologian, *Oration 38 on the Theophany*, 12; St. John of Damascus, *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*, III.1).
[^25]: Epigraphic witness on Fol. 20r: The lower margin contains an identical 19th-century cursive ownership inscription to that found on Fol. 10r (Cohort 2): *Принадлежитъ библїотекѣ Троицкой Сергїевой Лавры. 1859 года* ("Belongs to the library of the Trinity-St. Sergius Lavra, year 1859"), corroborating the provenance of this copy in the collection of the Trinity-St. Sergius Lavra.
[^26]: Canon of the Cross, Ode 8: Typological imagery of Aaron's rod that budded without cultivation (Numbers 17:8 [LXX 17:23]), prefiguring the virgin birth of Christ without male seed.
[^27]: Canon of the Resurrection, Ode 9: The Burning Bush (*he batos* / *neopalimaya kupina*) seen by the Prophet Moses on Mount Horeb (Exodus 3:2–4) as the pre-eminent Old Testament type of the Virgin Mary, whose womb contained the uncreated fire of Divinity without being consumed.
[^28]: Rubric `Нахвали́те. Стїхи̑ры. Гла́съ, а҃.` (At the Praises / *Lauds*, Tone 1): The praises of Sunday Matins in Tone 1 comprise four Resurrection Stichera, followed by three Anatolian (*vostochny*) Stichera, and conclude with an apologetic Sticheron (*Lyubomyatezhnyi rode evreiskii* / "Give ear, O riot-loving Hebrew race...") confronting the guards' testimony and the empty tomb (Matthew 27:62–66, 28:11–15).
[^29]: Rubric `Внедѣ́л[ю]. Налїтꙋргі́и, Бл҃же́ны. Гла́съ, а҃.` (On Sunday at the Divine Liturgy: The Beatitudes, Tone 1): In the Ruthenian and Byzantine liturgical synthesis, the Makarismoi at the Divine Liturgy are interspersed with eight troparia (six Resurrection troparia followed by a Triadikon and a Theotokion), each punctuated by the refrains of the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–12) and the Good Thief's petition (Luke 23:42).
[^30]: Rubric `Внеⷣ вече́ръ. Стїхи̑ры Покаѧ́нны. Кѵръ, І҆ѡ́сифа` (Sunday Evening at Vespers: Penitential Stichera, by Kyr Joseph): Sunday evening Vespers inaugurates the liturgical week with penitential (*katanyctic*) hymns alongside commemorations of the Bodiless Heavenly Hosts (traditionally assigned to Monday). The stichera are authored by St. Joseph the Hymnographer (c. 816–886). Note also the typographical error on Fol. 25r: `Чл҃коче́нче` for `Чл҃колю́бче`.
[^31]: Stichera to the Bodiless Powers, Tone 1: The hymnography systematically incorporates the nine orders of the celestial hierarchy derived from the corpus of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (*De Coelesti Hierarchia*), invoking Angels, Archangels, Principalities, Thrones, Dominions, Seraphim, Cherubim, Powers, and Authorities.
[^32]: Primary Landmark: Tone 2 (*Гласъ Второй*) opens on Fol. 27r with a monumental full-page ornamental woodcut headpiece bearing the inscription `ВЪ КНИГИ ОКТѠИХЪ ВСКРⷭ҇НЪ` and the explicit attribution `Творе́нїе І҆ѡа́нна . Дамаски́на` (Composed by John Damascene). It contains the first cycle of Saturday evening Great Vespers Stichera at *Lord, I have cried* (*Pervovechnomu ot Otsa* / "Come, let us worship God the Word...").
[^33]: Theotokion / Dogmatikon of Tone 2 at Saturday Great Vespers (*Preide sen' zakonnaya, blagodati prishedshi* / "The shadow of the Law passed away when grace arrived..."): St. John of Damascus's classic theological hymn expounding the transition from Old Testament types and shadows to New Testament grace and the reality of the Incarnation.

[^34]: Typology of the Exodus in the Dogmatikon of Tone 2 (*Preide sen' zakonnaya*): The hymn explicitly contrasts the shadow of the Mosaic Law with the grace of the Gospel, drawing upon the pillar of fire (Exodus 13:21–22) that led the Israelites through the wilderness, which is fulfilled and superseded by Christ, the spiritual Sun of righteousness (Malachi 4:2 [LXX 3:20]). In place of Moses the lawgiver stands Christ the Savior, effecting the redemption of mankind.
[^35]: The Aposticha Stichera of the Resurrection, Tone 2 (*Voskreseniye Tvoye, Khriste Spase*): These four hymns, traditionally attributed to St. John of Damascus, celebrate the cosmic victory of the Cross over the tree of the Garden of Eden (Genesis 3:1–6), the harrowing of Hades, the piercing of the Lord's side by the soldier's spear (John 19:34), and the empty tomb sealed by the Judean authorities (Matthew 27:62–66).
[^36]: Theotokion of the Aposticha (*O chudese novago vsekho drevnikho chudes* / "O wonder new beyond all wonders of old!"): Composed by St. John of Damascus, this venerable hymn ponders the mystery of the Virgin Mother holding in her maternal embrace the uncircumscribed God Who sustains all creation in the palm of His hand. It affirms the orthodox dogma of the Theotokos as defined at the Council of Ephesus (431 AD) and the Council of Chalcedon (451 AD).
[^37]: Sunday Midnight Office (*Mesonyktikon*), Canon to the Most Holy and Undivided Trinity (*Kanon Troichen*), Tone 2: Composed by Metrophanes, Metropolitan of Smyrna (c. 820–880; author of the Triadikos Canons of the Octoechos). The canon provides an exhaustive patristic and scholastic synthesis of Trinitarian theology, systematically articulating the consubstantiality (*homoousion*), co-eternity, and unconfused hypostatic properties of the Father (the unbegotten Source and Cause), the Son (begotten before all ages), and the Holy Spirit (eternally proceeding from the Father).
[^38]: Sessional Hymn to the Holy Trinity, Tone 2, Ode 3 (*Svyataya Troitse, pomilui ikhzhe sozda*): Chanted according to the melodic model (*prosomoion*) *Vyshnikh ishcha* ("Seeking the things on high"), entreating the consubstantial Trinity to deliver creation from fire and condemnation and grant forgiveness of sins.
[^39]: Canon to the Holy Trinity, Ode 5, Troparion 2: Moses and the vision in the Burning Bush (Exodus 3:2–14). The hymn incorporates the traditional patristic christology (patristic consensus of St. Justin Martyr, St. Irenaeus of Lyons, and St. Athanasius the Great) identifying the "Angel of the Lord" who spoke from the flame as the pre-incarnate Logos, the "Angel of Great Counsel" (LXX Isaiah 9:6), revealing the tri-hypostatic nature of the one divine Principle.
[^40]: Sunday Matins Sessional Hymns of the Resurrection, Tone 2, after the First Kathisma: *Blagoobraznyi Iosif* ("The noble Joseph..."), the classic troparion of Great and Holy Saturday (commemorating Joseph of Arimathea taking down Christ's Body, wrapping it in a clean winding sheet, and placing it in a new rock-hewn sepulcher; Matthew 27:57–60, Mark 15:43–46), used here in the Sunday Resurrection cycle to proclaim the Lord's third-day rising.
[^41]: Hypakoe of Tone 2 (*Po strasti shedshe na grob* / "After the Passion, the women having gone unto the tomb"): The Hypakoe (from the Greek *hypakoe*, "obedience" or "listening attentively") recounts the holy Myrrh-bearing women who went at early dawn to anoint the Lord's sacred Body with aromatic spices, beholding the angelic messengers who declared that Christ is not among the dead (Mark 16:1–8, Luke 24:1–10).
[^42]: The Gradual Hymns (*Anavathmoi*) of Tone 2: Structured into three antiphons inspired by the Old Testament Psalms of Ascents (Psalms 119–133 [LXX 118–132]) sung by Hebrew pilgrims journeying up to Jerusalem. Each antiphon concludes with a pneumatological troparion (*Svyatomu Dukhu...*) extolling the Holy Spirit as the sovereign Principle of life, wisdom, and sanctification alongside the Father and the Son.
[^43]: Canons of Sunday Matins, Tone 2: The classic Byzantine-Ruthenian tri-canon structure prescribed for the Resurrection Matins in the Octoechos: (1) The Canon of the Resurrection by St. John of Damascus (*Vo glubine potopi drevle* / "Of old, the all-armed power drowned..."); (2) The Canon of the Cross (*Netrennuyu neobychuyu* / "Traversing an untrodden, unaccustomed path..."); and (3) The Canon of the Most Holy Theotokos to the same heirmos model.

[^44]: Codicological Rubric Anomaly in the Canon of the Cross, Tone 2, Ode 3: The rubric printed in red cinnabar reads *Бгородиченъ* (*Theotokion*), but the liturgical hymn text itself (*Mertv sobezakonnymi vmenisya, siyaya zhenam krasen dobrotoyu, Khriste yavisya voskreseniyem*) is a Christological Resurrectional/Stavro-resurrectional troparion celebrating Christ being numbered among the lawless (Isaiah 53:12) and appearing in transcendent beauty to the holy Myrrh-bearing women upon His third-day rising.

[^45]: The Resurrection Heirmos of Ode 4, Tone 2 (*Prishestvoval ot Devy, ni khodatai ni angel*): Composed by St. John of Damascus, grounded in the Septuagint of Isaiah 63:9 ("Not an ambassador, nor an angel, but the Lord Himself saved them...") and the prophetic vision of Habakkuk (Habakkuk 3:2–3). The hymn exalts the unmediated, personal condescension of the Divine Logos in taking upon Himself true human nature from the Virgin Mother of God.

[^46]: Typology of the Tripartite Wood of the Holy Cross in Ode 5 (*Yako kedry Khriste*): The troparion evokes Christ's voluntary crucifixion upon the cypress, the pine, and the cedar (*na kyparise, i na pevge i kedre*). This draws directly from the Septuagint prophecy of Isaiah 60:13 ("The glory of Lebanon shall come unto thee, with the cypress tree, the pine tree, and the cedar together, to beautify the place of My sanctuary"), a primary patristic and hymnographic type for the construction of the Venerable Cross.

[^47]: The Cherubim and the Reopening of Paradise in Ode 6 (*Blistitelya polozhil yesi padshemu kheruvim*): Direct scriptural allusion to Genesis 3:24, where the Cherubim and the turning, flashing sword of fire guarded the way to the tree of life after Adam's expulsion. Upon the Lord's crucifixion and triumph over death, the gates of Eden swung open to welcome the repentant thief (Luke 23:43), manifesting the royal highway to eternal communion.

[^48]: The Heirmos of Ode 7, Tone 2 (*Bogoprotivnoye veleniye, bezakonuyushchago muchitelya*): Typology of the Three Holy Youths in the Babylonian furnace (Daniel 3:19–28). While the tyrannical decree of King Nebuchadnezzar raised the flame forty-nine cubits high, Christ manifested spiritual dew (*khlad dukhovnyi*) amidst the inferno to preserve His confessors, prefiguring His victorious descent into Hades to deliver the righteous souls held captive from the beginning of the world.

[^49]: Heirmos of the Canon of the Cross, Ode 8 (*Telu zlatomu nepoklonishasya tribogatyya yunosha*): Commemorating the steadfast refusal of the three holy youths (Ananias, Azarias, and Misael) to bow down before the golden colossus on the plain of Dura (Daniel 3:1–18). Gazing upon the true, living image of God, they prefigured the mystery of the Incarnation. The technical theological term *osushchestvivyisya* ("having taken substance" / Greek *ousiotheis*) confirms the orthodox dogma of the true hypostatic enfleshment of the Divine Logos.

[^50]: Typology of the Thrice-Rich Tree of the Cross on Golgotha in Ode 9 (*Yako vrai nasazhdenno vo kraniyevi Spase*): The Venerable Cross planted upon Golgotha (*Kranion* / the Place of the Skull) is hailed as the new tree of life, far surpassing the tree of life in the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2:9). Irrigated by the water and the blood that flowed from the pierced side of the Savior (John 19:34), it buds forth incorruptible life for the whole world.

[^51]: Polemical Paschal Apologetic at the Praises (*Da rekut iyudeye*): This celebrated Resurrection sticheron at the Praises directly refutes the guard deception concocted by the chief priests (Matthew 27:62–66, 28:11–15). Employing dramatic rhetorical irony, the hymn demands of the guards: if they were asleep, how could they know the disciples stole Him; and why would grave-robbers take a naked corpse while leaving the linen cloths and face veil carefully rolled up in place (John 20:5–7)?

[^52]: The Sunday Beatitudes (*Blazheny* / *Makarismoi*) of Tone 2 at the Divine Liturgy: In the Byzantine-Ruthenian liturgical tradition, the Beatitudes from the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:3–12) are sung at the Little Entrance, interpolated with troparia from the Octoechos commemorating the penitent thief, the harrowing of Hades, the empty sepulcher, and the angelic greeting to the Myrrh-bearing women.

[^53]: Sunday Evening Vespers (Entering Monday): Liturgically, Sunday evening marks the transition from the festive Lord's Day of the Resurrection into the penitential rhythm of the weekly cycle. Because Monday is dedicated to the Bodiless Powers of Heaven (the holy Angels), Vespers commences with penitential stichera (*pokayanny*) imploring the mercy of the Holy Trinity, followed by stichera in honor of the Nine Angelic Choirs to the melodic model (*podoben*) *Yegda ot dreva Tya* ("When from the Tree...").

[^54]: On the Dionysian Angelology of the Stichera to the Bodiless Powers on Sunday evening (entering Monday). The hymn directly reflects the *Celestial Hierarchy* of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, depicting the first hierarchy (Thrones, Cherubim, Seraphim) receiving the unmediated rays of the Godhead (*nekhodotaystvennymi siyanii*) and transmitting these divine illuminations down through the subordinate ranks according to their capacity and hierarchical purity (*k yerarkhichney komuzhdo chistote*).

[^55]: Codicological Transition to Tone 3 on Fol. 50r: The concluding Sunday evening office of Tone 2 ends on Fol. 49v with a decorative floral tailpiece and the catchword *Vsubotu* ("On Saturday..."). On Fol. 50r, Tone 3 opens with a magnificent woodcut headpiece and monumental vyaz lettering *ВЪ КНИГИ ВОСКРЕСНЫ* ("Into the Resurrection Books"). The text immediately commences the first Saturday Vespers sticheron on *Lord, I have cried* (*Tvoim krestom Khriste Spase*), containing the early printed typographical haplography *rozhe* for *rod zhe*.

[^56]: The Dogmatikon of Tone 3 (*Kako ne divimsya*): Attributed to St. John of Damascus, this classic Sunday Vespers Theotokion articulates the Chalcedonian Christology of the Incarnation. The Son is begotten of the Father without a mother before all ages, and born in the flesh of the Virgin Mother without a human father, undergoing neither change (*izmeneniya* / Greek *alloiōsin*), confusion (*smesheniya* / *synchysin*), nor division (*razdeleniya* / *diairesin*), but preserving intact the specific properties (*svoystvo tselo sokhransha* / *ten idiotēta sōsan*) of each nature in one divine Hypostasis.

[^57]: The Resurrection Troparion of Tone 3 (*Da veselyatsya nebesnaya*): "Let the heavens be glad and let the earth rejoice..." (Psalm 95:11 LXX). The troparion summarizes the Paschal victory through the Magnificat typology of the arm of the Lord (*sotvori derzhavu myshtseyu svoyeyu*, Luke 1:51), trampling death by death and becoming the firstborn from the dead (Colossians 1:18), delivering humanity from the belly of Hades.

[^58]: Sunday Midnight Office Canon to the Trinity by Metrophanes (Ode 1 & 3, Tone 3): Metrophanes of Smyrna (9th c.) composed the Sunday Midnight Office canons to the Holy Trinity found across the Octoechos. In Ode 1, he invokes the classical psychological analogy of St. Gregory of Nazianzus: Mind, Word, and Spirit (*Um, Slovo, i Dukh*), imaging the unoriginate begetting of the Son and the procession of the Holy Spirit. In Ode 3, he interprets Elijah commanding water to be poured three times over the wood of the sacrifice on Mount Carmel (3 Kings / 1 Kings 18:33–34) as an Old Testament type of the Thrice-Radiant Sovereignty of God.

[^59]: Botanical Trinitarian Imagery in Ode 4 (*Otrasl sogub ot Otsa*): Metrophanes develops the patristic metaphor of the Root and the Offshoots (first articulated by Tertullian and refined by the Cappadocian Fathers): the Father is the eternal Root (*koren*), from Whom spring forth the two uncreated, consubstantial shoots (*otrasli*)—the Son by begetting and the Holy Spirit by procession—blooming as co-eternal, divine flowers sharing one essence and three sovereign hypostases.

[^60]: The Hospitable Theophany to Abraham in Ode 6 (*Preselnik syi Avraam*): Allusion to Genesis 18:1–15, where Abraham the stranger/sojourner welcomed three travelers beneath the oak of Mamre. Patristic tradition, codified liturgically and iconographically (as in the classic Byzantine *Philoxenia*), views this event as an Old Testament typological revelation of the Holy Trinity—the supra-essential Godhead foreshadowed in three distinct persons under human forms.

[^61]: Trinitarian Terminology in Ode 7 (*Bozhestva zrak yedin vozveshchayem*): The troparion balances unity and distinction: "One Form/Aspect of Godhead (*zrak yedin* / Greek *morphe*) in three hypostatic and distinct properties (*vo triyekh sostavnykh i razdelnykh svoystvekh* / *en trisi hypostatikois kai diakekrimenois idiotēsi*), Father, Son, and Holy Spirit." The response echoes the Song of the Three Youths: "Blessed art Thou, O God of our fathers!" (Daniel 3:52).

[^62]: Sunday Matins Transition and First Kathisma Sessional Hymn (*Khristos ot mertvykh vosta*): Following the conclusion of the Midnight Office Triadikon canon, the text transitions directly to Sunday Matins. The Troparion at *God is the Lord* (*Da veselyatsya*) and its Theotokion are cross-referenced to Saturday Vespers (*pisa vo subotu na vecherni*). The Sessional Hymn following the first Kathisma of the Psalter celebrates Christ risen as the firstfruits of those that sleep and the firstborn of creation (1 Corinthians 15:20; Colossians 1:15).

[^63]: The Hypakoe and Anabathmoi (Antiphon 1) of Tone 3: The Hypakoe (*Udivlyaya videniyem*) presents the shining angel at the tomb telling the Myrrh-bearers: "Why seek ye the Living One in the tomb? He is risen, emptying the graves!" quoting Psalm 65:3 LXX. Antiphon 1 of the Songs of Ascent (*Stepennа*) interprets the historical return of the captivity of Zion from Babylon (Psalm 125:1 LXX) as a mystical figure of the soul's liberation from passions into life by the Divine Logos, sowing tears in the south wind to reap sheaves of joy (Psalm 125:5–6 LXX).

[^36]: Theotokion of the Aposticha (*O chudese novago vsekho drevnikho chudes* / "O wonder new beyond all wonders of old!"): Composed by St. John of Damascus, this venerable hymn ponders the mystery of the Virgin Mother holding in her maternal embrace the uncircumscribed God Who sustains all creation in the palm of His hand. It affirms the orthodox dogma of the Theotokos as defined at the Council of Ephesus (431 AD) and the Council of Chalcedon (451 AD).

The canon provides an exhaustive patristic and scholastic synthesis of Trinitarian theology, systematically articulating the consubstantiality (*homoousion*), co-eternity, and unconfused hypostatic properties of the Father (the unbegotten Source and Cause), the Son (begotten before all ages), and the Holy Spirit (eternally proceeding from the Father).
[^38]: Sessional Hymn to the Holy Trinity, Tone 2, Ode 3 (*Svyataya Troitse, pomilui ikhzhe sozda*): Chanted according to the melodic model (*prosomoion*) *Vyshnikh ishcha* ("Seeking the things on high"), entreating the consubstantial Trinity to deliver creation from fire and condemnation and grant forgiveness of sins.
[^39]: Canon to the Holy Trinity, Ode 5, Troparion 2: Moses and the vision in the Burning Bush (Exodus 3:2–14). The hymn incorporates the traditional patristic christology (patristic consensus of St. Justin Martyr, St. Irenaeus of Lyons, and St. Athanasius the Great) identifying the "Angel of the Lord" who spoke from the flame as the pre-incarnate Logos, the "Angel of Great Counsel" (LXX Isaiah 9:6), revealing the tri-hypostatic nature of the one divine Principle.
[^40]: Sunday Matins Sessional Hymns of the Resurrection, Tone 2, after the First Kathisma: *Blagoobraznyi Iosif* ("The noble Joseph..."), the classic troparion of Great and Holy Saturday (commemorating Joseph of Arimathea taking down Christ's Body, wrapping it in a clean winding sheet, and placing it in a new rock-hewn sepulcher; Matthew 27:57–60, Mark 15:43–46), used here in the Sunday Resurrection cycle to proclaim the Lord's third-day rising.
[^41]: Hypakoe of Tone 2 (*Po strasti shedshe na grob* / "After the Passion, the women having gone unto the tomb"): The Hypakoe (from the Greek *hypakoe*, "obedience" or "listening attentively") recounts the holy Myrrh-bearing women who went at early dawn to anoint the Lord's sacred Body with aromatic spices, beholding the angelic messengers who declared that Christ is not among the dead (Mark 16:1–8, Luke 24:1–10).
[^42]: The Gradual Hymns (*Anavathmoi*) of Tone 2: Structured into three antiphons inspired by the Old Testament Psalms of Ascents (Psalms 119–133 [LXX 118–132]) sung by Hebrew pilgrims journeying up to Jerusalem. Each antiphon concludes with a pneumatological troparion (*Svyatomu Dukhu...*) extolling the Holy Spirit as the sovereign Principle of life, wisdom, and sanctification alongside the Father and the Son.
[^43]: Canons of Sunday Matins, Tone 2: The classic Byzantine-Ruthenian tri-canon structure prescribed for the Resurrection Matins in the Octoechos: (1) The Canon of the Resurrection by St. John of Damascus (*Vo glubine potopi drevle* / "Of old, the all-armed power drowned..."); (2) The Canon of the Cross (*Netrennuyu neobychuyu* / "Traversing an untrodden, unaccustomed path..."); and (3) The Canon of the Most Holy Theotokos to the same heirmos model.

[^44]: Codicological Rubric Anomaly in the Canon of the Cross, Tone 2, Ode 3: The rubric printed in red cinnabar reads *Бгородиченъ* (*Theotokion*), but the liturgical hymn text itself (*Mertv sobezakonnymi vmenisya, siyaya zhenam krasen dobrotoyu, Khriste yavisya voskreseniyem*) is a Christological Resurrectional/Stavro-resurrectional troparion celebrating Christ being numbered among the lawless (Isaiah 53:12) and appearing in transcendent beauty to the holy Myrrh-bearing women upon His third-day rising.

[^45]: The Resurrection Heirmos of Ode 4, Tone 2 (*Prishestvoval ot Devy, ni khodatai ni angel*): Composed by St. John of Damascus, grounded in the Septuagint of Isaiah 63:9 ("Not an ambassador, nor an angel, but the Lord Himself saved them...") and the prophetic vision of Habakkuk (Habakkuk 3:2–3). The hymn exalts the unmediated, personal condescension of the Divine Logos in taking upon Himself true human nature from the Virgin Mother of God.

[^46]: Typology of the Tripartite Wood of the Holy Cross in Ode 5 (*Yako kedry Khriste*): The troparion evokes Christ's voluntary crucifixion upon the cypress, the pine, and the cedar (*na kyparise, i na pevge i kedre*). This draws directly from the Septuagint prophecy of Isaiah 60:13 ("The glory of Lebanon shall come unto thee, with the cypress tree, the pine tree, and the cedar together, to beautify the place of My sanctuary"), a primary patristic and hymnographic type for the construction of the Venerable Cross.

[^47]: The Cherubim and the Reopening of Paradise in Ode 6 (*Blistitelya polozhil yesi padshemu kheruvim*): Direct scriptural allusion to Genesis 3:24, where the Cherubim and the turning, flashing sword of fire guarded the way to the tree of life after Adam's expulsion. Upon the Lord's crucifixion and triumph over death, the gates of Eden swung open to welcome the repentant thief (Luke 23:43), manifesting the royal highway to eternal communion.

[^48]: The Heirmos of Ode 7, Tone 2 (*Bogoprotivnoye veleniye, bezakonuyushchago muchitelya*): Typology of the Three Holy Youths in the Babylonian furnace (Daniel 3:19–28). While the tyrannical decree of King Nebuchadnezzar raised the flame forty-nine cubits high, Christ manifested spiritual dew (*khlad dukhovnyi*) amidst the inferno to preserve His confessors, prefiguring His victorious descent into Hades to deliver the righteous souls held captive from the beginning of the world.

[^49]: Heirmos of the Canon of the Cross, Ode 8 (*Telu zlatomu nepoklonishasya tribogatyya yunosha*): Commemorating the steadfast refusal of the three holy youths (Ananias, Azarias, and Misael) to bow down before the golden colossus on the plain of Dura (Daniel 3:1–18). Gazing upon the true, living image of God, they prefigured the mystery of the Incarnation. The technical theological term *osushchestvivyisya* ("having taken substance" / Greek *ousiotheis*) confirms the orthodox dogma of the true hypostatic enfleshment of the Divine Logos.

[^50]: Typology of the Thrice-Rich Tree of the Cross on Golgotha in Ode 9 (*Yako vrai nasazhdenno vo kraniyevi Spase*): The Venerable Cross planted upon Golgotha (*Kranion* / the Place of the Skull) is hailed as the new tree of life, far surpassing the tree of life in the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2:9). Irrigated by the water and the blood that flowed from the pierced side of the Savior (John 19:34), it buds forth incorruptible life for the whole world.

[^51]: Polemical Paschal Apologetic at the Praises (*Da rekut iyudeye*): This celebrated Resurrection sticheron at the Praises directly refutes the guard deception concocted by the chief priests (Matthew 27:62–66, 28:11–15). Employing dramatic rhetorical irony, the hymn demands of the guards: if they were asleep, how could they know the disciples stole Him; and why would grave-robbers take a naked corpse while leaving the linen cloths and face veil carefully rolled up in place (John 20:5–7)?

[^52]: The Sunday Beatitudes (*Blazheny* / *Makarismoi*) of Tone 2 at the Divine Liturgy: In the Byzantine-Ruthenian liturgical tradition, the Beatitudes from the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:3–12) are sung at the Little Entrance, interpolated with troparia from the Octoechos commemorating the penitent thief, the harrowing of Hades, the empty sepulcher, and the angelic greeting to the Myrrh-bearing women.

[^53]: Sunday Evening Vespers (Entering Monday): Liturgically, Sunday evening marks the transition from the festive Lord's Day of the Resurrection into the penitential rhythm of the weekly cycle. Because Monday is dedicated to the Bodiless Powers of Heaven (the holy Angels), Vespers commences with penitential stichera (*pokayanny*) imploring the mercy of the Holy Trinity, followed by stichera in honor of the Nine Angelic Choirs to the melodic model (*podoben*) *Yegda ot dreva Tya* ("When from the Tree...").

[^54]: On the Dionysian Angelology of the Stichera to the Bodiless Powers on Sunday evening (entering Monday). [^56]: The Dogmatikon of Tone 3 (*Kako ne divimsya*): Attributed to St. John of Damascus, this classic Sunday Vespers Theotokion articulates the Chalcedonian Christology of the Incarnation. The Son is begotten of the Father without a mother before all ages, and born in the flesh of the Virgin Mother without a human father, undergoing neither change (*izmeneniya* / Greek *alloiōsin*), confusion (*smesheniya* / *synchysin*), nor division (*razdeleniya* / *diairesin*), but preserving intact the specific properties (*svoystvo tselo sokhransha* / *ten idiotēta sōsan*) of each nature in one divine Hypostasis.

[^58]: Sunday Midnight Office Canon to the Trinity by Metrophanes (Ode 1 & 3, Tone 3): Metrophanes of Smyrna (9th c.) composed the Sunday Midnight Office canons to the Holy Trinity found across the Octoechos. [^59]: Botanical Trinitarian Imagery in Ode 4 (*Otrasl sogub ot Otsa*): Metrophanes develops the patristic metaphor of the Root and the Offshoots (first articulated by Tertullian and refined by the Cappadocian Fathers): the Father is the eternal Root (*koren*), from Whom spring forth the two uncreated, consubstantial shoots (*otrasli*)—the Son by begetting and the Holy Spirit by procession—blooming as co-eternal, divine flowers sharing one essence and three sovereign hypostases.

Patristic tradition, codified liturgically and iconographically (as in the classic Byzantine *Philoxenia*), views this event as an Old Testament typological revelation of the Holy Trinity—the supra-essential Godhead foreshadowed in three distinct persons under human forms.

[^61]: Trinitarian Terminology in Ode 7 (*Bozhestva zrak yedin vozveshchayem*): The troparion balances unity and distinction: "One Form/Aspect of Godhead (*zrak yedin* / Greek *morphe*) in three hypostatic and distinct properties (*vo triyekh sostavnykh i razdelnykh svoystvekh* / *en trisi hypostatikois kai diakekrimenois idiotēsi*), Father, Son, and Holy Spirit." The response echoes the Song of the Three Youths: "Blessed art Thou, O God of our fathers!" (Daniel 3:52).

The Troparion at *God is the Lord* (*Da veselyatsya*) and its Theotokion are cross-referenced to Saturday Vespers (*pisa vo subotu na vecherni*). Composed to the prosomoion melody *Е҆гда̀ ѿ дре́ва тя̀* (*Hote ek tou xylou se*), these hymns weave together the biblical titles of the Baptist: "seal of the prophets" (*пррⷱ҇кѡмъ печа́ть*), "voice of the Word" (*свѣ́тлыи сло́вꙋ гла́се* / Isaiah 40:3), and the Johannine proclamation of the Lamb of God taking away the sin of the world (*Сѐ а҆́гнецъ бж҃і́й* / John 1:29).

[^215]: Codicologically and liturgically, Tuesday Matins in the Octoechos contains Sessional Hymns (*Сѣда́льны*) centered upon compunction (*покаѧ́нны*) and the intercession of the Theotokos and the Baptist. Theotokion imagery on Fol. 210r draws directly upon the patristic doctrine of deification (*theosis*) formulated from 2 Peter 1:4: "Partakers of the divine nature have we become through thee" (*Бж҃тве́нномꙋ є҆стествꙋ̀ бы́хомъ прича́стници*), asserting that through the Incarnation of God from the Virgin, humanity is elevated into communion with the divine life.

[^216]: The Sessional Hymn following the third Kathisma versification contrasts the repentance of King David with the inertia of the soul. David's profound contrition over two specific transgressions—the adultery with Bathsheba and the engineered death of Uriah the Hittite (2 Samuel 11–12)—manifested in drenching his couch with tears (Psalm 6:6 LXX). The hymnographer laments that while David wept bitterly for two sins, the sinner remains dry-eyed and callous despite accumulating countless offenses.

[^217]: The first ode of the Canon to the Forerunner introduces the classical heirmos *Воглꙋбинѣ̀ потопѝ дре́вле* ("Of old He drowned in the deep"), referring to the destruction of Pharaoh's host in the Red Sea (Exodus 14). Troparia on Fol. 212r present the typological paradox of the baptism of Christ: the Forerunner, a created mortal, immersed into the waters of the Jordan Him Who is the unsearchable "Abyss" (*бе́зднꙋ*) of divine essence, thereby drowning the ancient flood of human transgression.

[^218]: The third ode of the Canon of Compunction incorporates the Pauline doctrine of the body as the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19). The hymnographer prays that through the intercession of the Baptist—who resides in the heavenly sanctuary—believers may themselves be fashioned into living temples (*хра́мы и҆ на́съ сотворѝ дх҃ꙋ ст҃о́мꙋ*). The ode concludes with the classic Byzantine hymnographic antithesis: the Virgin mother suckles Him Who nourishes the entire cosmos (*дои́ши ма́теролѣ́пно, пита́теля всѣ́мъ*).

[^219]: Ode 4 of the Canon of Compunction employs the Habakkuk theophany heirmos (*И҆́же ѿ дв҃ы твоѐ ржⷭ҇тво*), based on Habakkuk 3:2–3 ("God shall come from Teman, and the Holy One from the thick and shady mountain of Paran"). In the Canon to the Forerunner on Fol. 213v, an explicit ktetorik petition is incorporated: the hymnographer petitions the Baptist on behalf of those who built and lovingly maintain his sacred temple (*и҆́же любо́вїю тѝ созда́ннаго бж҃тве́ннаго хра́ма*), beseeching that they be granted eternal life in the heavenly dwellings.

[^220]: Codicological Note: At the bottom margin of Fol. 215r (Page 217 Right), quire signature *Оо* (Quire 46) begins, demonstrating that the preceding quire *Нн* (Quire 45) was printed as a regular four-leaf gathering (bifolium fold, folios 211–214). The hymnography on this leaf emphasizes the Baptist's courageous defense of divine law before Herod Antipas, which led to his unlawful execution (*беззако́нно закала́ешися*, Matthew 14:1–12).

[^221]: Ode 5 of the Canon of Compunction incorporates the prophetic vision of Daniel 2:34–35, 45, in which a stone cut without hands from a great mountain smashes the pagan statue of composite metals—an established patristic type of Christ's virgin birth from the Theotokos shattering the idols of demonology. In the second canon, the Baptist is characterized as standing at the threshold of salvation history (*посредѣ̀ зако́на и҆ блгⷭ҇ти ста̀*), linking the Old Covenant Law with New Testament grace.

[^222]: In Ode 6, the Theotokion on Fol. 217r portrays the Virgin as the mystical Table bearing the heavenly Bread of Life (*Ꙗ҆́кѡ тра́пеза хлѣ́бъ вмѣсти́вши та́йный*), directly evoking Christ's discourse in John 6:32–51. The martyrikon troparia on Fol. 216v offer an epigraphic witness to the varied torments endured by early Christian martyrs, explicitly recording gouging of eyes (*ѻ҆́чїи и҆збода́еми*) and exposure to freezing winter frost (*померза́еми стꙋде́нїи лю́тѣ*), imagery characteristic of the Passion of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste.

[^223]: The Heirmos of Ode 7 (*Херꙋві́момъ подо́бящеся дѣ́ти*) introduces the Canticle of the Three Holy Youths in the Babylonian furnace, drawing directly upon the Prayer of Azariah (Daniel 3:27–28 LXX: "For Thou art righteous in all that Thou hast done to us... for we have sinned"). The penitential troparion on Fol. 217v invokes David's classic confession from Psalm 50:5 LXX (*Во беззако́нїихъ гдⷭ҇и сѐ зача́тъ є҆́смь* / "Behold, in iniquities was I conceived"), anchoring the Tuesday morning compunction office in Davidic repentance.

[^224]: The Heirmos of Ode 8 (*В кꙋпи́нѣ мѡѷсе́и дв҃ческое чꙋ́до*) links the Old Testament theophany of the unburnt bush on Mount Sinai (Exodus 3:2) with the virginal conception and birth of Christ without corruption. In the preceding Theotokion (Fol. 219r), the hymnographer unites this typological Marian imagery with the Johannine witness: the infant John the Baptist, while still in the barren womb of Elizabeth, recognizes the incarnate Word in the Virgin's womb and leaps for joy (*бж҃тве́ннымъ взыгра́нїемъ* / Luke 1:41–44), thereby acting as the first prophet to confess Christ in the flesh.

[^225]: The Theotokion on Fol. 219v explicitly proclaims the patristic doctrine of deification (*theosis*), derived from 2 Peter 1:4 and St. Athanasius of Alexandria: "That God might deify us, having become incarnate of thy pure blood, and become earthly" (*Да̀ ѡ҆божи́тъ на́съ бг҃ъ, вопло́щся и҆́зъ чи́стыхъ кро́вій твои́хъ, и҆ бы́сть зе́менъ*). In the second canon to the Forerunner, the hymnographer petitions the Baptist—who physically laid his right hand upon the head of the Lord in the Jordan—to stretch forth that same right hand to lift up the sinner fallen in spiritual inertia.

[^226]: Ode 9 of Tuesday Matins combines two seminal biblical exemplars of repentance: the conversion of King Manasseh (*и҆́же манасі́ю ѡ҆браще́й согрѣши́вшаго*), whose apocryphal penitential prayer (2 Chronicles 33 / Odes 12 LXX) serves as a cornerstone of Byzantine compunction, and the nameless sinful woman / harlot (*спасы́й блꙋдни́цꙋ*) who washed Christ's feet with tears (Luke 7:36–50). The hymnographer models his prayer upon their contrition, begging for a continuous stream of tears to wash away his spiritual stains before the dread judgment-seat.

[^227]: In Ode 9 of the Canon to the Forerunner (Fol. 221v), the Baptist is acclaimed through a triad of botanical and aromatic biblical metaphors: the fragrant rose (*шипо́къ бл҃гово́ненъ*), the fragrant cypress (*кѵпари́съ* / Sirach 50:10), and the unfading lily (*неꙋвяда́емый крі́нъ* / Song of Songs 2:1–2), alongside honorable myrrh (*мѵ́ро чⷭ҇тно*), reflecting his ascetic purity in the Judean wilderness. In the subsequent troparion, an explicit ktetorik petition is offered for those who lovingly keep choir in the Baptist's sacred temple (*въ сщ҃тве́ннѣмъ хра́мѣ твое́мъ ликꙋ́ющимъ*), asking for divine deliverance and amendment of life.

[^228]: The weekday office on Tuesday concludes with the singing of the Beatitudes (*Блаже́нна*), a standard feature of the Studite and Ruthenian Octoechos tradition wherein the evangelical verses (Matthew 5:3–12) are interspersed with penitential, martyrial, and Johannine troparia. The concluding Triadikon (*Безнача́льный трⷪ҇ци тебѣ̀ покланя́емся*) provides an explicit dogmatic formula confessing the Holy Trinity as a simple, unoriginate, and tri-hypostatic essence (*є҆сттво́мъ тя̀ зна́я, є҆дини́цꙋ про́стꙋ и҆ трїѵпоста́внꙋ*), guarding Nicene-Constantinopolitan orthodoxy against Arian and Sabellian errors.

[^229]: On Tuesday evening, the liturgical cycle shifts from the commemoration of the Baptist to Wednesday, dedicated weekly throughout the Byzantine year to the Precious and Life-giving Cross and the betrayal and passion of Christ. The stichera at *Lord, I have cried* (Fol. 223v–224r) present the theological antithesis between Adam's condemnation at the tree of knowledge and his redemption through Christ's lifting up upon the Tree of the Cross under the legal curse (Deuteronomy 21:23; Galatians 3:13). The accompanying Staurotheotokion captures the maternal lament of the Virgin at Golgotha: "O my most beloved Child, whither has Thy light-bearing beauty set?"

[^230]: The Sessional Hymns of Wednesday Matins (Fol. 225r) incorporate the typological interpretation of Isaiah 60:13 ("The glory of Lebanon shall come unto thee, the fir tree, the pine tree, and the box together, to beautify the place of my sanctuary"), traditionally understood in Byzantine hymnography as the three woods of Christ's Cross: cypress, pine, and cedar (*На кипари́сѣ и҆ пе́ѵгѣ и҆ ке́дрѣ*). The preceding Aposticha sticheron (Fol. 224v) invokes Moses stretching forth his hands upon Mount Sinai/Rephidim in the cruciform sign to vanquish the host of Amalek (Exodus 17:8–16).

[^231]: Codicological & Rubrical Note: The rubric *По г҃ каѳі́ Сѣ̃ гл҃ по кра̃* (*Po 3 kathismi Sedalen glagolemy po krayegranesiyu*) indicates that following the third Kathisma versification, the sessional hymn is chanted in Tone 2 according to the poetic refrain or alphabetic/acrostic ordering. The accompanying Staurotheotokion on Fol. 226r portrays the Mother of God witnessing the voluntary humiliation and crucifixion of her Son, appealing to the boundless "bowels of mercies" (*за оу҆тро́бꙋ мл҃тей*) of the Redeemer Who takes away the sin of the world.

[^232]: The Matins Canon to the Precious Cross on Wednesday opens with the heirmos indication *И҆́же во́ды дре́вле, писа́нъ внѣ̀* ("He Who of old [gathered] the waters, written outside"), directing the chanter to find the full musical text in the external Heirmologion or the opening Sunday section. The hymnographer formulates a classic Pauline and patristic soteriological paradox: by accepting voluntary bonds in His incarnation and passion, Christ permanently bound the demonic adversary who had deceitfully ensnared humanity through the transgression of the commandment in Eden.

[^233]: In Ode 3 of the second Canon (to the Theotokos, Fol. 227v), the soul is likened to agricultural soil whose hardened furrows (*браздо́мъ дш҃а моеѧ̀*) must be softened and watered by tears of compunction in order to bring forth a hundredfold spiritual harvest, directly evoking the Parable of the Sower (Matthew 13:8, 23). The canon to the Cross on Fol. 228r formulates the foundational mystery of the communicatio idiomatum: Christ, Who in His divine nature is impassible (*є҆сттво́мъ бж҃твеннымъ безстра́стна*), voluntarily became passible in the flesh to bestow immortality upon mankind.

[^234]: The Heirmos of Ode 4 (*Положи́лъ ны* / *Polozhil ny*) opens the fourth ode of the Matins Canon to the Precious Cross. The subsequent troparia develop the Christological paradox of the Passion: Christ, Who in His divine majesty is above all celestial and earthly honor (*izhe chesti vsyakiya prevyshshi*), voluntarily endures blows upon His head with a reed (Matthew 27:30) and is crowned with thorns, thereby cutting down the prickly briars of ancestral sin and restoring human nature to its pristine dignity. Codicological note: quire signature `Сс г` appears at the bottom of Fol. 229r, which bears the early printed folio number `ск҃є` (225r), reflecting early misfoliation in the 1629 imprint.

[^235]: In Ode 5 (Fol. 230r), the hymnographer articulates the dogmatic definition of the Sixth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople III, 680–681) regarding the two natural operations or energies in the incarnate Word: Christ, though one from the Father (*ot Otsa Tya yedinago vedushchim*), bears two distinct operations (*deystvii dve nosyashcha*) corresponding to His divine and human natures. The two streams of blood and water flowing from His pierced side (John 19:34) serve as sacramental fountains of salvation. On Fol. 229v, the prophetic metaphor of the unrolled scroll (*Svitok*) derives from Isaiah 8:1 and Ezekiel 2:9–10, wherein Christ as the living Word is inscribed by the Father's finger.

[^236]: Ode 6 (Fol. 231r) presents a vivid eucharistic martyr-theology: the holy martyrs exposed to the flames are described as "baked like bread and offered upon the table unto God the King of all" (*yakozhe khleb ispetsayemi, i Bogu vsekh tsaryu na trapezu prinosimi*), directly reflecting the ancient patristic imagery of St. Ignatius of Antioch (*Epistle to the Romans*, IV). In the Canon to the Cross, Christ's voluntary piercing with the lance reverses the wound of the serpent in Eden, bestowing upon humanity "the ancient entrance" (*drevniy vkhod*) to Paradise.

[^237]: In Ode 7 (Fol. 232r), the hymnographer contrasts the cosmic convulsions attending Christ's crucifixion (the quaking of the earth, splitting of the rocks, and eclipse of the sun; Matthew 27:51) with the inner spiritual drama of repentance. The penitent petitions the Mother of God to dry up the "murky rivers of evil deeds" (*mutnyya reki lukavykh moikh deianiy*) through "streams of compunction" (*struyami umileniya*), that he may be directed toward the eschatological "waters of release" (*k vodam oslableniya*) at the dread judgment.

[^238]: Ode 8 (Fol. 233r) links the Piercing of the Side (John 19:34) with the revocation of the Edenic judgment: the turning flaming sword guarding the gates of Eden (Genesis 3:24) turns its back (*obrashchayushcheyesya nany oruzhiye, pleshchi dayet*), put to shame by the soldier's lance. The maternal lament of the Theotokos beneath the Cross (*nebes-chadnu yavi mya* — "make me not childless") underscores the reality of her maternal grief, addressing her Son Who preserved her virginal integrity even in childbirth.

[^239]: In Ode 9 (Fol. 234r), the hymnographer interprets the Parable of the Lost Coin (Luke 15:8–10) in an original Staurotheological synthesis: Christ is the seeker Who kindles the candle of His own sacred flesh upon the Tree of the Cross (*vzheg na kreste svoyu plot*) to find and recover the lost drachma of fallen humanity in the darkness of the world, thereby bringing joy to the celestial hosts. Quire signature `Тт д` marks Fol. 234r (printed `сл` / 230r).

[^240]: Ode 9 of the second canon (Fol. 235r) incorporates two distinct scriptural metaphors: the Pauline athletic exhortation to strengthen enfeebled hands and palsied knees for the spiritual race (Hebrews 12:12; Isaiah 35:3), and the moralized allegory of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30), wherein the sinner is ambushed by "spiritual thieves" (*razboyniki myslennyya*) who strip him of the garment of virtues. Quire `Уу` commences on Fol. 235r (printed `сла` / 231r).

[^241]: The Wednesday Matins Exapostilarion (*Oruzhiye nepobedimoye*, Fol. 235v) acclaims the Life-giving Cross with an elevated fourfold ecclesiological title: "the invincible weapon and confirmation of the faith, the great refuge and boast of Christians, the glory of the Orthodox, and the salvation of the faithful." The accompanying Aposticha stichera (Fol. 236r) pair the salvific Tree of Golgotha with the Edenic tree of disobedience, invoking Psalm 4:7 LXX (*Znamenasya na nas svet litsa Tvoyego*).

[^242]: The Beatitudes (*Blazhenna*) of Wednesday Matins (Fol. 236v–237r) alternate the evangelical verses of Matthew 5:3–12 with stauro-theological troparia. The hymnographer sets the Good Thief's confession (*razboynika zhe shchedre ispovedavsha Tya na kreste*) against Adam's transgression and expulsion from Eden, acclaiming Christ Who was numbered with the transgressors (Isaiah 53:12) and crowned with thorns in order to root out the thorns of the ancestral curse (Genesis 3:18).

[^243]: Codicological and modal rubric: On Wednesday evening (Fol. 237v), Vespers inaugurates the liturgical commemoration of Thursday (the Holy Apostles and St. Nicholas of Myra). The catalog of Apostles on Fol. 238r enumerates the Twelve, the Evangelists, and the broader synaxis of the Seventy.

[^244]: The Thursday Aposticha stichera in Tone 4 (Fol. 239r) celebrate the transformation of the apostles by the Holy Spirit at Pentecost: Christ revealed "unlettered disciples as teachers" (*neknizhnyya ucheniki kazatelya yavi*) who by their divinely inspired tongues overturned pagan delusion. The hymn draws upon the biblical witness of Acts 4:13 (*anthropoi agrammatoi eisin kai idiotai*), where the apostolic proclamation confounds worldly philosophy. Codicological note: quire signature `Ф҃ ф҃` marks the lower margin of Fol. 239r, which bears the printed folio number `сл҃є` (235r).

[^245]: On Fol. 239v, the concluding Martyrion of the Aposticha applies sacrificial and pastoral imagery to the martyrs: they are "living victims, rational whole-burnt offerings" (*zhertvy odushevlennyya, vsesozhzheniya slovesnyya*; cf. Romans 12:1) and sheep pastured "beside the water of rest" (*na vode pokoishchiney*; Psalm 22:2 LXX). Fol. 240r typifies Moses' passage through the Red Sea (Exodus 14) as a prophetical figure for the apostles cleaving the tumultuous waters of pagan polytheism and guiding the nations to the unoriginate Word. Quire signature `Ф҃ ф҃ в҃` appears at the foot of Fol. 240r (printed `сл҃ѕ` / 236r).

[^246]: The Matins Canon to the Holy Apostles in Tone 4 (Fol. 241r) opens with Ode 1 to the heirmos *Morya chermnaya* (*The Red Sea*). The hymnographer deploys the classic pneumatological metaphor of the apostles as "divine instruments of the Comforter" (*Bozhestvennyya Uteshitelya organy* / ὄργανα τοῦ Παρακλήτου), resonating with the breath of the Holy Spirit to chant a saving song. In the subsequent troparion, the hymnographer addresses the apostles as "eyewitnesses of Christ" (*Khristovy samovidtsy* / αὐτόпται τοῦ Χριστοῦ; Luke 1:2), begging their healing visitation for a soul paralyzed on the bed of sloth. [^247]: Fol. 241v introduces the second canon of Thursday Matins, dedicated to St. Nicholas of Myra. Fol. 242r develops the ecclesiological typology of Psalm 45:5 LXX (*The streams of the river make glad the city of God*): the apostles are revealed as spiritual rivers whose teachings gladden the Church. Quire signature `Ф҃ ф҃ д҃` appears on Fol. 242r (printed `сл҃и` / 238r).

[^248]: In Ode 3 of the second canon (Fol. 242v), the hymnographer invokes the Lycian hagiographical tradition of St. Nicholas demolishing the pagan temple and statues of Artemis (*Artemide bezdushnyya kumiry*), transposing this historical action into an allegorical petition to destroy the soul's internal idols of passion. Ode 4 (Fol. 243r) interprets Habakkuk 3:8/15 (*Thou didst ride upon Thy horses...*) by acclaiming the apostles as Christ's "elect steeds" troubling the stormy waters of error, and as the sharpened arrows of the Mighty One (Psalm 44:6 LXX; 119:4 LXX). The Theotokion bridging Fol. 256r and 256v sets the mystery of the Incarnation into high dogmatic relief, juxtaposing Christ as the "new Child" (*novo otrocha*) with His eternal identity as the Danielic "Ancient of Days" (*Vetkhi denmi*; Daniel 7:9). Fol. 256r is signed `Ч҃ ч҃ в҃` (printed `сн҃в` / 252r).

The troparia celebrate the martyrs who put to shame the impious tormentors and arrived at the unwaning light of Christ.

[^274]: Fol. 268r continues Saturday Matins with troparia celebrating the holy women who overcame the demonic dragon by their manly ascetic struggles (*trudy muzheskimi*). Signature `Ъ҃ Ъ҃ в҃` on Fol. 268r (printed `с҃ѯд` / 264r) marks the second signed recto of quire `Ъ`.

The troparia celebrate the martyrs who by their fortitude preserved the "divine sheep of Christ" and the holy women who joined the celestial choir before the Mother of God.

[^276]: Fol. 269v–270r contains Ode 4 of the Saturday Matins Canons in Tone 6, chanted to the heirmoi *Uslyshakh Gospodi slukh tvoy* (*I heard, O Lord, Thy report, and was afraid; I considered Thy works and was astonished*; Habakkuk 3:2 LXX) and *Tymi Khriste Gospod* (*Thou art my Lord, O Christ, Thou art my strength*). The hymnography compares the holy ascetics to glowing coals (*ugliye*) consumed by love for God that burn away the material passions of this world.

Fol. 271v contains an expansive eschatological supplication asking Christ to settle the faithful departed in the bosom of Abraham (Luke 16:22) where the voice of those who feast is sweet (Psalm 41:5 LXX). Fol. 272r (printed `с҃ѯи` / 268r) is signed `Ы҃ Ы҃ в҃`, the second signed recto of quire `Ы`.

Jonah 2:3) and *Svyatii otrotsy* / *Deti yevreyskiya* (*The holy Children in the furnace...*; Daniel 3). Fol. 272v features a vivid Anastasis troparion describing Christ tearing apart the belly of hostile death and releasing all who were bound in Hades. Fol. 273r (printed `с҃ѯѳ` / 269r) is signed `Ы҃ Ы҃ г҃`.

[^280]: Fol. 273v–274r carries Ode 8 of the Saturday Matins Canons in Tone 6, chanted to the heirmoi *Svyatii otrotsy v Vavylone* (*The holy children in Babylon, burning with divine zeal...*; Daniel 3) and *Muchenicheskym* (*In the martyrs' fire...*). Fol. 276v features the celebrated Hymn for the Departed (Поко́инъ) contrasting the poison of the ancient serpent in Eden which brought universal death upon Adam's line with the life-giving advent of Christ Who trampled death. Fol. 277r (printed `с҃ог` / 273r) is signed `Ь҃ Ь҃ г҃`.

[^299]: Physical leaf 295 constitutes the lower (rear) outer binding board of the codex.
