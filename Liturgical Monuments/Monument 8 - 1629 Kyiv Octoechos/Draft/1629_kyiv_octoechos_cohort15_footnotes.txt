[^144]: Folios 138v–139r conclude Ode 6 of the Tone 6 Sunday Matins Canons and open Ode 7. The Theotokion of Ode 6 on Fol. 139r compiles the classical Old Testament tabernacle types of the Virgin from Exodus and Hebrews 9:4: the Ark of the Covenant (*ковче́гъ* / κιβωτός), the Table of Showbread (*тра́пеза* / τράпеζα), the golden Candlestick (*свѣ́щникъ* / λυχνία), and the golden Jar of Manna (*рꙋ́чка* / στάмνος). At the bottom of Fol. 139r, the Heirmos of Ode 7 (*Хладода́вцꙋ ᲂу҆́бѡ пе́щь* / *Δροσοβόλον μὲν τὴν κάμινον*) exhibits the curious spelling *ᲂу҆бѣ́йца* (for *ᲂу҆бѣди́въ* / *ᲂу҆бѣжда́ѧ*, persuading the tyrant to cry: "Blessed is the God of our fathers").
[^145]: Folios 139v–140r present the troparia of Ode 7 across the three Sunday Matins canons. In the first canon, the eclipse of the sun at the Crucifixion (Matthew 27:45) is coupled with Adam's ecstatic dancing (*ли́кѡствꙋѧ и҆гра́ше*) upon the Harrowing of Hades. On Fol. 140r, the Theotokion of the Cross-Resurrection canon provides a strict Chalcedonian christological formula: the Virgin bore One of the Trinity (*Є҆дина́го ѿ Тро́ицы*) Who appeared upon earth "in two natures" (*водво́ю є҆стествꙋ̀* / ἐν δύο φύσεσιν). The subsequent Theotokos canon invokes the Magnificat prophecy ("all generations shall call me blessed", Luke 1:48) alongside the typological interpretation of the burning yet unconsumed Babylonian furnace (Daniel 3) as an image of the divine fire dwelling in the Virgin's womb (*бж҃е́ственный бо ѻ҃гнь тебѐ неопалѝ всели́сѧ вътѧ̀*).
[^146]: Folios 140v–141r conclude Ode 7 and open Ode 8 of the Tone 6 Matins canons. In the Theotokion on Fol. 140v, the Virgin is called the sanctuary (*ᲂу҆свѣти́лище*) of human nature, bearing upon her arms the Lord Who sits upon the cherubim. Ode 8 of the first canon pairs the furnace of Babylon with Elijah's sacrifice upon Mount Carmel consumed by fire from heaven despite being soaked with water (3 Kings / 1 Kings 18:38). On Fol. 141r, Christ's descent into Hades is depicted as lifting fallen man from the dung-heap (*вогнои́щи*, Psalm 112:7 / 113:7 LXX: "He raises up the poor out of the dust, and lifts the needy out of the dunghill").
[^147]: Folios 141v–142r encompass the close of Ode 8 and the opening of Ode 9 of the Sunday Matins canons. In the Theotokos troparia of Ode 8 on Fol. 141v, Psalm 44:10 LXX ("At Thy right hand stood the queen in a vesture of gold") is applied to the Mother of God glorified in heaven. The theological language of Fol. 142r for Ode 9 captures the paradox of the impassible Word entering into passion (*стра́стемъ бы́въ стра́сть*, becoming the destruction of passions through His own Passion), reflecting St. John of Damascus's profound antithetical soteriology.
[^148]: Folios 142v–143r present the completion of Ode 9 across the three canons. In the first canon on Fol. 142v, the troparion cites Psalm 15:10 LXX / Acts 2:27 ("Neither wilt Thou leave My soul in Hades, neither wilt Thou suffer Thine Holy One to see corruption"). In the Cross-Resurrection canon, the Heirmos *Не рыда́й менѐ* (*Weep not for Me, O Mother*, celebrated on Holy Saturday) introduces the angel's proclamation to the myrrhbearers that Adam is raised and Hades despoiled. On Fol. 143r, Balaam's messianic oracle (Numbers 24:17: "There shall come a Star out of Jacob") is linked to Christ illuminating those held in spiritual gloom.
[^149]: Folios 143v–144r transition from the concluding troparia of Ode 9 of the Matins Canons to the Sunday Praises (*Хвали́тны*, Psalms 148–150). On Fol. 143v, the Virgin is hailed as a "divinely-woven loom" (*поста́въ бг҃отка́ненъ*, Greek ἱστός θεοΰφαντος) upon which the Word wove the tunic of His human flesh, deifying human nature. Fol. 144r presents the first four Resurrection Stichera followed by the Anatolian (*Восто́чны*) stichera, which evoke Psalm 18:6 LXX ("as a bridegroom coming out of his chamber") to celebrate Christ's triumphant exit from the sealed tomb.
[^150]: Folios 144v–145r contain the concluding Praises stichera, the rubric for the Evangelical Sticheron (*Стїхи́ра є҆ѵлїскаѧ*), and the opening of the Sunday Beatitudes (*Блаже́нны*) of Tone 6. In the upper margin of Fol. 145r, the folio numeral is typographically misprinted as *рм҃д* (144) instead of *рм҃є* (145), repeating the folio number of the previous recto. The polemical stichera on Fol. 144v challenge the Sanhedrin and soldiers over the empty tomb (Matthew 27:62–66, 28:11–15): "Either produce Him as dead, or worship Him as God" (*и҆лѝ ꙗ҆́кѡ ме́ртва предста́вите , и҆лѝ ꙗ҆́кѡ бг҃ꙋ поклони́тесѧ*).
[^151]: Folios 145v–146r complete the Sunday Beatitudes and open Sunday Evening Vespers (*Внеде́лю ве́черъ*) of Tone 6, which initiates the weekly liturgical commemorations (Sunday evening honoring the Bodiless Heavenly Hosts for Monday). On Fol. 146r, a notable 17th-century codicological feature appears: a printed slip has been pasted over the heading rubric to supply the missing model-melody indicator (*Подо́бенъ*), preserving `[...а́ггл]а́скїѧ пред̾идꙋ́тъ силы` (*The angelic hosts go before*). The second penitential sticheron incorporates the gospel parable of the barren fig tree (Luke 13:6–9, Matthew 21:19) as a warning against unfruitfulness and moral insensibility.
[^152]: Folios 146v–147r encompass the concluding Vespers stichera for the Bodiless Hosts and the opening of the Aposticha (*На стїхо́внѣ*). In the third angelic sticheron on Fol. 147r, the heavenly bodiless ranks are described as "formless and immaterial minds" (*безѡ҆́бразны , и҆ невеще́ственны ᲂу҆мы̀*, reflecting the Dionysian angelic hierarchy of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite), created by the Father with the Word and the Spirit to hymn the thrice-radiant glory. The opening Aposticha sticheron draws upon the solemn dread of the Last Judgment and Christ's rejection of the unvigilant in Matthew 25:12 ("I know you not").
[^153]: Folios 147v–148r mark the conclusion of Tone 6 and the monumental opening of Tone 7 (Grave Tone / *Гласъ Седмый*). Fol. 148r initiates a new gathering (marked by signature `д҃ б`) with an ornate woodcut headpiece, a ligatured vyaz title (*ВЪ КНИГИ ВОСКРЕСНЫ*), and the opening Sunday Resurrection Stichera at "Lord, I have cried" (*Прїидѣ́те возра́дꙋемсѧ Го́сподеви*, based on Psalm 94:1 LXX). The hymnography celebrates the destruction of death's dominion and Christ's enlightenment of the human race in unison with the bodiless angelic powers.
