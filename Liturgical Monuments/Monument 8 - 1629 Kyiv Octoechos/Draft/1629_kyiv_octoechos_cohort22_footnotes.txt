[^214]: The Monday evening Vespers stichera (chanted at *Lord, I have cried*) initiate the liturgical celebration of Tuesday, weekly dedicated to the Holy Prophet, Forerunner, and Baptist John. Composed to the prosomoion melody *Е҆гда̀ ѿ дре́ва тя̀* (*Hote ek tou xylou se*), these hymns weave together the biblical titles of the Baptist: "seal of the prophets" (*пррⷱ҇кѡмъ печа́ть*), "voice of the Word" (*свѣ́тлыи сло́вꙋ гла́се* / Isaiah 40:3), and the Johannine proclamation of the Lamb of God taking away the sin of the world (*Сѐ а҆́гнецъ бж҃і́й* / John 1:29).

[^215]: Codicologically and liturgically, Tuesday Matins in the Octoechos contains Sessional Hymns (*Сѣда́льны*) centered upon compunction (*покаѧ́нны*) and the intercession of the Theotokos and the Baptist. Theotokion imagery on Fol. 210r draws directly upon the patristic doctrine of deification (*theosis*) formulated from 2 Peter 1:4: "Partakers of the divine nature have we become through thee" (*Бж҃тве́нномꙋ є҆стествꙋ̀ бы́хомъ прича́стници*), asserting that through the Incarnation of God from the Virgin, humanity is elevated into communion with the divine life.

[^216]: The Sessional Hymn following the third Kathisma versification contrasts the repentance of King David with the inertia of the soul. David's profound contrition over two specific transgressions—the adultery with Bathsheba and the engineered death of Uriah the Hittite (2 Samuel 11–12)—manifested in drenching his couch with tears (Psalm 6:6 LXX). The hymnographer laments that while David wept bitterly for two sins, the sinner remains dry-eyed and callous despite accumulating countless offenses.

[^217]: The first ode of the Canon to the Forerunner introduces the classical heirmos *Воглꙋбинѣ̀ потопѝ дре́вле* ("Of old He drowned in the deep"), referring to the destruction of Pharaoh's host in the Red Sea (Exodus 14). Troparia on Fol. 212r present the typological paradox of the baptism of Christ: the Forerunner, a created mortal, immersed into the waters of the Jordan Him Who is the unsearchable "Abyss" (*бе́зднꙋ*) of divine essence, thereby drowning the ancient flood of human transgression.

[^218]: The third ode of the Canon of Compunction incorporates the Pauline doctrine of the body as the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:19). The hymnographer prays that through the intercession of the Baptist—who resides in the heavenly sanctuary—believers may themselves be fashioned into living temples (*хра́мы и҆ на́съ сотворѝ дх҃ꙋ ст҃о́мꙋ*). The ode concludes with the classic Byzantine hymnographic antithesis: the Virgin mother suckles Him Who nourishes the entire cosmos (*дои́ши ма́теролѣ́пно, пита́теля всѣ́мъ*).

[^219]: Ode 4 of the Canon of Compunction employs the Habakkuk theophany heirmos (*И҆́же ѿ дв҃ы твоѐ ржⷭ҇тво*), based on Habakkuk 3:2–3 ("God shall come from Teman, and the Holy One from the thick and shady mountain of Paran"). In the Canon to the Forerunner on Fol. 213v, an explicit ktetorik petition is incorporated: the hymnographer petitions the Baptist on behalf of those who built and lovingly maintain his sacred temple (*и҆́же любо́вїю тѝ созда́ннаго бж҃тве́ннаго хра́ма*), beseeching that they be granted eternal life in the heavenly dwellings.

[^220]: Codicological Note: At the bottom margin of Fol. 215r (Page 217 Right), quire signature *Оо* (Quire 46) begins, demonstrating that the preceding quire *Нн* (Quire 45) was printed as a regular four-leaf gathering (bifolium fold, folios 211–214). The hymnography on this leaf emphasizes the Baptist's courageous defense of divine law before Herod Antipas, which led to his unlawful execution (*беззако́нно закала́ешися*, Matthew 14:1–12).

[^221]: Ode 5 of the Canon of Compunction incorporates the prophetic vision of Daniel 2:34–35, 45, in which a stone cut without hands from a great mountain smashes the pagan statue of composite metals—an established patristic type of Christ's virgin birth from the Theotokos shattering the idols of demonology. In the second canon, the Baptist is characterized as standing at the threshold of salvation history (*посредѣ̀ зако́на и҆ блгⷭ҇ти ста̀*), linking the Old Covenant Law with New Testament grace.

[^222]: In Ode 6, the Theotokion on Fol. 217r portrays the Virgin as the mystical Table bearing the heavenly Bread of Life (*Ꙗ҆́кѡ тра́пеза хлѣ́бъ вмѣсти́вши та́йный*), directly evoking Christ's discourse in John 6:32–51. The martyrikon troparia on Fol. 216v offer an epigraphic witness to the varied torments endured by early Christian martyrs, explicitly recording gouging of eyes (*ѻ҆́чїи и҆збода́еми*) and exposure to freezing winter frost (*померза́еми стꙋде́нїи лю́тѣ*), imagery characteristic of the Passion of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste.

[^223]: The Heirmos of Ode 7 (*Херꙋві́момъ подо́бящеся дѣ́ти*) introduces the Canticle of the Three Holy Youths in the Babylonian furnace, drawing directly upon the Prayer of Azariah (Daniel 3:27–28 LXX: "For Thou art righteous in all that Thou hast done to us... for we have sinned"). The penitential troparion on Fol. 217v invokes David's classic confession from Psalm 50:5 LXX (*Во беззако́нїихъ гдⷭ҇и сѐ зача́тъ є҆́смь* / "Behold, in iniquities was I conceived"), anchoring the Tuesday morning compunction office in Davidic repentance.
