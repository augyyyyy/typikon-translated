[^224]: The Heirmos of Ode 8 (*В кꙋпи́нѣ мѡѷсе́и дв҃ческое чꙋ́до*) links the Old Testament theophany of the unburnt bush on Mount Sinai (Exodus 3:2) with the virginal conception and birth of Christ without corruption. In the preceding Theotokion (Fol. 219r), the hymnographer unites this typological Marian imagery with the Johannine witness: the infant John the Baptist, while still in the barren womb of Elizabeth, recognizes the incarnate Word in the Virgin's womb and leaps for joy (*бж҃тве́ннымъ взыгра́нїемъ* / Luke 1:41–44), thereby acting as the first prophet to confess Christ in the flesh.

[^225]: The Theotokion on Fol. 219v explicitly proclaims the patristic doctrine of deification (*theosis*), derived from 2 Peter 1:4 and St. Athanasius of Alexandria: "That God might deify us, having become incarnate of thy pure blood, and become earthly" (*Да̀ ѡ҆божи́тъ на́съ бг҃ъ, вопло́щся и҆́зъ чи́стыхъ кро́вій твои́хъ, и҆ бы́сть зе́менъ*). In the second canon to the Forerunner, the hymnographer petitions the Baptist—who physically laid his right hand upon the head of the Lord in the Jordan—to stretch forth that same right hand to lift up the sinner fallen in spiritual inertia.

[^226]: Ode 9 of Tuesday Matins combines two seminal biblical exemplars of repentance: the conversion of King Manasseh (*и҆́же манасі́ю ѡ҆браще́й согрѣши́вшаго*), whose apocryphal penitential prayer (2 Chronicles 33 / Odes 12 LXX) serves as a cornerstone of Byzantine compunction, and the nameless sinful woman / harlot (*спасы́й блꙋдни́цꙋ*) who washed Christ's feet with tears (Luke 7:36–50). The hymnographer models his prayer upon their contrition, begging for a continuous stream of tears to wash away his spiritual stains before the dread judgment-seat.

[^227]: In Ode 9 of the Canon to the Forerunner (Fol. 221v), the Baptist is acclaimed through a triad of botanical and aromatic biblical metaphors: the fragrant rose (*шипо́къ бл҃гово́ненъ*), the fragrant cypress (*кѵпари́съ* / Sirach 50:10), and the unfading lily (*неꙋвяда́емый крі́нъ* / Song of Songs 2:1–2), alongside honorable myrrh (*мѵ́ро чⷭ҇тно*), reflecting his ascetic purity in the Judean wilderness. In the subsequent troparion, an explicit ktetorik petition is offered for those who lovingly keep choir in the Baptist's sacred temple (*въ сщ҃тве́ннѣмъ хра́мѣ твое́мъ ликꙋ́ющимъ*), asking for divine deliverance and amendment of life.

[^228]: The weekday office on Tuesday concludes with the singing of the Beatitudes (*Блаже́нна*), a standard feature of the Studite and Ruthenian Octoechos tradition wherein the evangelical verses (Matthew 5:3–12) are interspersed with penitential, martyrial, and Johannine troparia. The concluding Triadikon (*Безнача́льный трⷪ҇ци тебѣ̀ покланя́емся*) provides an explicit dogmatic formula confessing the Holy Trinity as a simple, unoriginate, and tri-hypostatic essence (*є҆сттво́мъ тя̀ зна́я, є҆дини́цꙋ про́стꙋ и҆ трїѵпоста́внꙋ*), guarding Nicene-Constantinopolitan orthodoxy against Arian and Sabellian errors.

[^229]: On Tuesday evening, the liturgical cycle shifts from the commemoration of the Baptist to Wednesday, dedicated weekly throughout the Byzantine year to the Precious and Life-giving Cross and the betrayal and passion of Christ. The stichera at *Lord, I have cried* (Fol. 223v–224r) present the theological antithesis between Adam's condemnation at the tree of knowledge and his redemption through Christ's lifting up upon the Tree of the Cross under the legal curse (Deuteronomy 21:23; Galatians 3:13). The accompanying Staurotheotokion captures the maternal lament of the Virgin at Golgotha: "O my most beloved Child, whither has Thy light-bearing beauty set?"

[^230]: The Sessional Hymns of Wednesday Matins (Fol. 225r) incorporate the typological interpretation of Isaiah 60:13 ("The glory of Lebanon shall come unto thee, the fir tree, the pine tree, and the box together, to beautify the place of my sanctuary"), traditionally understood in Byzantine hymnography as the three woods of Christ's Cross: cypress, pine, and cedar (*На кипари́сѣ и҆ пе́ѵгѣ и҆ ке́дрѣ*). The preceding Aposticha sticheron (Fol. 224v) invokes Moses stretching forth his hands upon Mount Sinai/Rephidim in the cruciform sign to vanquish the host of Amalek (Exodus 17:8–16).

[^231]: Codicological & Rubrical Note: The rubric *По г҃ каѳі́ Сѣ̃ гл҃ по кра̃* (*Po 3 kathismi Sedalen glagolemy po krayegranesiyu*) indicates that following the third Kathisma versification, the sessional hymn is chanted in Tone 2 according to the poetic refrain or alphabetic/acrostic ordering. The accompanying Staurotheotokion on Fol. 226r portrays the Mother of God witnessing the voluntary humiliation and crucifixion of her Son, appealing to the boundless "bowels of mercies" (*за оу҆тро́бꙋ мл҃тей*) of the Redeemer Who takes away the sin of the world.

[^232]: The Matins Canon to the Precious Cross on Wednesday opens with the heirmos indication *И҆́же во́ды дре́вле, писа́нъ внѣ̀* ("He Who of old [gathered] the waters, written outside"), directing the chanter to find the full musical text in the external Heirmologion or the opening Sunday section. The hymnographer formulates a classic Pauline and patristic soteriological paradox: by accepting voluntary bonds in His incarnation and passion, Christ permanently bound the demonic adversary who had deceitfully ensnared humanity through the transgression of the commandment in Eden.

[^233]: In Ode 3 of the second Canon (to the Theotokos, Fol. 227v), the soul is likened to agricultural soil whose hardened furrows (*браздо́мъ дш҃а моеѧ̀*) must be softened and watered by tears of compunction in order to bring forth a hundredfold spiritual harvest, directly evoking the Parable of the Sower (Matthew 13:8, 23). The canon to the Cross on Fol. 228r formulates the foundational mystery of the communicatio idiomatum: Christ, Who in His divine nature is impassible (*є҆сттво́мъ бж҃твеннымъ безстра́стна*), voluntarily became passible in the flesh to bestow immortality upon mankind.
