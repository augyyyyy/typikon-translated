[^234]: The Heirmos of Ode 4 (*Положи́лъ ны* / *Polozhil ny*) opens the fourth ode of the Matins Canon to the Precious Cross. The subsequent troparia develop the Christological paradox of the Passion: Christ, Who in His divine majesty is above all celestial and earthly honor (*izhe chesti vsyakiya prevyshshi*), voluntarily endures blows upon His head with a reed (Matthew 27:30) and is crowned with thorns, thereby cutting down the prickly briars of ancestral sin and restoring human nature to its pristine dignity. Codicological note: quire signature `Сс г` appears at the bottom of Fol. 229r, which bears the early printed folio number `ск҃є` (225r), reflecting early misfoliation in the 1629 imprint.

[^235]: In Ode 5 (Fol. 230r), the hymnographer articulates the dogmatic definition of the Sixth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople III, 680–681) regarding the two natural operations or energies in the incarnate Word: Christ, though one from the Father (*ot Otsa Tya yedinago vedushchim*), bears two distinct operations (*deystvii dve nosyashcha*) corresponding to His divine and human natures. The two streams of blood and water flowing from His pierced side (John 19:34) serve as sacramental fountains of salvation. On Fol. 229v, the prophetic metaphor of the unrolled scroll (*Svitok*) derives from Isaiah 8:1 and Ezekiel 2:9–10, wherein Christ as the living Word is inscribed by the Father's finger.

[^236]: Ode 6 (Fol. 231r) presents a vivid eucharistic martyr-theology: the holy martyrs exposed to the flames are described as "baked like bread and offered upon the table unto God the King of all" (*yakozhe khleb ispetsayemi, i Bogu vsekh tsaryu na trapezu prinosimi*), directly reflecting the ancient patristic imagery of St. Ignatius of Antioch (*Epistle to the Romans*, IV). In the Canon to the Cross, Christ's voluntary piercing with the lance reverses the wound of the serpent in Eden, bestowing upon humanity "the ancient entrance" (*drevniy vkhod*) to Paradise.

[^237]: In Ode 7 (Fol. 232r), the hymnographer contrasts the cosmic convulsions attending Christ's crucifixion (the quaking of the earth, splitting of the rocks, and eclipse of the sun; Matthew 27:51) with the inner spiritual drama of repentance. The penitent petitions the Mother of God to dry up the "murky rivers of evil deeds" (*mutnyya reki lukavykh moikh deianiy*) through "streams of compunction" (*struyami umileniya*), that he may be directed toward the eschatological "waters of release" (*k vodam oslableniya*) at the dread judgment.

[^238]: Ode 8 (Fol. 233r) links the Piercing of the Side (John 19:34) with the revocation of the Edenic judgment: the turning flaming sword guarding the gates of Eden (Genesis 3:24) turns its back (*obrashchayushcheyesya nany oruzhiye, pleshchi dayet*), put to shame by the soldier's lance. The maternal lament of the Theotokos beneath the Cross (*nebes-chadnu yavi mya* — "make me not childless") underscores the reality of her maternal grief, addressing her Son Who preserved her virginal integrity even in childbirth.

[^239]: In Ode 9 (Fol. 234r), the hymnographer interprets the Parable of the Lost Coin (Luke 15:8–10) in an original Staurotheological synthesis: Christ is the seeker Who kindles the candle of His own sacred flesh upon the Tree of the Cross (*vzheg na kreste svoyu plot*) to find and recover the lost drachma of fallen humanity in the darkness of the world, thereby bringing joy to the celestial hosts. Quire signature `Тт д` marks Fol. 234r (printed `сл` / 230r).

[^240]: Ode 9 of the second canon (Fol. 235r) incorporates two distinct scriptural metaphors: the Pauline athletic exhortation to strengthen enfeebled hands and palsied knees for the spiritual race (Hebrews 12:12; Isaiah 35:3), and the moralized allegory of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30), wherein the sinner is ambushed by "spiritual thieves" (*razboyniki myslennyya*) who strip him of the garment of virtues. Quire `Уу` commences on Fol. 235r (printed `сла` / 231r).

[^241]: The Wednesday Matins Exapostilarion (*Oruzhiye nepobedimoye*, Fol. 235v) acclaims the Life-giving Cross with an elevated fourfold ecclesiological title: "the invincible weapon and confirmation of the faith, the great refuge and boast of Christians, the glory of the Orthodox, and the salvation of the faithful." The accompanying Aposticha stichera (Fol. 236r) pair the salvific Tree of Golgotha with the Edenic tree of disobedience, invoking Psalm 4:7 LXX (*Znamenasya na nas svet litsa Tvoyego*).

[^242]: The Beatitudes (*Blazhenna*) of Wednesday Matins (Fol. 236v–237r) alternate the evangelical verses of Matthew 5:3–12 with stauro-theological troparia. The hymnographer sets the Good Thief's confession (*razboynika zhe shchedre ispovedavsha Tya na kreste*) against Adam's transgression and expulsion from Eden, acclaiming Christ Who was numbered with the transgressors (Isaiah 53:12) and crowned with thorns in order to root out the thorns of the ancestral curse (Genesis 3:18).

[^243]: Codicological and modal rubric: On Wednesday evening (Fol. 237v), Vespers inaugurates the liturgical commemoration of Thursday (the Holy Apostles and St. Nicholas of Myra). The rubric explicitly directs the stichera to the Apostles at *Lord, I have cried* to be chanted in Tone 4 according to the prosomoion *Yako dobla* (*Os gennaion en martysin*, `гла д, Подо́бенъ, Ꙗ҆кодо́бла`), even though the Octoechos remains situated within the weekly Tone 3 section. The catalog of Apostles on Fol. 238r enumerates the Twelve, the Evangelists, and the broader synaxis of the Seventy.
