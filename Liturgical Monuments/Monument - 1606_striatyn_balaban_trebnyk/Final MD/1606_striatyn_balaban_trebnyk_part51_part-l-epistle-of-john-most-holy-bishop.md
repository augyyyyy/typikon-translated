# PART L: EPISTLE OF JOHN, MOST HOLY BISHOP OF KITROS, UNTO THE MOST SACRED BISHOP OF DYRRACHIUM, CABASILAS: ON THE DISPENSATION OF FOODS IN THE HANDED-DOWN WEEKS, AND WHICH THESE ARE [^3889]

We dispense therefore Wednesday and Friday of the New Week, reckoning this entire week as one single day, and rendering it nothing less than Sunday in radiance; therefore also on every day, at Matins, the reading of the Resurrection Gospels takes place.[^3890] We dispense also the Twelve Days, for the repulse[^3891] of the Armenian fast;[^3892] for these men, fasting the twelve days before Theophany, celebrate thus three feasts combined on the fifth of the month of January: the Annunciation, I say, the Nativity of Christ, and the Baptism; and even more so, not openly in some manner, but in shadowy guise, holding forth the ruinous and defiled glory of their heresiarch, and the name blasphemous unto Him, asserting therefrom that the dispensation of the Word of God was in appearance and not in truth.[^3893]

We dispense also the entire week before Meatfare, deeming another crafty custom of those same Armenians overthrown thereby; for[^3894] they fast throughout this [week] also on account of the loss of a dog, whose namesake was Artzivourion, badly going before and proclaiming in cities or villages the coming of their teacher Sergius; for Artzivourion in the Armenian tongue means "messenger"; which [dog], when Sergius wept for him having been devoured by wild beasts, he commanded all the Armenians to fast during all those days with total abstinence unto the end, and to lament fittingly and mourn;[^3895] even though, craftily concealing the abomination, they strive to accomplish it in remembrance of the Ninevite fast.[^3896]

Throughout the entire week before Cheesefare, the so-called[^3897] Middle-Fasters have a great fast; and for the refutation of them all, we dispense the fasting days equally with the non-fasting days.

From Renewal [Week] even unto Pentecost, on Wednesdays and Fridays, we do not praise fasting until the ninth [hour]; but even if we reject the eating of cheese and meat out of reverence for the Apostolic Canon, yet we receive oil and fish;[^3898] for on these days, even though making remembrance of the Lord's Crucifixion, we sing troparia suitable to the Crucifixion, yet we also join the Resurrectional [hymns] unto them, as also on others we intermingle penitential [hymns].[^3899]

The same do we also at the feast of the Transfiguration, in the midst of the fasting days[^3900] it being in the midst of the fasting days; and at all the universal festivals of the Mother of God, if they fall on a Wednesday or on a Friday; and also of the honorable Forerunner and Baptist, and of the foremost Apostles Peter and Paul, and of any other saint who is celebrated and radiant.[^3901]

If, however, Mid-Pentecost fall on Wednesday, we are accustomed indeed to sing the Resurrectional troparia, and not those of the Cross; yet we do not make a dispensation of meat or cheese; nor do we read the Resurrection Gospel at Matins, as [we do] on the Wednesday and Friday of the New Week, and for this reason neither do we eat meat thereon.[^3902]

Nor yet on the Wednesday before the Ascension; for although we sing the Resurrection Canon thereon, yet with it we also combine the Forefeast hymns of the Ascension, and neither do we read the Resurrection Gospel at Matins.[^3903]

Whenever therefore on Wednesday or Friday honorable feasts occur, it is meet to observe both the joyful character thereof and the reverence of the fast in the single dispensation of oil and fish; for in this manner wilt thou find all our Fathers to have walked.[^3904]

We dispense also the entire week after Pentecost in honor of the Holy Spirit, even as [we dispense] the other in honor of our Savior[^3905] Jesus Christ; for the Holy Spirit is equally honorable, even as unto the Father, so also unto the Son; for by Them, through the Father's good pleasure, the mystery of our edification was ordered, and the illumination of the knowledge of God shone forth unto us.[^3906]

If the Eve of any Master's feast happen to fall on Wednesday or on Friday, it is not fitting to dispense the fast in the evening for the sake of the morrow's feast.[^3907]

He who dispenses the fast of Wednesday and Friday on account of bodily weakness shall be judged according to what the sixty-ninth Apostolic Canon prescribes; but he who is constrained by weakness may comfort the body with oil and[^3908] fish; but as for meat and cheese, neither in the Great Forty Days shall he be forgiven to touch them, even if he be drawing his last breath; nor on any Wednesday or Friday. For when we dispense these, lest we be thought to share the mindset of the heterodox, eating both meat and cheese as an armor of intention—and especially if we happen to dwell among such—it is not unto these appetites that we indulge the belly![^3909]

On fasting days, from the ninth hour, after the customary prayers, with bread only and water, and vegetables[^3910] or figs, or dates, do we satisfy bodily necessity; unless some illness distress him. For since even on the day before the Holy Theophany, it is commanded to fast until after the evening psalmody and the divine hierarchical service joined thereto, when we are accustomed to come unto bodily food: let it be without partaking of oil and fish.[^3911] For if this day—I say, the Eve of the Holy Theophany—happen to fall on a Sunday, Theophilus of Alexandria commands to partake of figs only, or dates, so much as to preserve ourselves from appearing[^3912] to fast on Sunday: how then, if it fall upon another day, shall we touch richer food?

If, however, the day of the Holy Theophany itself fall on a Wednesday or on a Friday: it is sufficient for us to dispense the fast prescribed on such days, according to the sixty-ninth Canon of the Holy Apostles, partaking of oil and fish; but to eat meat or cheese is the invention of those who manifest belly-madness, and not the command of Apostolic legislation. For the Kingdom of God is not food and drink, and the rest.[^3913]

### Notes

[^3889]: Canonical epistle of John, Bishop of Kitros (Ἰωάννης ἐπίσκοπος Κίτρους, 13th c.), addressed to Constantine Cabasilas (Κωνσταντῖνος Καβάσιλας), Bishop of Dyrrachium (modern Durrës, Albania; Slavonic ДѪ́ЧЬ / Дрѧчь) and later Archbishop of Ohrid. Preserved in Byzantine canonical collections (Syntagma of Rhalles and Potles, V, 403–420; PG 119) and Slavonic Nomocanons / Trebnyks, this canonical ruling defines the authentic Orthodox discipline regarding the fast-free weeks (раздрѣше́нїе бра́шенъ въ седми́цахъ) and refutes sectarian distortions, particularly those of the Armenians and Middle-Fasters.
[^3890]: Dispensation of Bright Week (Renewal Week, Διακαινήσιμος ἑβδομάς). In Byzantine and Ruthenian typika, the entire octave of Pascha is mystically celebrated as a single continuous radiant Day of the Lord (μιας οὔσης ἡμέρας), during which fasting on Wednesday and Friday is wholly suspended. As a liturgical witness to this unceasing festal unity, the Matins service on each day of Bright Week includes the chanting of the Paschal canon and the solemn reading of the eleven Resurrection Gospels in succession.
[^3891]: The Twelve Days of Christmastide (the Dodekahemeron, Δωδεκαήμερον), spanning from the Nativity of Christ (December 25) to the Eve of Theophany (January 5). During this twelve-day period, fasting on Wednesdays and Fridays is suspended in honor of the Incarnate Lord, with the sole exception of the Paramon (Eve) of Theophany.
[^3892]: Refutation of the non-Chalcedonian Armenian liturgical custom. The Armenian Church did not adopt the Byzantine December 25 feast of the Nativity, but observed a twelve-day preliminary fast culminating on January 5/6 with a combined solemnity uniting the Annunciation, the Nativity in the flesh, and the Theophany (Baptism) of Christ into a single feast.
[^3893]: Patristic and Byzantine canonical polemic against Armenian Monophysite and Aphthartodocetic Christology (associated with Julian of Halicarnassus and Severus of Antioch). Byzantine Orthodox canonists maintained that conflating the Nativity and the Theophany obscured the true, historical, passible reality of Christ's human flesh, reducing the Incarnation to an illusory appearance (привидѣ́нїемъ а҆ не и҆́стинною; Greek φαντασίᾳ καὶ οὐκ ἀληθείᾳ).
[^3894]: Dispensation of the week before Meatfare (the Week of the Publican and Pharisee, ten weeks before Pascha). In the Orthodox Church, Wednesday and Friday of this week are made entirely fast-free specifically to overthrow and dissociate the faithful from the preliminary fast of the Armenians.
[^3895]: Byzantine polemical etiology of the Armenian fast known as *Arachavorats* (Առաջավորաց պահք, "Preliminary Fast" or "Fast of the Precursors"). Byzantine controversialists (including Euthymios Zigabenos, Balsamon, and John of Kitros) claimed that this fast was instituted by an Armenian monk named Sergius to mourn the death of his messenger dog Artzivourion (Armenian *Artsiv*, "eagle" or herald), who had been killed by wild beasts while announcing Sergius's arrival.
[^3896]: Jonah 3:5–10: the Armenian Church maintained that their preliminary fast commemorated the three-day fast of repentance observed by the Ninevites at the preaching of the Prophet Jonah. The Byzantine canonists rejected this explanation as a disingenuous cover for an unorthodox sect-specific ordinance.
[^3897]: The week before Cheesefare (Meatfare Week / Cheesefare Week) and the refutation of the Middle-Fasters (сре́дници; Greek Μεσονήστιοι / Μεσονηστίαι). These sectarian groups practiced severe fasting during the week before Cheesefare; Orthodox typika countered this by permitting dairy products, eggs, and fish on Wednesday and Friday of Cheesefare Week.
[^3898]: Apostolic Canon 69: "If any Bishop, or Presbyter, or Deacon, or Reader, or Singer, fast not the holy Forty Days of Pascha, or the Wednesday, or the Friday, let him be deposed, unless he be hindered by bodily weakness. But if he be a layman, let him be excommunicated." From Pascha to Pentecost, fasting until the ninth hour is suspended; while meat and dairy remain forbidden on Wednesdays and Fridays out of deference to Apostolic Canon 69, oil and fish are permitted in honor of the Resurrection season.
[^3899]: Hymnographic synthesis of the Pentecostarion: although the Paschal season commemorates the Lord's Crucifixion on Wednesdays and Fridays by chanting Stauroanastasima (hymns of the Cross and Resurrection), the sorrow of the Passion is transfigured by the joy of the third-day Resurrection.
[^3900]: Dispensation of fish, wine, and oil on the Great Feast of the Holy Transfiguration of our Lord (August 6), which occurs in the midst of the strict two-week Dormition Fast (August 1–14).
[^3901]: Canonical rule governing Despotic, Theotokion, and Polyeleos feasts falling on ordinary Wednesdays or Fridays throughout the year: solemn feasts of the Mother of God, the Nativity and Beheading of Saint John the Baptist, the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul (June 29), and radiant saints receive the dispensation of fish, wine, and oil.
[^3902]: Liturgical rubrics for the Feast of Mid-Pentecost (Преполове́нїе Пѧтидесѧ́тницы, celebrated on the fourth Wednesday after Pascha). While Resurrectional and festal troparia are sung instead of the penitential staurosima, meat and cheese remain strictly forbidden, and the Matins Resurrection Gospel is omitted.
[^3903]: Rubrics for the Wednesday before the Ascension (the Apodosis or Apodosis of Pascha). The full Paschal canon is chanted together with the Forefeast hymns of the Ascension; however, no Matins Resurrection Gospel is read, and the dispensation is limited to fish and oil.
[^3904]: Patristic consensus on ascetic equilibrium: the harmonious balance between celebrating festal joy and preserving the reverence due to the Lord's fast by means of the single dispensation of fish and oil.
[^3905]: Dispensation of the week following the Feast of Holy Pentecost (Trinity Week). Fasting on Wednesday and Friday is entirely relaxed in honor of the Holy and Life-Creating Spirit, exactly parallel to the dispensation of Bright Week and the Twelve Days in honor of Christ our Savior.
[^3906]: Trinitarian dogmatic theology of the Holy Spirit: the Spirit is equal in dignity and honor (є҆диночⷭ҇тенъ; Greek ὁμότιμος) with the Father and the Son, cooperating in the eternal divine counsel and the dispensational mystery of salvation.
[^3907]: Paramon (Eve) of Great Dominical Feasts (such as the Nativity of Christ or Theophany): if the Eve falls on a Wednesday or Friday, the strict fast of the Paramon may not be broken at evening prior to the liturgical celebration of the feast day itself.
[^3908]: Mitigation of fasting for the infirm under Apostolic Canon 69. A Christian suffering from severe bodily weakness is permitted the consolation of oil and fish, but presbyters and canonists are strictly forbidden from authorizing meat or cheese during the Great Fast (Lent) or on ordinary Wednesdays and Fridays, even in cases of terminal illness.
[^3909]: Confessional and pedagogical purpose of liturgical dispensations: eating meat and dairy during appointed fast-free weeks serves as a deliberate ecclesiastical demonstration of Orthodox unity against Armenian, Latin, and sectarian customs, rather than a concession to bodily gluttony or self-indulgence.
[^3910]: Traditional monastic and canonical rule for fasting days: complete abstinence from food until the ninth hour (approximately 3:00 PM), followed by xerophagy (сухояде́нїе)—partaking solely of bread, water, vegetables, and dried fruits (figs and dates).
[^3911]: Ascetic rule for the Paramon (Eve) of Holy Theophany (January 5): strict fasting until the conclusion of Vespers and the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great with the Great Blessing of Waters, after which a frugal meal of xerophagy is taken without wine, oil, or fish.
[^3912]: Canon 1 of Saint Theophilus, Patriarch of Alexandria (385–412 AD). When the Eve of Theophany falls on a Sunday, an ascetic dilemma arises: Apostolic Canon 66 forbids fasting on Sunday, yet Theophany requires preparatory fasting. Saint Theophilus resolved this by directing the faithful to partake of a few dates or figs after the Liturgy, thereby honoring the Lord's Day while preserving ascetic sobriety for the impending feast.
[^3913]: Rom. 14:17: "For the kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but righteousness, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost." When the Feast of Theophany itself falls on a Wednesday or Friday, the 69th Apostolic Canon permits the dispensation of oil and fish, but the consumption of meat and dairy remains an uncanonical innovation of gluttony.
