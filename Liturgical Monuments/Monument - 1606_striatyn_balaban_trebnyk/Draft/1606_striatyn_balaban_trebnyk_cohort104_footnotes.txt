[^2996]: Church Slavonic rubric: *Егда кр҃тъ творитъ їерей надъ страстїю недꙋга съ ст҃ымъ копїемъ* ("When the Priest makes the sign of the cross over the suffering of an infirmity with the holy spear"). In Ruthenian and Byzantine Euchologia, the liturgical spear (*kopie*, λόγχη)—customarily employed during the Proskomedia to extract the Lamb and commemorative particles from the prosphora—is utilized as an instrument of blessing over those suffering acute physical afflictions, tumors, or localized pain, making cruciform signs over the afflicted member while chanting troparia honoring the Cross and Christ's side pierced by the lance (John 19:34).
[^2997]: The troparion *Искоупилъ ны єси ѿ клѧтвы законныѧ* ("Thou hast redeemed us from the curse of the law") is the classical Apolytikion of Great and Holy Friday (Galatians 3:13), here prescribed together with the Troparion of the Pierced Side (*Копїемъ въ ребро твоѐ ѡ х҃е мой прободе́ бысть*) to invoke the healing stream of blood and water that flowed from Christ's pierced side for the life and incorruption of mankind.
[^2998]: Church Slavonic heading: *Мл҃тва, на бо́лимымъ и неспѧ́щымъ* ("Prayer for the Ailing and Sleepless"). This corresponds to the Byzantine Euchologion office *Εὐχὴ ἐπὶ ἀσθενοῦντος καὶ μὴ ὑπνοῦντος*, appointed for those suffering severe illness accompanied by intractable insomnia or delirium.
[^2999]: The prayer invokes an extensive litany of intercessors renowned for healing, monastic discipline, and victorious confession, including the three Great Hierarchs (Basil, Gregory, John Chrysostom), Cyril and Athanasius of Alexandria, Nicholas of Myra, Spyridon of Trimythous, Stephen the Protomartyr, the great military martyrs (George, Demetrius, Theodore the Stratelates), and the anchorites of the Egyptian and Palestinian deserts (Antony, Euthymius, Sabbas, Theodosius, Onuphrius).
[^3000]: Church Slavonic: *а҆вимеле́ха слоугꙋ своего въ хра́мѣ а҆гри́ппы* ("Abimelech Thy servant in the estate of Agrippa"). This refers to the narrative in the pseudepigraphical *Paralipomena of Jeremiah* (4 Baruch 5:1–34), wherein Ebed-melech (Abimelech) the Ethiopian (cf. Jeremiah 38:7–13) is sent by Jeremiah to gather figs from the estate of Agrippa, falls asleep under a tree, and sleeps undisturbed for 66 years through the Babylonian destruction and exile of Jerusalem, waking with the figs still fresh and dripping milk. In Eastern Christian Euchologia, this miraculous slumber is invoked as the archetype of peaceful, restorative, and providential sleep.
[^3001]: A reference to the Seven Sleepers of Ephesus (Maximian, Malchus, Martinian, Dionysius, John, Serapion, and Constantine), who were sealed in a cave on Mount Celion/Pion under Emperor Decius (c. 250 AD) and awoke during the reign of Emperor Theodosius II. The duration of 372 years (*три́ста и седмьдесѧ́тъ и двѣ лѣ́тѣ*) reflects the standard chronological figure preserved across Byzantine and Slavonic Euchologia and synaxaria, presented as an empirical pledge of the General Resurrection.
[^3002]: An explicit quotation of James 1:17: *всѧ́ко даа́нїе бл҃го, и въсѧ́къ да́ръ съвръше́нъ* ("Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights"), which also serves as the foundation of the Prayer behind the Ambo in the Divine Liturgy.
[^3003]: Church Slavonic heading: *Въ ст҃ꙋю пѧтидесѧ́тницꙋ мл҃тва надъ коливомъ* ("On Holy Pentecost, Prayer over the Kolyvo"). In the Ruthenian typikon tradition witness of the Striatyn Euchologion, this unique blessing is formulated specifically for Pentecost, directly invoking the historical descent of the Holy Spirit in fiery tongues (*ѡ҆гньными ꙗзы́кы сꙋще́ственнѣ посла́въ всест҃ыи дх҃ъ твой*) upon the Apostles, while sanctifying boiled wheat (*kolyvo*) offered in thanksgiving and in memory of all sanctified souls.
[^3004]: Daniel 1:11–16: Daniel and the three holy youths (Ananias, Azarias, and Misael) requested pulse (seeds/vegetables) and water rather than defiling themselves with the meat and wine from the table of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon; after ten days, their countenances appeared fairer and fatter in flesh than all the youths who ate the portion of the king's delicacies.
[^3005]: Church Slavonic heading: *Мл҃тва на гдⷭ҇кыѧ пра́здники надъ коливомъ* ("Prayer on Feasts of the Lord, over the Kolyvo"). In traditional Byzantine and Ruthenian practice, *kolyvo* (boiled wheat decorated with honey, nuts, and raisins) is offered not only at memorials for the departed, but also on major Feasts of the Lord (*Dominical / Despotic Feasts*) and patronal celebrations, symbolizing the seed that falls into the earth and bears much fruit (John 12:24) in the resurrection.
[^3006]: Church Slavonic heading: *Мл҃тва глаго́лемаѧ надъ коутї́ю на па́мѧти ст҃ыхъ* ("Prayer Said over the Kutia on the Memory of the Saints"). Note the appearance of the East Slavic/Ruthenian term *kutiya* (*коутї́ѧ*) alongside *kolyvo* (*ко́ливо*), demonstrating recensional vernacular realia in the 1606 Striatyn print.
[^3007]: The prayer explicitly links the commemoration of the saint of the day (*ст҃го и́мѧ реⷦ҇, є҆го́же и҆ па́мѧть съвръша́емъ*) with prayer for the departed faithful (*и҆ въ па́мѧть и҆же въ бл҃гоче́стїи, и҆ вѣ́рѣ сконча́вшихсѧ*), continuing across the foliation boundary onto leaf p1041.
