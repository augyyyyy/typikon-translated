[^3182]: Title rubric: *Prayer at the Construction of a Ship, or a Boat* (Слав. *Мл҃тва · на съѻ҆рꙋже́нїе кораблѧ̀, и҆ли ло́дьи*; Greek *Εὐχὴ ἐπὶ κατασκευῇ πλοίου*). This Euchological blessing consecrated newly constructed maritime vessels—both seagoing ships (*кора́бль*, Greek *πλοῖον*) and river craft or boats (*ло́дьꙗ*, Greek *λέμβος* / *σκάφη*)—before launching, placing the vessel under divine and angelic guardianship.
[^3183]: Allusion to Noah building the Ark (Genesis 6:14-22). The printer supplies an explicit marginal gloss in the right margin: *кїѡ́тъ* (from Greek *κιβωτός*, "ark/chest"), explaining the Slavonic word *ковче́гъ* (*съѻ҆рꙋжи́ти ковче́гъ на сп҃сенїе ми́ра*). The theology of the prayer contrasts the multiplicity of wooden planks joined into a single unified hull (*ѿ мнѡ́гихъ дре́въ... є҆ди́нѣмъ дре́вомъ бы́ти, и҆ съчета́тисѧ*) with God's sovereign governance over inanimate creation, typologically prefiguring the Church as the Ark of salvation navigating the turbulent waters of the world.
[^3184]: Title rubric: *Prayer over Salt* (Слав. *Мл҃тва, надъ со́лью*; Greek *Εὐχὴ ἐπὶ ἅλατος*). In Byzantine and Ruthenian domestic liturgy, salt was formally blessed as a staple of sustenance, a preservative against decay, and an agent of purification, recalling Christ's word: "Ye are the salt of the earth" (Matthew 5:13) and Saint Paul's exhortation: "Let your speech be always with grace, seasoned with salt" (Colossians 4:6).
[^3185]: Scriptural typology: The healing of the poisonous and barren spring at Jericho by the Prophet Elisha through casting salt into a new cruse (2 Kings 2:19-22 / LXX 4 Kingdoms 2:19-22: "Thus saith the Lord: I have healed these waters; there shall not be from thence any more death or barren land").
[^3186]: Slav. *преложѝ ю҆ въ жръ́твоу радова́нїа*—Greek *ποίησον αὐτὸ θυσίαν ἀγαλλιάσεως* ("make it a sacrifice of exultation/joy"), echoing Psalm 26:6 (LXX 26:6: "And I offered in His tabernacle a sacrifice of joy").
[^3187]: Title rubric: *Prayer over a Woman when She Has Miscarried* (Слав. *Мл҃, на[д] жено́ю є҆гда̀ и҆звръ́жетъ*; Greek *Εὐχὴ ἐπὶ γυναικὶ ὅταν ἐκτρώσῃ* or *Εὐχὴ ἐπὶ γυναικὶ ἀποβαλούσῃ*). In Eastern Christian canon law and pastoral theology, miscarriage or stillbirth—whether caused by maternal trauma, physical infirmity, involuntary accident, or intentional intervention—was treated as a profound bereavement and spiritual crisis requiring formal prayers of physical healing, consolation, purification, and reconciliation.
[^3188]: Introductory liturgical rubric: The priest begins with the customary Trisagion prayers (*Тр҃тое* = Trisagion, *пр҃таа тр҃це* = All-Holy Trinity, *Ѿч҃е на́* = Our Father, *Ꙗ҆́ко твоѐ є҆́стъ цр҃тво* = priestly doxological exclamation "For Thine is the kingdom"), followed by the Troparion of the current day (*Тро · дню*), and the deacon's/priest's invitation to prayer: "Let us pray to the Lord" (*Гд҃ꙋ помоли́мсѧ*).
[^3189]: Incarnational invocation: The opening address of the prayer commemorates Christ's Nativity and His reclining as a newborn babe in the manger at Bethlehem (Luke 2:7, 12, 16), juxtaposing Christ's holy infancy with the lost life of the miscarried child.
[^3190]: Canonical and pastoral terminology: Slav. *въпа́дшоую грѣхмѝ въ оу҆бїи́ство во́лное* (Greek *περιπεσοῦσαν ἁμαρτίαις εἰς φόνον ἑκούσιον ἢ ἀκούσιον*). Ancient canonical tradition (e.g. Saint Basil the Great, Canons 2 and 8; Ancyra, Canon 21; Trullo, Canon 91) categorizes the destruction of a fetus—whether procured through abortifacients (*во́лное* / voluntary) or resulting from bodily trauma, distress, or involuntary mishap (*нево́лное* / involuntary)—under the broader juridical heading of homicide (*φόνος* / *оу҆бїи́ство*), requiring canonical absolution, physical healing, and spiritual restoration. On leaf p1115, the text explicitly adds the balanced phrase: *во́лею, и҆ли нево́лею зача́тое въ не́й и҆звръ́гшей* ("whether by will or against her will having cast forth that which was conceived within her").
[^3191]: Rubrical and pastoral prayer for physical recovery: Slav. *възста́ви ю҆ ѿ ло́жа нане́мже ѡ҆бложе́на є҆́стъ*—Greek *ἀνάστησον αὐτὴν ἀπὸ τῆς κλίνης ἐν ᾗ περίκειται*. The woman experiencing a miscarriage was typically bedridden from hemorrhage, shock, and weakness; the prayer asks Christ, the Physician of souls and bodies, to raise her up as He did the mother-in-law of Saint Peter (Matthew 8:14-15) and the paralytic (Mark 2:11).

[^3193]: Intercession for the attendants and midwives: Slav. *и҆ всѣ́хъ ѡ҆брѣта́ющих́сѧ, и҆ прикосноу́вшихсѧ є҆́й*—Greek *καὶ πάντων τῶν εὑρεθέντων καὶ ἁψαμένων αὐτῆς*. Under ancient ritual purity canons derived from Levitical law (Leviticus 15:19-30), touching a person during active postpartum flux or after contact with stillbirth was considered ritual defilement requiring priestly blessing and prayer before approaching the Holy Mysteries.
