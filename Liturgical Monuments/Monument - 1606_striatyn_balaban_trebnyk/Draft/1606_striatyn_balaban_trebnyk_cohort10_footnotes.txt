[^139]: Fol. 37r. Conclusion of the Baptismal Dismissal (*ѿпоу́стъ*). The text completes the liturgical formula begun on Fol. 36v: *ꙗ҆́ко бл҃гъ и҆ чл҃колꙕ́бецъ* ("...for He is good and the Lover of mankind"). In early modern Ruthenian euchologia, the dismissal seals the triple mystery of initiation (Baptism, Chrismation, and the subsequent Communion), acknowledging Christ's condescension to be baptized in the Jordan and invoking the intercession of the Theotokos, the precious Cross, and all the saints.

[^140]: Fol. 37r. The Great Transfer / Entrance (*вели́кїй перено́съ* / Greek: μεγάλη εἴσοδος) and Post-Baptismal Communion. The 1606 Striatyn Euchologion provides crucial historical evidence for the liturgical integration of Baptism within the Divine Liturgy. If the Liturgy is celebrated, the newly baptized infant is solemnly carried with tapers at the Great Transfer (*на вели́комъ перено́сѣ*), whereas an adult candidate walks in personal procession immediately preceding the Holy Gifts, holding a lighted taper in both hands (*дръжа̀ по свѣщѝ въ ѻ҆бо́ю рꙋкоу*). Both infant and adult immediately partake of the Holy Mysteries of Christ, preserving the undivided primitive order of Christian initiation. Furthermore, upon dismissal, the neophyte is escorted in joyful triumph back to his home bearing burning tapers.

[^141]: Fol. 37v. The Baptismal Octave of Illumination (*да́же до ѿсма́го днѐ*). In strict accordance with ancient Byzantine and Jerusalem practice (witnessed in the mystagogical catecheses of St. Cyril of Jerusalem and St. John Chrysostom), the newly illumined Christian wore the white baptismal robe (*chiton* / *kryzhmo*) and carried the lighted baptismal candle to church daily for seven consecutive days, attending Vespers, Matins, and the Divine Liturgy. Only on the eighth day (*ѿсмы́й дн҃ь*) was this octave brought to a liturgical close by the solemn ritual of Ablution and Hair-Tonsure.

[^142]: Fol. 38r. Part V: The Order for the Ablution of the Baptized on the Eighth Day (*ПОСЛѢДОВÁНЇЄ ВЪ Є҆́ЖЕ ѼМЫ́ТИ КР҃ТИ́ВШАГО СѦ ВЪ Ѽ́СМЫЙ ДН҃Ь* / Greek: Εὐχαὶ τῆς Ἀπολούσεως μεθ' ἡμέρας ὀγδόην). Folio 38r opens with a monumental full-width woodcut headpiece characteristic of the Striatyn printing house (depicting two classical floral urns flanked by rolling acanthus scrolls), introducing the distinct liturgical office for releasing the candidate from the baptismal garments and washing away the physical chrism so as to guard the holy mystery from profane contamination.

[^143]: Fol. 38r. The Loosing of the Baptismal Garments and the Ruthenian Term *па́пишъ* (*раздрѣша́етъ ꙗ҆́же па́пишъ ѿ кр҃ще́нїа ѻ҆дѣва́ла , и҆ по́ѧсъ*). The rubric specifies that the priest unlooses the belt and the baptismal garments designated as *па́пишъ* (repeated on Fol. 40r: *ѻ҆дѣва́ло и҆́же па́пишъ*). In western Ukrainian and Galician-Ruthenian historical dialect, *папуш* / *папиш* designates a swaddled bundle, wrap, or linen infant binder (cognate with Romanian *păpușă* / Latin *pupa*, and related to Galician *папуша* for a wrapped sheaf or swaddling bundle). Here it serves as a vernacular technical term for the white swaddling cloth or baptismal wrapper (*savanon* / *kryzhmo*) wrapped securely around the infant over the white tunic.

[^144]: Fol. 38v. The First Euchological Prayer of Ablution (*И҆́же и҆збавле́нїе съгрѣше́нїемъ ра́ди ст҃го кр҃ще́нїа...* / Greek: Ὁ τὴν ἄφεσιν τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν διὰ τοῦ ἁγίου βαπτίσματος...). The prayer addresses God as the Author of spiritual regeneration, petitioning that the divine illumination of His countenance may shine perpetually in the candidate's heart, that the "shield of faith" may be guarded from demonic ambush, and that the "garment of incorruptibility" (*нетлѣ́нїе ѻ҆дѣѧ́нїа*) and "spiritual seal" (*дх҃овнаѧ печа́ть*) received in Chrismation may remain spotless, inviolate, and unassailable.

[^145]: Fol. 39r. The Second Prayer of Ablution: Heavenly Illumination and the Unstolen Pledge (*Влⷭ҇ко Гн҃и Бг҃е на́шь , и҆́же ра́ди коу́пели нб҃наго ѻ҆сїѧ́нїа...* / Greek: Δέσποτα Κύριε ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν, ὁ διὰ τῆς κολυμβήθρας...). The euchology commemorates the font as the conduit of heavenly illumination (*нб҃ное ѻ҆сїѧ́нїе* / οὐράνιος ἔλλαμψις) and invokes the divine hand of protection over the child to keep the "pledge of the Spirit" (*ѻ҆броуче́нїе дх҃а*, Greek: ἀρραβὼν τοῦ Πνεύματος; cf. 2 Corinthians 1:22, 5:5; Ephesians 1:14) "unstolen" (*некра́домо* / ἄσυλον) by adversary powers.

[^146]: Fol. 39v. The Prayer of Bowing of Heads: The Neophyte as an Unconquered Champion (*Ѻ҆дѣ́выисѧ въ тѧ̀ Хрⷭ҇та̀ и҆ Бг҃а на́шего...* / Greek: Ὁ ἐνδυσάμενος σὲ τὸν Χριστὸν...). Reflecting the Pauline imagery of Galatians 3:27 ("as many of you as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ"), the prayer portrays the baptized person as an athlete or champion (*подви́жникъ* / Greek: ἀθλητής) entering the spiritual arena against invisible foes, beseeching the Lord to grant him final victory and the crown of incorruption.

[^147]: Fol. 40r. The Ritual Ablution and Sacramental Formula (*Кр҃ти́лсѧ є҆сѝ . просвѣти́лсѧ є҆си . мѵ́ромъ пома́залсѧ є҆сѝ...*). The rubric directs the priest to unbind the belt and the baptismal swaddling cloth (*па́пишъ*), dip their extremities into the water, sprinkle the child with the declaration "Thou hast been justified, thou hast been enlightened" (1 Corinthians 6:11), and then wipe the anointed sensory centers (head, face, breast) with a fresh sponge saturated with water. The formula explicitly enumerates the stages of initiation: baptism (*кр҃ти́лсѧ є҆си*), illumination (*просвѣти́лсѧ є҆си*), chrismation (*мѵ́ромъ пома́залсѧ є҆си*), sanctification (*ѡ҆ст҃и́лсѧ є҆си*), and washing (*ѡ҆мы́лсѧ є҆си*).

[^148]: Fol. 40v. The Mystery of the Tonsure of Hair: The Theological Anthropology of the Craftsman (*МЛ҃ТВА на постриже́нїе вла́сѡвъ* / Greek: Εὐχὴ ἐπὶ κουρᾷ τριχῶν). This profound euchological prayer develops a classical Patristic theology of the human organism (drawing upon Gregory of Nyssa and Nemesius of Emesa): God is hailed as the "excellent Craftsman" (*до́брый хꙋдо́жникъ* / ἄριστος τεχνίτης) Who endowed man with a rational soul (*дꙋша́ слове́снаѧ*) and an harmonious physical body, setting the head as a citadel upon the heights, adorning it with unconfused sensory organs, and covering it with hair as a shield against meteorological tempests.

[^149]: Fol. 41r. The Apostolic Mandate and the Dedication of the First-Fruits (*сътвори́ти нача́токъ стри́щи вла́сы*). The prayer cites the apostolic rule of St. Paul—the "chosen vessel" (Acts 9:15)—to do all things unto the glory of God (1 Corinthians 10:31). The clipping of the hair is offered not as an aesthetic trimming, but as a sacrificial first-fruits (*aparchē* / *нача́токъ*) of the candidate's body offered unto Christ in thanksgiving for his illumination.

[^150]: Fol. 41v. The Royal Anointing of David and the Blessing of Old Age (Psalm 127:5 LXX). The concluding prayer of tonsure typologically links the blessing of the candidate's head with the biblical anointing of King David by the Prophet Samuel at Bethlehem (1 Samuel / 1 Kings 16:13). The priest prays that the newly enlightened Christian may advance in age from infancy unto venerable grey hairs (*сѣди́ны ста́рости* / πολιὰ γήρως), beholding the prosperity of the mystical Jerusalem all the days of his life (Psalm 127:5 LXX; Masoretic Psalm 128:5).
