[^182]: Fol. 52r. The Communion of the Reconciled Latin Penitent and Concluding Rites of Part VII (*и҆ причаща́етъ є҆го̀ ст҃ы́хъ та́инъ...*). The reception of Roman Catholics culminates in their immediate admission to the Holy Mysteries (*прича́стїе ст҃ы́хъ та́инъ*), following the ancient canonical principle articulated by Canon 95 of the Council in Trullo (692) and confirmed by the Council of Constantinople (1484). Having abjured Latin liturgical divergence, confessed the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed without the *Filioque*, and received the unction of holy chrism (*myron*), the penitent is admitted to full Eucharistic fellowship, followed by the concluding litany and dismissal (*ѿпоу́стъ*).

[^183]: Fol. 53r. Part VIII: The Office of Betrothal, the Royal Doors, and the Gold and Silver Rings on the Altar (*ПОСЛѢ́ДОВА́НЇЕ БЫВА́ЄМОЕ Ѡ҆ БРꙊЧЕ́НЇЮ*). In the 1606 Striatyn Trebnyk, Betrothal (*Обруче́нїе* / ἀρραβών) is preserved as a distinct liturgical office conducted immediately following the Divine Liturgy (*По Бж҃тве́нои литꙋргі́и*). The bridal pair stands outside the iconostasis before the Royal Doors (*предъ ст҃ы́ми двьрьмѝ*), the bridegroom on the right and the bride on the left. The two rings—one gold and one silver—are placed upon the right side of the holy altar table (*ст҃а́ѧ трапе́ꙁа*). The Ruthenian text specifies: *зла́тыи перше́ный, сре́брный ѹ҆кланѧ́ѧсѧ къ де́снымъ, зла́тыи же къ шꙋ́и̑мъ* ("the golden one first, [and the] silver [one]; the silver leaning toward the right, and the golden toward the left, near one another"). In Byzantine and Ruthenian mystagogy (commented upon by St. Symeon of Thessalonica, *De Sacramentis* 273), the gold ring signifies the solar brilliance of Christ and the authority of the husband, while the silver ring signifies the lunar radiance of the Church and the spouse; their proximity on the altar sanctifies their impending exchange and marital bond.

[^184]: Fol. 53v. The Signing of the Bridal Pair, Lighted Tapers, Entrance, and Initial Blessing (*зна́менаетъ гла́вы новобра́чныхъ г҃. и҆ да́стъ и҆̀мъ свѣ́ща въжже́ны...*). The priest blesses the bridal pair by making the sign of the cross thrice over their heads and gives them lighted tapers, symbolizing the lamps of the wise virgins awaiting the Heavenly Bridegroom (Matthew 25:1–13) and the spiritual joy and purity of Christian union. The rubric directs him to lead them within the nave of the church (*вну́тръ въ цр҃ковь*) and cense cruciformly. The deacon petitions: "Bless, Master" (*Бл҃ви влⷣко*), and the priest initiates the rite with the euchological doxology: "Blessed is our God always, now and ever, and unto ages of ages" (*Бл҃ве́нъ Бг҃ъ на́шъ...*), followed immediately by the Great Litany (*Ми́ромъ Гдⷭ҇у помоли́мсѧ*).

[^185]: Fol. 54r. Special Petitions of the Betrothal Litany (*Ѡ҆ рабѣ́хъ Бж҃їихъ, и҆́мѧ ре́къ, и҆́же нн҃ѣ ѡ҆брꙋча́ющихсѧ дроу́гъ дроу́гꙋ...*). The Great Litany is enriched with specific euchological petitions for the betrothed couple, faithfully translating the Byzantine Greek Euchologion (*ὑπὲρ τῶν δούλων τοῦ Θεοῦ τῶν νῦν ἀρραβωνιζομένων ἀλλήλοις*): praying for their mutual salvation; for the granting of children for the succession of the human race (*ча́да къ воспрїѧ́тїе ро́да* / Greek: εἰς διαδοχὴν γένους); for the sending down upon them of perfect and peaceful love and divine assistance (*любвѝ съвръше́ннѣй ми́рнѣй, и҆ по́мощи*); and for their blessing in conjugal unity.

[^186]: Fol. 54v. The Petition for an Honorable Marriage and Undefiled Bed (*Ꙗ҆́ко да̀ Гдⷭ҇ь Бг҃ъ на́шъ да́рꙋетъ и҆̀мъ бра́къ че́стенъ, и҆ ло́же несквръ́но...*). The litany weaves the apostolic injunction of Hebrews 13:4 ("Marriage is honorable in all, and the bed undefiled") directly into the liturgical petition, praying that the Lord God grant them an honorable marriage and an undefiled bed, and that they may be preserved in blameless conduct (*непоро́чное пребыва́нїе*) and unwavering faith (*твръ́даѧ вѣ́ра*).

[^187]: Fol. 55r. The First Euchological Prayer of Betrothal: Isaac and Rebecca (*Бж҃е вѣ́чный, и҆́же раздѣле́ннаѧ събра́въ въ є҆ди́нство...*). This ancient euchological prayer (Greek: Ὁ Θεὸς ὁ αἰώνιος, ὁ τὰ διῃρημένα συναγαγὼν εἰς ἑνότητα) invokes the biblical archetype of Isaac and Rebecca (Genesis 24) as the historical pattern of divinely guided marital union and inheritance of the covenant promise (*наслѣ́дники тѣ́хъ свое́му ѡ҆бѣтова́нїю*). God is addressed as the One Who establishes an indissoluble bond of love (*сою́ꙁъ любвѐ положи́въ нераздрꙋши́мъ*), gathering together disparate persons into ontological conjugal unity.

[^188]: Fol. 55v. The Bridal Ecclesiology of the Church and the Bestowal of the Rings (*и҆́же ѿ ꙗ҆ꙁы́къ пре́ѡ҆броучи́въ цр҃ковь дв҃оу чи́стоую...*). The second prayer roots Christian betrothal in Pauline bridal ecclesiology (2 Corinthians 11:2: "For I have espoused you to one husband, that I may present you as a chaste virgin to Christ"), confessing Christ as having pre-espoused the Church from among the Gentiles. The priest takes the rings from the altar table and bestows the gold ring first upon the bridegroom and then the silver ring upon the bride, pronouncing the sacramental declaration: *Ѡ҆броуча́етъ ра́бъ Бж҃їи...* ("The servant of God is betrothed unto the handmaid of God...").

[^189]: Fol. 56r. The Threefold Cross with the Rings, the Sponsor (*Kum*), and the Friend of the Bridegroom (*по́семъ и҆ꙁмѣнѧ́етъ пе́рстени новобра́чныхъ, кꙋ́мъ, и҆лѝ дрꙋ́гъ женихо́въ...*). The rubric specifies that the priest makes the sign of the cross thrice with the ring over the head of each candidate before placing it upon their right hands (*на де́сныхъ и҆́хъ пе́рстѣхъ*). The subsequent threefold exchange of rings is performed not by the priest, but by the sponsor (*коу́мъ* / *kum*) or the best man / friend of the bridegroom (*дрꙋ́гъ женихо́въ*, rendering the Greek παράνυμφος), reflecting the ancient customary participation of the wedding sponsor in sealing the covenant.

[^190]: Fol. 56v. Eliezer's Mission in Mesopotamia and the Water Pitcher Sign (*ходата́йствомъ водоно́са ѡ҆броучи́ти реве́коу ѿкры́въ...*). The lengthy final prayer of betrothal (Greek: Κύριε ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν, ὁ τῷ παιδὶ τοῦ πατριάρχου Ἀβραὰμ συμπορευθεὶς ἐν τῇ Μεσοποταμίᾳ) recalls the mission of Abraham's servant Eliezer to Haran in Mesopotamia (Genesis 24). Eliezer prayed for a divine sign at the well of water: that the maiden who offered to draw water from her pitcher (*водоно́съ* / ὑδρία) for him and his camels would be the bride appointed by God for Isaac. The prayer proclaims God as the Creator of male and female (Genesis 1:27, Matthew 19:4), establishing woman as a helper unto man (Genesis 2:18) and for the continuation of mankind (*прїѧ́тїе ро́да чл҃ча* / διαδοχὴν τοῦ γένους τῶν ἀνθρώπων).
