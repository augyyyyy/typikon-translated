[^191]: Fol. 57r. Biblical Typology of the Ring: Joseph, Daniel, Tamar, and the Prodigal Son (*пе́рстенѧ ра́ди да́стьсѧ вла́сть і҆ѡ́сифоу въ є҆гѵ́птѣ...*). This euchological prayer marshals a sequence of Old and New Testament precedents illustrating the signet ring (*пе́рстень* / δακτύλιος) as the divine token of authority, vindication, covenant fidelity, and paternal adoption: Pharaoh investing Joseph with his signet ring as viceroy of Egypt (Genesis 41:42); the sealing of the lions' den and Daniel's deliverance in Babylon (Daniel 6:17, Bel and the Dragon 14:11–14); Tamar's vindication before Judah by means of his signet ring, cord, and staff (Genesis 38:18, 25); and the father bestowing the signet ring upon the repentant prodigal son as the restoration of sonship (Luke 15:22). The prayer links these historical tokens to the cosmic covenant established by the right hand of God, which divided the Red Sea under Moses (Exodus 15:6) and founded the heavens by the Word of Truth (Psalm 32:6 LXX [MT 33:6]).

[^192]: Fol. 57v. The Cosmic Foundation and the Angelic Guide of the Espoused (*землѧ̀ ѡ҆сно́вана... и҆ а҆́гг҃лъ Гдⷭ҇нь да пре́йдетъ пре́д ними...*). Continuing the betrothal prayer (Greek: Ὁ Θεὸς ὁ αἰώνιος...), the euchology extends God's cosmic ordering—the establishment of the heavens and foundation of the earth (Psalm 103:5 LXX [MT 104:5])—unto the micro-cosmos of the Christian home. The priest petitions for the angel of the Lord to precede the couple as their guardian and companion throughout all the days of their earthly life, evoking the angel who guided Eliezer (Genesis 24:7) and Raphael who accompanied Tobias (Tobit 5:16–22).

[^193]: Fol. 57v. The Betrothal Exclamation and Insistent Litany (*Та́же є҆кте́нїа: Поми́лꙋй на́съ Бж҃е...*). Following the euchological doxology ("For Thou art He Who blesses and sanctifies all things... unto ages of ages"), the deacon intones the Insistent Litany (*Сугубая ектения* / ἐκτενὴς ἱκεσία). In the 1606 Striatyn imprint, the litany contains specific petitions for the ruling bishop and for the newly betrothed couple (*ѡ҆ рабѣ́хъ Бж҃їихъ... ѡ҆броуча́ющихсѧ дроу́гъ дроу́гоу*), culminating in the communal petition *реце́мъ всѝ* ("let us all say").

[^194]: Fol. 58r. Autonomous Betrothal Dismissal and Structural Independence of the Rite (*Та́же ѿпоу́стъ*). In early Byzantine and 16th–17th century Ruthenian liturgical practice, Betrothal (*Обруче́нїе*) frequently remained an autonomous liturgical office celebrated weeks or months prior to the solemn Crowning (*Вѣнча́нїе*), rather than immediately conflated with it as in standard modern practice. The rubric *Та́же ѿпоу́стъ* ("Then the dismissal") followed by an ornamental tailpiece confirms the completion of Betrothal as a self-contained liturgical unit.

[^195]: Fol. 58v. Part IX: The Order of Crowning, the Entrance into the Nave, and Psalm 127 (*ПѠСЛѢДОВА́НЇЕ ВѢНЧА́НЇЮ* / *А҆́щели же въсхо́щꙋтъ а҆́бїе вѣнча́тисѧ...*). Part IX opens with an elaborate Renaissance woodcut headpiece depicting the Mystery of Crowning celebrated under an architectural baldachin before the tetrapod. The transitional rubric specifies: *А҆́щели же въсхо́щꙋтъ а҆́бїе вѣнча́тисѧ...* ("And if they wish to be crowned immediately, they enter into the church with lighted tapers..."). The celebrant precedes them into the nave with the censer, chanting Psalm 127 LXX [MT 128], the ancient bridal processional psalm (*Блаже́ни всѝ боѧ́щїисѧ Гдⷭ҇а...* / "Blessed are all they that fear the Lord").

[^196]: Fol. 59r. The Concluding Verses of Psalm 127 and the Hierarchical Petition (*Ѡ҆ а҆рхїепⷭ҇пѣ наше[мъ]...*). Psalm 127 concludes with the eschatological and familial blessing of Zion: children as olive shoots, seeing children's children, and peace upon Israel (Psalm 127:3–6 LXX). Immediately upon the psalm's completion, the deacon initiates the Great Litany of Crowning (*Ми́ромъ Гдⷭ҇у помоли́мсѧ*). The petition for the hierarchy uses the title *Ѡ҆ а҆рхїепⷭ҇пѣ наше[мъ]* ("For our archbishop"), reflecting the Ruthenian provincial terminology for the Metropolitan of Kyiv and Galicia or the diocesan eparch.

[^197]: Fol. 59v. Special Euchological Petitions of the Crowning Litany (*Ѡ҆ рабѣ́хъ Бж҃їихъ... и҆́же нн҃ѣ съчетава́ющихсѧ дроу́гъ дроу́гоу къ бра́ка приѡбще́нїе...*). The Crowning litany incorporates distinctive euchological petitions: praying that the marriage be blessed after the model of Cana of Galilee (*ꙗ҆́коже въ ка́нѣ Галиле́йстѣ*); for chastity (*цѣломꙋ́дрїе* / σωφροσύνη); for the fruit of the womb (*пло́дъ чре́вный*); for joy in the sight of sons and daughters; and for the granting of fair offspring (*бл҃гоча́дїе* / εὐτεκνία) and blameless conduct of life (*незазо́рное пребыва́нїе*).

[^198]: Fol. 60r. The First Euchological Prayer of Crowning: Creation of Eve and the Primordial Blessing (*Бг҃е пречи́стыи, и҆ вьсѧ́коѧ тва́ри създа́телю...*). The solemn prayer of Crowning (Greek: Ὁ Θεὸς ὁ ἄχранτος, καὶ πάσης κτίσεως δημιουργός...) roots the sacrament in the primordial creation narrative of Genesis 1–2: God fashioning Eve from Adam's rib out of His love for mankind, imparting the primordial mandate to increase, multiply, and have dominion over the earth (Genesis 1:28), and establishing husband and wife as one flesh (*є҆ди́нъ оу҆́дъ* / ἓν μέλος).

[^199]: Fol. 60v. The Old and New Testament Patriarchal Chain of Marriage (*и҆ и҆́хже Бг҃ъ съпрѧжѐ чл҃къ да не раꙁлоуча́етъ...*). The prayer recites the foundational covenantal marriages of salvation history: Adam and Eve (Genesis 2:24, Matthew 19:6); Abraham and Sarah's miraculous conception in old age (Genesis 17:4–5, 18:10–14); Isaac and Rebecca (Genesis 24); Jacob and Rachel with the twelve patriarchs (Genesis 29–35); Joseph and Aseneth with their sons Ephraim and Manasseh (Genesis 41:45–52); Zechariah and Elizabeth giving birth to St. John the Baptist (Luke 1); culminating in the rod of Jesse blossoming into the Theotokos (Isaiah 11:1).

[^200]: Fol. 61r. The Christological Transfiguration of Marriage at Cana (*прише́дъ въ ка́ноу Галиле́йскоую, и҆ сꙋ́щїи та́мо бра́къ бл҃вивъ...*). Christian matrimony finds its supreme sanction and sanctification in Christ's presence and first miracle at the wedding of Cana of Galilee (John 2:1–11). The prayer confesses that by His presence Christ revealed lawful wedlock (*зако́нное сꙋпрꙋ́жество*) and childbearing (*чадотворе́нїе*) to be His holy will, petitioning for His invisible episcopal oversight (*неви́димымъ твои́мъ прѣдстоѧ́тельствомъ*) to bless the bridal pair.

[^201]: Fol. 61v. Patriarchal Blessings of Dew, Fatness, and Generous Almsgiving (*ѿ росы̀ нб҃ныѧ свы́ше, и҆ ѿ тꙋ́ка земна́го...*). The conclusion of the first Crowning prayer adopts the patriarchal blessing of Isaac upon Jacob: "God give thee of the dew of heaven, and the fatness of the earth, and plenty of corn and wine" (Genesis 27:28). This abundance of wheat, wine, and oil (*пшени́ца, вина̀ и҆ є҆ле́а*) is sought not for self-indulgence, but so the newlyweds may exercise generous Christian charity and almsgiving (*да преподаю́тъ и҆ тре́боующимъ*), securing both earthly longevity and an unfading crown of celestial glory.
