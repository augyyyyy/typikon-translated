[^124]: Fol. 32r. Psalm 31:1–6a LXX (*Блаже́ни и҆́мже ѿпоусти́шасѧ безако́нїа...* / *Beati quorum remissae sunt iniquitates*). In early Byzantine and Ruthenian euchologia, Psalm 31 is the joyful post-baptismal thanksgiving psalm chanted immediately following the candidate's emergence from the font. It proclaims the core mystery of baptismal regeneration: the absolute remission and covering of sins (Romans 4:7–8), the lifting of guilt, and deliverance from ungodliness. Note the archaic Ruthenian graphic variants in Fol. 32r: *възврати́хсѧ на стра́сть, є҆гда̀ оу҆нзѐ ми те́рнъ* (rendering LXX ἐν τῷ ἐνпаγῆναί μοι ἄκανθαν) and the abbreviation *позна̑* for *позна́хъ*.

[^125]: Fol. 32v. Psalm 31:6b–11 LXX and the threefold repetition (*Сї́й же ѱло́мъ гл҃етсѧ г҃.*). The euchology contrasts the spiritual understanding granted to the newly illuminated with the uncomprehending brute nature of the horse and mule (v. 9), whose jaws must be held with bit and bridle. The rubric requiring the psalm to be sung thrice reflects the ancient baptismal station before the chrismation and vesting, mirroring the Trinitarian nature of the mystery.

[^126]: Fol. 33r. The Ruthenian Pastoral Rubric on Infant Baptism vs. Candidates of Understanding (*смы́сленъ*). In this celebrated historical rubric, the 1606 Striatyn Euchologion records the early modern Ruthenian pastoral practice: if the candidate is an infant, the priest seats him in the font with the water reaching unto his neck, holding him securely with the left hand, and pours warm water over his head with the right hand (*водѣ̀ соу́щи по́шїю, придръжа̀ лѣ́вою роуко́ю. де́сноюже прїе́мъ те́плою во́доу възлива́етъ на главоу̀ є҆го̀*), explicitly citing pastoral prudence and safety lest the infant drown (*зане́же блюстѝ зали́тисѧ младе́нца*). If, however, the candidate has reached understanding (*а́ще ли є҆́стъ смы́сленъ кр҃ща́емый*), the priest immerses him completely in three immersions (*погружа́етъ є҆го̀ въ трѝ погроуже́нїа*).

[^127]: Fol. 33r. The Vesting of the Newly Baptized in the Robe of Righteousness (*ѡ҆де́жда пра́вды*, Greek: χιτὼν δικαιοσύνης). The priest clothes the newly illuminated in a clean white tunic or garment, pronouncing the Trinitarian vesting formula. The white garment represents the recovery of the robe of light and glory originally given to Adam in Paradise before the Fall, now restored through baptismal incorruption, prefiguring the eschatological wedding garment (Matthew 22:11–12; Galatians 3:27; Revelation 7:9).

[^128]: Fol. 33v. The Baptismal Troparion in Tone 8: *Ри́зоу ми пода́ждь свѣ́тлꙋ, ѡ҆дѣѧ́йсѧ свѣ́томъ ꙗ҆́ко ри́зою...* ("Grant unto me a robe of light, O Thou that deckest Thyself with light as with a garment..."). Based upon Psalm 103:2 LXX, this troparion is sung while the sponsor and priest arrange the white baptismal robe upon the child. It expresses the mystical illumination (*photismos*) of the soul through Christ, Who wraps Himself in uncreated light.

[^129]: Fol. 33v–34r. The Pre-Chrismation Euchological Prayer (*Блгⷭ҇ве́нъ є҆си Гдⷭ҇и Бг҃е въседръжи́телю...* / Greek: Εὐχὴ τοῦ ἁγίου Μύρου). Addressed to God the Father as the "Sun of Righteousness" (Malachi 4:2), this prayer explicitly articulates the indivisible organic unity of the three traditional mysteries of Christian initiation: cleansing in holy water (Baptism), the seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit through the life-creating anointing (Chrismation), and the immediate worthy reception of the Body and Blood of Christ (Eucharist: *и причаще́нїе ст҃а́го тѣ́ла, и҆ чт҃ныѧ кро́ве Хрⷭ҇та твое́го*).

[^130]: Fol. 34v. The Priestly Ekphonesis of Chrismation (*Ꙗ҆́ко ты̀ є҆си Бг҃ъ на́шъ Бг҃ъ ми́ловати и҆ сп҃сати насъ...*). The doxology seals the invocation of the Holy Trinity with the Father Paul standard ("now and ever, and unto ages of ages"), concluding the solemn prayer of consecration before the sacramental unction.

[^131]: Fol. 34v. The Holy Mystery of Chrismation (*Myropomazanie* / Greek: Χρῖσμα). The priest takes the consecrated Holy Chrism (*ст҃ымъ мѵ́ромъ*) and anoints the five bodily sensory centers in the form of the Cross: forehead (*чело̀*), eyes (*ѡ҆чеса̀*), breast (*пръ́си*), hands (*роу́цѣ*), and feet (*но́зѣ*), sealing each with the statutory biblical formula: "The seal of the gift of the Holy Spirit. Amen" (*Печа́ть да́ра ст҃го дх҃а, а҆ми́нь*; cf. 2 Corinthians 1:21–22; Ephesians 1:13; 4:30; Council of Laodicea, Canon 48).

[^132]: Fol. 34v. The Baptismal Circular Procession (*ѡ҆бхо́дѧтъ ѡ҆́крꙋгъ а҆нало́ѧ*). The priest, sponsor (*въспрїе́мникъ* / *кумъ*), and newly illuminated process three times around the analogion (or font) while chanting the ancient baptismal entrance hymn: *Є҆ли́ци въ Хрⷭ҇та̀ крⷭ҇ти́стесѧ, въ Хрⷭ҇та̀ ѡ҆блеко́стесѧ, а҆ллилоу́їа* (Galatians 3:27, sung thrice). The circular motion symbolizes the eternal, unending nature of the new covenant with Christ and participation in the joy of the heavenly choir.

[^133]: Fol. 34v. The Baptismal Prokeimenon in Tone 3 from Psalm 26:1 LXX (*Гдⷭ҇ь просвѣще́нїе моє̀ и҆ сп҃си́тель мо́й, кого́ сѧ оу҆бою̀...* / "The Lord is my illumination and my Savior; whom shall I fear?"). Illumination (*просвѣще́нїе*, Greek: φωτισμός) serves as the primary patristic term for Holy Baptism (cf. Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 61; Gregory of Nazianzus, *Oration 40*).

[^134]: Fol. 35r–35v. The Baptismal Epistle: Romans 6:3–11 (Pericope 91). The locus classicus of Pauline baptismal theology. St. Paul teaches that baptism is not a mere symbolic washing, but a mystical co-burial (*съпогребо́хомсѧ*) into the death of Christ and a resurrection unto newness of life, crucifying the "old man" and destroying the "body of sin" so that the believer is liberated from servitude to sin.

[^135]: Fol. 35v. Conclusion of the Epistle and the Liturgical Gospel Dialogue. The Pauline pericope culminates in the exhortation to reckon oneself dead unto sin, but alive unto God in Christ Jesus (Romans 6:11). The deacon then commands liturgical vigilance: *Премоу́дрость прѡ́сти оу҆слы́шимъ ст҃го є҆ѵⷢгліа* (Greek: Σοφία. Ὀρθοί. Ἀκούσωмен τοῦ ἁγίου εὐαγγελίου), answered by the announcement of the Holy Gospel according to Matthew.

[^136]: Fol. 35v–36r. The Great Commission Gospel: Matthew 28:16–20 (Pericope 116). The final verses of the Gospel of Matthew, proclaimed at every Byzantine baptism. The resurrected Lord, having received all power in heaven and earth, commissions the eleven apostles to go forth, make disciples of all nations, baptize them into the threefold Name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and keep His commandments, concluding with the perpetual promise: "And lo, I am with you always, even unto the end of the age."

[^137]: Fol. 36r. The Baptismal Augmented Litany (*є҆ктенїа̀*). In addition to the civil petitions for the sovereign and the hierarchy (the local bishop), the euchology introduces a unique personal petition for the sponsor/godparent (*ра́боу Бж҃їю [и҆́мѧ ре́къ въспрїе́мникоу]*), praying for mercy, life, peace, health, and remission of sins in recognition of the sponsor's grave spiritual sponsorship and covenantal pledge made at the font.

[^138]: Fol. 36v. The Petition for the Newly Illumined and the Baptismal Dismissal (*ѿпоу́стъ*). The litany concludes with a fervent supplication that the newly baptized child may be preserved in the "faith of a pure confession" (*въ вѣ́рѣ чи́стаго и҆сповѣда́нїа*) all the days of his life. The euchology then prescribes the proper baptismal dismissal formula commemorating Christ's baptism in the Jordan by John (*И҆́же въ І҆ѿрда́ни ѿ І҆ѡа́нна на́шего ра́ди сп҃се́нїа кр҃ти́тисѧ прїе́мый...*), which concludes on the succeeding folio (Fol. 37r / p91).
