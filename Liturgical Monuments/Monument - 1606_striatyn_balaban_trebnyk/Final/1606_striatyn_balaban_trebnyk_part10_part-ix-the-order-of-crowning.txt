### Notes

[^195]: Fol. 58v. Part IX: The Order of Crowning, the Entrance into the Nave, and Psalm 127 (*ПѠСЛѢДОВА́НЇЕ ВѢНЧА́НЇЮ* / *А҆́щели же въсхо́щꙋтъ а҆́бїе вѣнча́тисѧ...*). Part IX opens with an elaborate Renaissance woodcut headpiece depicting the Mystery of Crowning celebrated under an architectural baldachin before the tetrapod. The transitional rubric specifies: *А҆́щели же въсхо́щꙋтъ а҆́бїе вѣнча́тисѧ...* ("And if they wish to be crowned immediately, they enter into the church with lighted tapers..."). The celebrant precedes them into the nave with the censer, chanting Psalm 127 LXX [MT 128], the ancient bridal processional psalm (*Блаже́ни всѝ боѧ́щїисѧ Гдⷭ҇а...* / "Blessed are all they that fear the Lord").
[^196]: Fol. 59r. The Concluding Verses of Psalm 127 and the Hierarchical Petition (*Ѡ҆ а҆рхїепⷭ҇пѣ наше[мъ]...*). Psalm 127 concludes with the eschatological and familial blessing of Zion: children as olive shoots, seeing children's children, and peace upon Israel (Psalm 127:3–6 LXX). Immediately upon the psalm's completion, the deacon initiates the Great Litany of Crowning (*Ми́ромъ Гдⷭ҇у помоли́мсѧ*). The petition for the hierarchy uses the title *Ѡ҆ а҆рхїепⷭ҇пѣ наше[мъ]* ("For our archbishop"), reflecting the Ruthenian provincial terminology for the Metropolitan of Kyiv and Galicia or the diocesan eparch.
[^197]: Fol. 59v. Special Euchological Petitions of the Crowning Litany (*Ѡ҆ рабѣ́хъ Бж҃їихъ... и҆́же нн҃ѣ съчетава́ющихсѧ дроу́гъ дроу́гоу къ бра́ка приѡбще́нїе...*). The Crowning litany incorporates distinctive euchological petitions: praying that the marriage be blessed after the model of Cana of Galilee (*ꙗ҆́коже въ ка́нѣ Галиле́йстѣ*); for chastity (*цѣломꙋ́дрїе* / σωφροσύνη); for the fruit of the womb (*пло́дъ чре́вный*); for joy in the sight of sons and daughters; and for the granting of fair offspring (*бл҃гоча́дїе* / εὐτεκνία) and blameless conduct of life (*незазо́рное пребыва́нїе*).
[^198]: Fol. 60r. The First Euchological Prayer of Crowning: Creation of Eve and the Primordial Blessing (*Бг҃е пречи́стыи, и҆ вьсѧ́коѧ тва́ри създа́телю...*). The solemn prayer of Crowning (Greek: Ὁ Θεὸς ὁ ἄχранτος, καὶ πάσης κτίσεως δημιουργός...) roots the sacrament in the primordial creation narrative of Genesis 1–2: God fashioning Eve from Adam's rib out of His love for mankind, imparting the primordial mandate to increase, multiply, and have dominion over the earth (Genesis 1:28), and establishing husband and wife as one flesh (*є҆ди́нъ оу҆́дъ* / ἓν μέλος).
[^199]: Fol. 60v. The Old and New Testament Patriarchal Chain of Marriage (*и҆ и҆́хже Бг҃ъ съпрѧжѐ чл҃къ да не раꙁлоуча́етъ...*). The prayer recites the foundational covenantal marriages of salvation history: Adam and Eve (Genesis 2:24, Matthew 19:6); Abraham and Sarah's miraculous conception in old age (Genesis 17:4–5, 18:10–14); Isaac and Rebecca (Genesis 24); Jacob and Rachel with the twelve patriarchs (Genesis 29–35); Joseph and Aseneth with their sons Ephraim and Manasseh (Genesis 41:45–52); Zechariah and Elizabeth giving birth to St. John the Baptist (Luke 1); culminating in the rod of Jesse blossoming into the Theotokos (Isaiah 11:1).
[^200]: Fol. 61r. The Christological Transfiguration of Marriage at Cana (*прише́дъ въ ка́ноу Галиле́йскоую, и҆ сꙋ́щїи та́мо бра́къ бл҃вивъ...*). Christian matrimony finds its supreme sanction and sanctification in Christ's presence and first miracle at the wedding of Cana of Galilee (John 2:1–11). The prayer confesses that by His presence Christ revealed lawful wedlock (*зако́нное сꙋпрꙋ́жество*) and childbearing (*чадотворе́нїе*) to be His holy will, petitioning for His invisible episcopal oversight (*неви́димымъ твои́мъ прѣдстоѧ́тельствомъ*) to bless the bridal pair.
[^201]: Fol. 61v. Patriarchal Blessings of Dew, Fatness, and Generous Almsgiving (*ѿ росы̀ нб҃ныѧ свы́ше, и҆ ѿ тꙋ́ка земна́го...*). The conclusion of the first Crowning prayer adopts the patriarchal blessing of Isaac upon Jacob: "God give thee of the dew of heaven, and the fatness of the earth, and plenty of corn and wine" (Genesis 27:28). This abundance of wheat, wine, and oil (*пшени́ца, вина̀ и҆ є҆ле́а*) is sought not for self-indulgence, but so the newlyweds may exercise generous Christian charity and almsgiving (*да преподаю́тъ и҆ тре́боующимъ*), securing both earthly longevity and an unfading crown of celestial glory.
[^202]: Fol. 62r. Christ as the Priest and Lawgiver of Mystical Marriage (*та́йнаго и҆ прⷭ҇чтаго бра́ка сщ҃еннодѣ́йственникъ...*). Explaining the euchological title `ἱερουργός` / `сщ҃еннодѣ́йственникъ` applied to God/Christ in this second prayer of Crowning (Greek: Εὐλογητὸς εἶ, Κύριε ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν, ὁ τοῦ μυστικοῦ καὶ ἀχράντου γάμου ἱερουργὸς...), which defines God not merely as the creator of physical humanity, but as the active liturgical celebrant and consecrator of holy matrimony, establishing its sacramental character and guarding its incorruptibility against worldly decay.
[^203]: Fol. 62r. Primordial Creation and the Institution of Marriage (Genesis 2:18, 23). The euchology quotes the divine counsel prior to the creation of Eve: "It is not good for man to be alone; let us make him a helper fit for him" (Genesis 2:18), followed by Adam's ecstatic prophetic recognition of woman as bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh upon awakening from his deep sleep (Genesis 2:23).
[^204]: Fol. 62v. Scriptural Genesis and Synoptic Conflation (*сего̀ ра́ди ѡ҆ста́витъ чл҃къ ѿца̀ своего̀...*). The prayer combines the primordial covenantal formula of Genesis 2:24 ("Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife, and they shall be one flesh") with Christ's solemn dominical ratifying gloss in Matthew 19:6 and Mark 10:9: "What therefore God hath joined together, let not man put asunder."
[^205]: Fol. 63r. The Typology of Divine Preservation (*съхранѝ и҆̀хъ... ꙗ҆́коже съхрани́лъ є҆сѝ...*). A liturgical litany of Old Testament salvific deliverances petitioned on behalf of the spouses: Noah preserved from the universal cataclysm within the ark (Genesis 7–8); Jonah preserved alive within the belly of the sea-monster (Jonah 2); and the Three Holy Youths (Hananiah, Azariah, and Mishael) preserved unburnt in the fiery furnace of Babylon by the angel who made the midst of the furnace as it were a moist whistling wind (Daniel 3:49–50 LXX).
[^206]: Fol. 63v. The Joy of St. Helen and the Crowning of the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste (*бл҃же́наѧ є҆ле́на... ст҃ы́ѧ ти четыридесѧ́тъ мч҃никы...*). The euchology links matrimonial joy to the exalted spiritual joy of St. Helen upon discovering the True and Life-Giving Cross in Jerusalem (c. 326 AD), while the petition remembering the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste (d. 320 AD)—who received forty celestial crowns descending from heaven as they froze in the icy lake—explicitly establishes the bridal crowns as crowns of martyrdom, bearing witness to selfless ascetic sacrifice and victory over worldly passions.
[^207]: Fol. 63v. The Parental Blessing Establishing the Home (*занѐ мл҃твы роди́телей оу҆твръжа́ютъ ѡ҆снова́нїа дѡмо́въ...*). Quoting the canonical wisdom of Sirach (Ecclesiasticus) 3:9: "The blessing of the father establishes the houses of children; but the curse of the mother roots out foundations." The prayer explicitly extends the circle of euchological remembrance to the parents and sponsors assembled for the wedding feast.
[^208]: Fol. 64r. Vegetative Imagery of Blessing and Procreation (*ке́дры лива́нскыѧ, ꙗ҆́коже ло́зоу бл҃говѣ́твеноу...*). Integrating Old Testament poetic imagery of vital flourishing from Psalm 91:13 LXX [MT 92:12] ("the righteous shall flourish like a palm tree; he shall grow like a cedar of Lebanon") and Psalm 127:3 LXX [MT 128:3] ("thy wife shall be as a fruitful vine... thy children like olive plants round about thy table"), coupled with the apostolic benediction of 2 Corinthians 9:8 and the celestial luminescence of Philippians 2:15 ("among whom ye shine as lights in the world").
[^209]: Fol. 64v. The Third Crowning Prayer: Divine Imposition and Climax of Matrimonial Consecration (*Сщ҃е́нникъ па́ки мл҃твꙋ сїю̀ гл҃етъ велегла́сно: Бж҃е ст҃ы́и...*). This third euchological prayer (Greek: Ὁ Θεὸς ὁ ἅγιος, ὁ πλάσας ἐκ τοῦ χοὸς τὸν ἄνθρωπον...) constitutes the direct consecratory climax of the Crowning office. The rubric prescribes that the celebrant recite it *велегла́сно* ("with a loud voice" / ἐκφώνως), entreating God to reach down His invisible right hand from His holy habitation to yoke the bridegroom and bride together in mutual chastity and love prior to the imposition of crowns.
[^210]: Fol. 65r. The Rite of Imposition of Crowns (*прїе́мъ сщ҃е́нникъ вѣнца̀, вѣнча́етъ ѡ҆жени́вшагосѧ...*). The ritual imposition of wreaths or metallic crowns upon the heads of the couple constitutes the central sacramental act of Byzantine matrimony, signifying royal dignity as heads of a domestic church, mutual dominion over passions, and the crown of martyrdom.
[^211]: Fol. 65r. The Striatyn Crowning Formula: Recensional Witness (*Вѣнча́етсѧ ра́бъ Бж҃їи [и҆́мѧ], въ и҆́мѧ ѿц҃а...*). The 1606 Striatyn imprint preserves the concise archaic formula *Вѣнча́етсѧ ра́бъ Бж҃їи [и҆́мѧ], въ и҆́мѧ ѿц҃а и҆ сн҃а и҆ ст҃го дх҃а* without the explicit dative complement *рабѣ̀ Бж҃їей [и҆́мѧ]* found in standard later editions, reflecting an archaic early Byzantine euchological witness documented in Sinait. Gr. 959 and early South Slavic and Ruthenian imprints.
[^212]: Fol. 65r. The Triple Nuptial Blessing: Inked Recensional Witness (*Гдⷭ҇и Бж҃е на́шъ сла́вою и҆ чⷭ҇тїю вѣнча́лъ є҆сѝ и҆́хъ*). The 1606 text physically prints the past/perfect indicative form: *вѣнча́лъ є҆сѝ и҆́хъ* ("hast Thou crowned them"), directly citing the indicative wording of Psalm 8:6 LXX [MT 8:5] (*δόξῃ καὶ τιμῇ ἐστεφάνωσας αὐτόν* / *сла́вою и҆ че́стїю вѣнча́лъ є҆сѝ є҆го̀*), in contrast to the later harmonized precative imperative *вѣнча́й ѧ҆̀* ("crown them").
[^213]: Fol. 65r. The Nuptial Prokeimenon (Psalm 20:4 LXX [MT 21:3]). Tone 8: "Thou hast set upon their heads crowns of precious stones."
[^214]: Fol. 65v. The Prokeimenon Half-Verse (Psalm 20:5 LXX [MT 21:4]). "They asked life of Thee, and Thou gavest it them: length of days unto ages of ages." Note the grammatical dual verbal form in Church Slavonic: *проси́ста* ("they two asked"), precisely denoting the bridal pair.
[^215]: Fol. 65v. The Prokeimenon Verse (Psalm 20:2 LXX [MT 21:1]). "O Lord, in Thy strength the king shall be glad, and in Thy salvation how greatly shall he rejoice."
[^216]: Fol. 65v. The Nuptial Epistle Lection (Ephesians 5:20–33). Pericope 230: The foundational Pauline text setting forth the sacramental mystery of Christian marriage as the earthly mirror and icon of Christ's sacrificial love for His Bride, the Church.
[^217]: Fol. 66r. The Washing of Water by the Word (Ephesians 5:26). Alluding to baptismal rebirth (*τῷ λουτρῷ τοῦ ὕδατος ἐν ῥήмаτι* / *ба́нею водно́ю гл҃омъ*), through which the Church is purified and sanctified to become the unblemished Bride of Christ.
[^218]: Fol. 66v. The Conjugal Epilogue (Ephesians 5:33). Concluding the apostolic instruction with the call to mutual sacrificial love and reverence (*да бои́тсѧ* / *ἵνα φοβῆται*), understood not as servile terror, but as holy and affectionate awe within the hierarchy of love.
[^219]: Fol. 66v. The Nuptial Alleluia Tone 5. In Ruthenian chant tradition, the nuptial Alleluia is sung in Tone 5 with verses selected from Psalm 11 LXX.
[^220]: Fol. 66v. Nuptial Alleluia Verse (Psalm 11:8 LXX [MT 12:7]). "Thou, O Lord, shalt keep us, and shalt preserve us, from this generation, and forever."
[^221]: Fol. 66v. The Nuptial Gospel Lection (John 2:1–11). Pericope 6: The account of the Marriage at Cana of Galilee, where Jesus performed His first miracle at the request of His mother, changing water into wine and manifesting His glory.
[^222]: Fol. 67r. The Six Stone Waterpots and Nuptial Transformation (John 2:6–10). Explaining the euchological and typological significance of the six stone waterpots (*водоно́съ ка́менныхъ ше́сть*) in the Johannine wedding pericope (John 2:1–11, Pericope 6). In patristic and liturgical theology (e.g., St. John Chrysostom, *Homily 21 on John*, and St. Cyril of Alexandria), the waterpots of Jewish purification signify the old covenant of the Law and the Prophets, which Christ elevates and transforms by the New Covenant of grace into the rich nuptial wine of the Kingdom, prefiguring the Eucharistic communion of the Church and the spiritual sanctification of matrimonial union.
[^223]: Fol. 67v. The Johannine Conclusion and Manifestation of Glory (John 2:11). The conclusion of the nuptial Gospel pericope, noting the manifestation of Christ's divine glory (*и҆ ꙗ҆вѝ сла́воу свою̀*) and the awakening of apostolic faith at the wedding feast of Cana, which Byzantine euchologies identify as the sanctifying archetype for all Christian marriages.
[^224]: Fol. 67v. The Nuptial Augmented Litany (*Є҆ктенїа̀ согꙋ́баѧ*). The 1606 Striatyn text prints the petitions of the Augmented Litany in compressed epigraphic form, supplying the incipits of the opening petitions (*Ѿ всеѧ̀ дꙋша̀...*, *Гдⷭ҇и вседръжи́телю...*, *Поми́лоуй на́съ Бж҃е...*) followed by the proper nuptial petition remembering the newly-crowned spouses by name. In accordance with Rule 16, these incipits are preserved as printed without silent unwritten expansion.
[^225]: Fol. 67v. Sacerdotal Memento of the Spouses (*Е҆ще мо́лимсѧ ѡ҆ рабѣ́хъ Бж҃їй [и҆мк]...*). The rubric specifies that the deacon or priest explicitly commemorates the bridegroom and bride by name (*ре́къ: И҆ помина́етъ и҆́хже хо́щетъ*), praying for their health, preservation, and salvation in their newly consecrated estate.
[^226]: Fol. 68r. Sacerdotal Prayer After the Nuptial Gospel (*Гдⷭ҇и Бж҃е на́шъ, и҆́же сп҃си́телнымъ свои́мъ смотре́нїемъ...*). Greek: *Κύριε ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν, ὁ ἐν τῇ σωτηριώδει σου οἰκονομίᾳ καταξιώσας ἐν Κανᾷ τῆς Γαλιλαίας τίμιον ἀναδεῖξαι τὸν γάμον...* This classic post-Gospel prayer of the Byzantine Crowning rite beseeches Christ to manifest His presence once again as at Cana, preserving the marital bed undefiled (*небла́знено и҆́хъ ло́же съблюдѝ*), granting mutual concordance (*въ ми́рѣ и҆ съединенїи*), and bringing the couple unto ripe old age (*къ ста́рости масти́тѣ сконча́тисѧ*) in fulfillment of the divine commandments.
[^227]: Fol. 68v. The Litany of Supplication (*Проси́тельнаѧ є҆ктенїа̀* / *Aitēseis*). Printed in the Striatyn Trebnyk with incipits for each petition (*Застоупѝ сп҃сѝ...*, *Днѐ въсего̀ съвръше́нна...*, *А҆́гг҃ла ми́рна...*, *Млⷭ҇ть и҆ ѡ҆ставле́нїе...*, *Дѡ́бры и҆ поле́зны...*, *Про́чее лѣ́тъ...*, *Хрⷭ҇тїа́нскыѧ кончи́ны...*, *Съєдине́нїе вѣ́ры...*). In historical liturgical practice, this litany precedes the Lord's Prayer, mirroring the structure of the Divine Liturgy and highlighting matrimony as a sanctified ecclesial entrance into divine communion.
[^228]: Fol. 68v. The Introductory Embolism to the Lord's Prayer (*И҆ сподо́би ны, влⷣко, съ дръзнове́нїемъ...*). Sacerdotal exclamation introducing the recitation of the Dominical Prayer (Greek: *Καὶ καταξίωσον ἡμᾶς, Δέσποτα, μετὰ παῤῥησίας, ἀκατακρίτως τολμᾶν ἐπικαλεῖσθαι σὲ τὸν ἐπουράνιον Θεὸν Πατέρα, καὶ λέγειν*), emphasizing the filial boldness (*παῤῥησία* / *дръзнове́нїе*) granted to believers through adoption in Christ.
[^229]: Fol. 68v. The Dominical Prayer (*Ѿче на́шъ*). In the ancient Byzantine nuptial ordo, the chanting of the Lord's Prayer by the assembled congregation originally preceded the reception of Holy Communion by the newly married couple from the Pre-Sanctified Gifts, a rite later transformed into the drinking of the Common Cup.
[^230]: Fol. 69r. The Rite of the Common Cup (*Ѻ҆́бщаѧ ча́ша* / *Κοινὸν ποτήριον*). The presentation and blessing of the common cup of wine constitutes a surviving historical vestige and surrogate of the ancient reception of Holy Communion at the nuptial Divine Liturgy. In late Byzantine and Ruthenian practice, unconsecrated wine blessed with this sacerdotal prayer is shared mutually by the bridegroom and bride as a symbol of shared life, common sorrow, and unified joy.
[^231]: Fol. 69r. Prayer of Blessing Over the Common Cup (*Бж҃е, и҆́же всѧ̀ сътвори́выи крѣ́постїю свое́ю...*). Greek: *Ὁ Θεός, ὁ πάντα ποιήσας τῇ ἰσχύϊ σου, καὶ στερεώσας τὴν οἰκουμένην, καὶ κοσμήσας τὸν στέφανον πάντων τῶν ὑπὸ σοῦ γεноμένων...* The euchological text recalls God's creative cosmic fashioning and petitions a spiritual blessing (*бл҃гослове́нїемъ дх҃овнымъ*) upon the cup of wine presented to the spouses.
[^232]: Fol. 69v. The Nuptial Dance of Isaiah (*Хѡжде́нїе ѡ҆́кръстъ а҆нало́ѧ*). The circular procession around the analogion or tetrapod three times represents eternity, unbroken marital fidelity, and liturgical rejoicing. The rubric specifies that the best man or sponsor (*дроу́гоу женихо́воу*) holds the crowns over the heads of the bridegroom and bride as they are led by the celebrant.
[^233]: Fol. 69v. The First Nuptial Troparion: "O Isaiah, Dance" (*І҆са́їа ликоу́и* / *Ἡσαΐα χόρευε*). Tone 5: Citing the messianic prophecy of Isaiah 7:14 and Zechariah 6:12 (*въсто́къ и҆́мѧ є҆моу́* / *Ἀνατολὴ ὄνομα αὐτῷ*), this hymn is chanted identically at holy ordinations, monastic tonsures, and weddings, linking marital union to the incarnation of Christ and celebrating the virgin birth as the fountain of all Christian sanctity.
[^234]: Fol. 69v. The Second Nuptial Troparion: "O Holy Martyrs" (*Ст҃їи мч҃нци* / *Ἅγιοι μάρτυρες*). Tone 7: Commemorating the martyrs who received heavenly crowns (*и҆ вѣнча́вшеисѧ*), this hymn directly interprets the wedding crowns as emblems of martyric self-denial and patient suffering in the domestic church.
[^235]: Fol. 70r. The Third Nuptial Troparion: "Glory to Thee, O Christ God" (*Сла́ва тебѣ̀ Хрⷭ҇ѐ Бж҃е* / *Δόξα σοι, Χριστὲ ὁ Θεός*). Concluding the analogion procession with apostolic praise and confession of the Holy Trinity, identical to the dismissal troparion at priestly ordination.
[^236]: Fol. 70r. The Rite of Deposition of Crowns (*С̾ѧ́тїе вѣнце́въ*). The ceremonial removal of the crowns from the bridegroom and bride, accompanied by personalized patriarchal blessings evoking the righteous couples of the Old Covenant.
[^237]: Fol. 70r. Benediction Over the Bridegroom (*Възвели́ченъ бꙋ́ди, женишѐ, ꙗ҆́ко же А҆враа́мъ...*). Invoking the three great Patriarchs of Israel: Abraham, known for magnified faith and hospitality (Genesis 12:2, 17:5); Isaac, known for divine blessing and peaceful submission (Genesis 26:12); and Jacob, known for abundant fruitfulness and endurance (Genesis 28:14, 30:43).
[^238]: Fol. 70r. Benediction Over the Bride (*И҆ ты̀, невѣ́сто, възвели́чена бꙋ́ди, ꙗ҆́ко же Са́рра...*). Invoking the righteous Matriarchs: Sarah, magnified as mother of nations (Genesis 17:15–16; 1 Peter 3:6); Rebecca, renowned for bridal joy and beauty (Genesis 24); and Rachel, celebrated for abundant motherhood and love (Genesis 29–30; Jeremiah 31:15; Ruth 4:11). The Slavonic witness prints *Ра́хи[ль]* abbreviated with titlo as *Рах̑и,*.
[^239]: Fol. 70v. Prayer at the Deposition of Crowns (*Гдⷭ҇и Бж҃е на́шъ, прише́дыи въ Ка́ноу Галиле́йскоую...*). Greek: *Κύριε ὁ Θεὸς ἡμῶν, ὁ παραγενόμενος ἐν Κανᾷ τῆς Γαλιλαίας, καὶ τὸν ἐκεῖσε γάμον εὐλογήσας...* Beseeching God to receive the bridal crowns into His celestial kingdom (*въспрїимѝ вѣнца̀ и҆́хъ въ црⷭ҇твїе твоѐ*), signifying that the earthly dignity and ascetic struggle of Christian marriage find their ultimate fulfillment in the everlasting Kingdom of God. The blessing of "comings-in and goings-out" (*въхѡ́ды и҆ и҆схѡ́ды*) cites Psalm 120:8 LXX [MT 121:8].
[^240]: Fol. 71r. Sacerdotal Prayer of Head-Bowing and Solemn Trinitarian Blessing (*Ѿц҃ъ и҆ сн҃ъ и҆ дх҃ъ ст҃ыи...*). Greek: *Ὁ Πατὴρ, καὶ ὁ Υἱὸς, καὶ τὸ Ἅγιον Пνεῦμα, ἡ παναγία καὶ ὁμοούσιος καὶ ζωаρχικὴ Тριάς...* This exalted prayer of benediction invokes the Holy Trinity to bestow upon the spouses longevity (*длъгожи́тїе*), fair children (*бл҃гоча́дъство* / *εὐτεκνία*), spiritual advancement (*преспѣва́нїе животоу̀ и҆ вѣ́рѣ*), abundance of earthly goods, and eternal salvation.
[^241]: Fol. 71r. Concluding Congratulatory Rubric and Complete Dismissal (*Съвръше́нный ѿпоу́стъ*). The rubric prescribes mutual fraternal congratulations (*здравствоу́ютъ и҆́хъ*) and the ritual bridal kiss (*цѣлоу́етъ дроу́гъ дроу́га*) prior to the solemn dismissal (*ἀπόлυσις*) pronounced by the celebrant.
[^242]: Fol. 71v. Nuptial Dismissal Patrons (*Ст҃ы́хъ Бг҃овѣнча́нныхъ цр҃еи... ст҃го великомч҃ника Проко́пїа...*). The proper nuptial dismissal commemorates: (1) St. Constantine and St. Helen, the first Christian sovereigns crowned by God (*Θеоστεφεῖς* / *Бг҃овѣнча́ннїи*); and (2) the Great-Martyr Procopius (d. 303 AD), who according to ancient tradition encouraged twelve Christian women to endure martyrdom by comparing their suffering to glorious bridal adornment, establishing him as a patron of Christian marriage.
