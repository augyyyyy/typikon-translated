[^1064]: Sessional Hymn (Kathisma) by St. Joseph the Hymnographer (c. 816–886), Tone 8, chanted after Ode 3. The hymn addresses the Twelve Apostles as radiant luminaries guiding believers through the nocturnal darkness of passions toward the joy of Christ's Holy Passion.
[^1065]: Second Sessional Hymn to the Holy Apostles, Tone 8, Podoben: "That which was commanded mystically" (Повелѣ́нное та́инственно / Τὸ προσταχθὲν μυστικῶς), imploring the apostolic college to intercede for the faithful during the spiritual course of the Fast.
[^1066]: Theotokion concluding the sessional hymns after Ode 3, invoking the intercession of the Mother of God together with the Holy Apostles for peace throughout the world and repentance before the end.
[^1067]: Rubric directing that the Triodion (the three-ode canon to the Apostles by St. Joseph the Hymnographer) is chanted without prostrations (metanias), distinguishing it from the Great Canon where three metanias accompany each troparion.
[^1068]: Ode 4 Heirmos of the Triodion to the Apostles, Tone 8: "I have heard, O Lord, the mystery of Thy dispensation" (Услышахъ Господи смотренія твоего таинство / Εἰσακήκοα Κύριε, τὴν ἀκοὴν τῆς σῆς οἰκονομίας), drawing upon the Canticle of Habakkuk (Habakkuk 3:2).
[^1069]: The Twelve Apostles are likened to a mystical twelve-stringed musical instrument (двоенадесятострунный органъ / δωδεκάχорδον ὄργανον), playing the saving melody of the Gospel and confounding evil deceit.
[^1070]: Second canon of the Triodion to the Apostles by St. Theodore the Studite (759–826), Tone 8, sharing the same heirmos.
[^1071]: Ode 4 Heirmos of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "The prophet heard of Thy coming, O Lord" (Услыша пророкъ пришествіе твое Господи / Ἀκήκοεν ὁ προφήτης, τὴν ἔλευσίν σου Κύριε), drawing upon Habakkuk 3:2 and the mystery of the Incarnation from the Virgin.
[^1072]: St. Andrew allegorizes Jacob's ladder (Genesis 28:12) and his two wives (Genesis 29): Leah represents active ascetical practice (πρᾶξις), fruitful in good deeds, while Rachel represents contemplative knowledge and spiritual vision (θεωρία), attained through great labor. "A mind seeing God" invokes the traditional Patristic etymology of Israel (ὁ ὁρῶν Θεόν), leading to the unwaning divine darkness (γνόφος) of mystical theology.
[^1073]: The twelve sons of Jacob (Genesis 35:22–26) are depicted as mystical steps of active ascent, contrasted with Esau (Edom), who sold his birthright for the gratification of sensual desire (Genesis 25:29–34; Hebrews 12:16).
[^1074]: Job on the dungheap (Job 2), his sudden loss of family, wealth, and royal dignity, and his patient endurance; contrasted with the soul's impatience under affliction. Christ's crucifixion offers His Body, Blood, and Spirit for the renewal, cleansing, and restoration of humanity unto the Father.
[^1075]: The Blood and Water issuing from Christ's pierced side (John 19:34) typify the cleansing bath of baptism and the cup of the Eucharist, uniting the Old and New Covenants. St. Andrew weaves together the parables of the wedding garment and foolish virgins (Matthew 22:11–14; 25:1–13) with the Publican and the Pharisee (Luke 18:9–14).
[^1076]: Troparia to St. Mary of Egypt inserted into Ode 4 of the Great Canon, praising her angelic life in the desert after her repentance from the depths of sin.
[^1077]: Ode 5 Heirmos of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "Rising early from the night, O Lover of mankind" (От нощи утренююща, Человѣколюбче / Ἐκ νυκτὸς ὀρθρίζοντα, Φιλάνθρωπε), based upon the Canticle of Isaiah (Isaiah 26:9).
[^1078]: St. Andrew laments falling into the sins of Reuben, who defiled his father's bed (Genesis 35:22; 49:4), and of the brothers who sold Joseph into Egyptian slavery (Genesis 37:28), seeing Joseph as a type of Christ's suffering, burial, and resurrection. Moses placed in the ark of bulrushes by the Nile (Exodus 2:1–10) prefigures preservation from the demonic decree of Pharaoh.
[^1079]: Ode 5 continues the Old Testament typology of the Exodus: the Hebrew midwives Shiphrah and Puah who preserved the male children (Exodus 1:15–21); Moses striking the Egyptian and fleeing to Midian (Exodus 2:11–15); the burning bush on Sinai (Exodus 3:1–6); the rod of Moses parting the Red Sea (Exodus 14:16); Aaron's pure incense offering contrasted with the profane fire of Hophni and Phinehas (1 Kingdoms 2:12–17 [1 Samuel 2:12–17]); and the Egyptian magicians Jannes and Jambres who opposed Moses (Exodus 7:11; 2 Timothy 3:8).
[^1080]: New Testament healing miracles in Ode 5: Christ healing lepers and the paralytic; the woman with the issue of blood healed by touching the hem of His garment (Matthew 9:20–22); the woman bowed down for eighteen years straightened (Luke 13:10–17); and the Samaritan woman at Jacob's well (John 4:5–42).
[^1081]: The washing in the pool of Siloam (John 9:7) as a figure of illumination through tears; Ode 6 Heirmos of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "I cried with my whole heart unto the compassionate God" (Возопихъ всѣмъ сердцемъ моимъ / Ἐβόησα, ἐν ὅλῃ καρδίᾳ μου), based upon the Canticle of Jonah (Jonah 2:2); the judgment upon Dathan and Abiram who were swallowed up by the earth (Numbers 16); and Ephraim stubborn as a wild heifer (Hosea 4:16; Jeremiah 31:18).
[^1082]: Old Testament types in Ode 6: the hand of Moses rendered leprous and restored (Exodus 4:6–7); the Red Sea returning upon Pharaoh's chariots (Exodus 14:26–28); Israel murmuring against the manna and longing for the fleshpots of Egypt (Numbers 11:4–6); the rock struck by Moses pouring forth water (Exodus 17:6; Numbers 20:11; 1 Corinthians 10:4); and Joshua conquering Amalek and defeating the deception of the Gibeonites (Joshua 5–10).
[^1083]: New Testament allusions in Ode 6: the Apostle Peter sinking in the Sea of Galilee and saved by Christ's hand (Matthew 14:28–31); and the Parable of the Lost Coin (Luke 15:8–10), wherein the Word lights the lamp of St. John the Baptist and Forerunner to restore the divine image in humanity.
[^1084]: The solemn Kontakion of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "My soul, my soul, arise, why sleepest thou?" (Душе моя, душе моя, востани, что спиши; / Ψυχή μου ψυχή μου, ἀνάστα, τί καθεύδεις;), a masterpiece of Byzantine hymnography commonly attributed to St. Romanos the Melodist (c. 490–556) or St. Andrew of Crete, sung with its Ikos contemplating the devil wounded and defeated by the medicine-chest of Christ's Incarnation and Passion.
[^1085]: Opening of the prose Synaxarion of the Lenten Triodion appointed for Thursday of the Fifth Week of the Great Fast, commemorating the life, education, monastic tonsure in Jerusalem, and theological and hymnographic output of St. Andrew, Archbishop of Crete (Jerusalemite).
