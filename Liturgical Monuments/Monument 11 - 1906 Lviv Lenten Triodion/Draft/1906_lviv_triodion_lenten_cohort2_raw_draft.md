*On this day we celebrate the return of the prodigal son,*[^27] *which our divine fathers instituted second in the Triodion, for the following reason: Because there are certain men who, conscious in themselves of many terrible deeds, and having lived very prodigally from youth, spending their time in drunkenness and impurities, and having thus fallen into the depth of evils, fall into despair (which is the offspring of pride); and hence they are by no means willing to come unto the care of virtue, and proposing evil deeds, they fall headlong into yet worse evils. The holy Fathers, having a loving and fatherly disposition toward such men, and desiring to rescue them from despair, instituted this parable here after the first, uprooting the passion of despair from the very root and raising them up to the acquisition of virtue, showing forth the loving and all-good compassions of God unto those who have sinned greatly and lived prodigally; demonstrating from this parable of Christ that there is no sin that can overcome His love for mankind. For the two sons of the man (that is, of the God-Man, the Word) are the righteous and the sinners. The elder is he who abides continually in the commandments of God and in His goodness, never departing from Him. The younger is he who loved sin, and by shameful deeds of self-will broke away from fellowship under the yoke; and in him the love of God for mankind was revealed, though he had lived prodigally: for he did not preserve whole that which was according to the image, but followed the wicked demon, and was enslaved through pleasures to the will of the evil one, yet was unable to satisfy his desire; for sin is an insatiable thing, tempting by custom for the sake of temporary enjoyment. This the parable likens unto husks, which are the food of swine: for husks at first offer something sweet, but afterward become something harsh and like chaff, which is very much the nature of shame and sin. Scarcely therefore recovering himself, the prodigal, perishing as it were from hunger for virtue, comes to the Father, saying: Father, I have sinned against heaven and before Thee, and am no more worthy to be called Thy son. And He receives him repenting, not reproaching him, but spreading forth His arms and embracing him, showing forth His divine and fatherly tender compassions. And He gives him a robe, that is, Holy Baptism; and a ring and pledge, the grace of the All-Holy Spirit; and unto these also sandals, that his steps toward God may no more be wounded by serpents and scorpions, but that he may rather be able to crush their heads. Afterward, with exceeding great joy, the Father slays the fatted calf for his sake, His Only-begotten Son, and gives him to partake of His Flesh and Blood. And though the elder son marveled at His overflowing mercy, saying whatever he said, the Lover of mankind silences him also, entering in with quiet, meek, and affectionate words: "Thou art ever with Me," saying, "and it was fitting to rejoice and celebrate with the Father, for this my son was dead in sin before, and is alive again, having repented of the irrational things he did; and he was lost, having been far from Me by custom of pleasures, and is found by Me Who suffered through My tender compassion and called him with a merciful disposition." This parable may also be applied to the Jewish people and to us. For this reason therefore this parable was instituted here by the Holy Fathers: uprooting, as was said, despair and fear of repenting by good deeds, and instructing unto repentance and compunction those who have sinned like the prodigal; which is indeed the greatest weapon for driving away the arrows of the adversary, and a mighty help. Through Thine ineffable love for mankind, O Christ our God, have mercy on us. Amen.*

Thou Who of old didst fashion me from nothingness, and didst honor me with Thy divine image, but through transgression of the commandment didst return me again unto the earth from which I was taken: lead me back unto that which is according to Thy likeness, to be refashioned in the ancient beauty.

*Blessed art Thou, O Lord:*

Give rest, O God, unto Thy servants, and establish them in Paradise, where the choirs of the Saints, O Lord, and the righteous shine as luminaries; give rest unto Thy departed servants, overlooking all their transgressions.

*Glory:*

Devoutly let us hymn the threefold radiance of the one Godhead, crying aloud: Holy art Thou, O unoriginate Father, co-unoriginate Son, and divine Spirit; enlighten us who serve Thee in faith, and snatch us from the eternal fire.

*Now and ever:*

Rejoice, O pure one, who didst bear God in the flesh for the salvation of all, through whom the human race found salvation; through thee may we find Paradise, O pure, blessed Theotokos.

*Tone 8. Ode 1. Heirmos: "Let us send up a song, O people:"*

Let us all pray unto Christ, making memory today of the dead from the ages, that He may deliver from the eternal fire those who have fallen asleep in faith and in the hope of eternal life.

By the depth of Thy judgments, O Christ, all-wisely hast Thou foreordained the end of life of each man, its term and manner; therefore save at the judgment, O all-compassionate One, those whom the grave has covered in every land.

O Thou Who gavest the terms of our life, show forth as sons of the day that knows no evening, O Lord, those who have fallen asleep from the night of this life: orthodox priests, rulers, and all Thy people.

Those whom the water covered, and whom war mowed down, whom earthquake seized, and whom murderers slew, and whom fire consumed: among the faithful, O merciful One, assign them unto the portion of the righteous.

Overlooking all our bodily debts, O our Savior, at every age of all the human race, place uncondemned before Thy judgment seat those who give answer unto Thee, the Creator.

*Ode 4. Heirmos: "From the overshadowed mountain:"*

Fathers and forefathers, grandfathers and great-grandfathers, from the first even unto the last, who have died in righteousness and in the right faith: remember them all, O our Savior.

Those left behind in faith on mountains, on journeys, in desert places, monks and laymen, youths and elders: settle them with the Saints, O Christ.

Those who have departed unexpectedly from sorrow and joy, who through faith immediately changed this life, who suffered in prosperity or in adversity: give rest unto all, O our Savior.

Those whom the sword slew, and the horse dragged away, whom hail, snow, and torrential rain destroyed, whom a falling tile suffocated, or dust covered over: give rest unto them, O Christ our Savior.[^44]
