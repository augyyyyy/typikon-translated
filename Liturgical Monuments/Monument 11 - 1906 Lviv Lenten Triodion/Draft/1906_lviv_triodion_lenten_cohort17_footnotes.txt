[^370]: First Sunday of the Great Fast (Sunday of Orthodoxy) at Little Vespers conclusion: Physical leaf p161 (Printed fol. 157). The Aposticha stichera (Tone 2, Podoben: *O House of Ephratha* / *До́ме Еѵфра́ѳовъ*) celebrate the triumph of Orthodoxy over five major historical heresies and their champions explicitly named in the second troparion: Arius (Arianism, 1st Ecumenical Council), Macedonius I of Constantinople (Pneumatomachianism, 2nd Ecumenical Council), Peter the Fuller of Antioch (Monophysitism / Theopaschism), Severus of Antioch (Monophysitism), and Pyrrhus of Constantinople (Monothelitism, 6th Ecumenical Council). The third troparion commemorates Empress Theodora (c. 815–867 A.D.), who as regent for her infant son Michael III orchestrated the Council of Constantinople in 843 A.D., giving "the rod of strength" unto the Orthodox. The service concludes with the dismissal troparion *Пречи́стомꙋ твоемꙋ̀ ѻ҆́бразꙋ* ("Thy pure image do we venerate").
[^371]: Sunday of the Triumph of Orthodoxy at Great Vespers (*На вели́цѣй вече́рни*): Physical leaf p161 (Printed fol. 157). Display headpiece featuring an ornamental woodcut band. The rubrics appoint the customary kathisma from the Psalter (*Блаже́нъ мꙋ́жъ*), followed at "Lord, I have cried" by 10 stichera: 2 of the Resurrection and 3 Anatolian stichera from the Octoechos in the Tone of the week, and 4 prosomoia from the Triodion in Tone 6, to the melody *Having laid aside all earthly hope* (*Всѐ ѿло́жше* / *Ὅλην ἀποθέμενοι*), authored by St. Theophanes Graptos. The hymnody develops the Christological basis of iconography: the incomprehensible, immaterial Word shone forth before all ages from the Father, but was foretold by the Holy Prophets to become a visible child born of the Virgin, taking upon Himself all human properties, therefore depicting His likeness raises the believer unto divine love.
[^372]: Doxasticon at "Lord, I have cried" (*Сла́ва, гласъ в: Блгдть возсіѧ̀ и҆́стины...*): Physical leaf p162 (Printed fol. 158). This famous hymn synthesizes the dogmatic definition of the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787 A.D.), contrasting the Old Testament types and shadows of the Tabernacle with the reality of the Incarnate Word. The text explicitly asserts the theology of veneration: the bodily form of the Savior is "devoutly venerated, not deified" (*блгче́стнѡ покланя́емый, не бг҃отвори́мый* / προσκυνούμενος, οὐ θεοποιούμενος), refuting the iconoclast charge that icon veneration constitutes idolatry.
[^373]: Aposticha Doxasticon at Great Vespers (*Сла́ва, гласъ в: И҆з̾ нече́стіѧ ко блгче́стіе преше́дше...*): Physical leaf p162 (Printed fol. 158). The hymn enjoins the faithful to venerate holy images depicted upon walls, wooden panels, and sacred vessels, citing the classic patristic formula of St. Basil the Great from *De Spiritu Sancto* 18.45: "For the honor of the image passes unto the Prototype" (*че́сть бо ѻ҆́браза, ꙗ҆́коже глаго́летъ Васи́лій, на первоwбра́зное прехо́дитъ* / ἡ γὰρ τῆς εἰκόνος τιμὴ ἐπὶ τὸ πρωτότυπον διαβαίνει). The dismissal troparion appointed for the feast is *Пречи́стомꙋ ѻ҆́бразꙋ твоемꙋ̀ покланя́емся Блгíй* ("Thy pure image do we venerate, O Good One"), which also serves as the chief Festal Troparion at Matins.
[^374]: Matins Canon of the Holy Prophets and Holy Icons: Physical leaf p163 (Printed fol. 159). Authored by St. Theophanes Graptos ("the Branded", c. 778–845 A.D.), Metropolitan of Nicaea, who with his brother St. Theodore suffered imprisonment and facial branding under Emperor Theophilus. The canon bears the alphabetic/metrical acrostic: *Дне́сь блгче́стія прїисквѣ́тлаѧ прїи́де зарѧ̀* (in Greek: *Σήμερον εὐσεβίης θεοφεγγέος ἤλυθεν αἴγλη* — "Today the all-radiant dawn of piety has arrived"). The Katavasiai for the feast are taken from the Canon of the Cross (*Мо́рѧ чермнꙋ́ю пꙋчи́нꙋ*).
[^375]: Historical commemoration of the Imperial Restorers in Ode 1 and Ode 3: Physical leaf p163 (Printed fol. 159). St. Theophanes commemorates the coregency of the pious Empress Theodora and her young son Michael III (*Мїхаи́ла же и҆ Ѳео́дѡры* / *съ бг҃овѣнча́нною Ѽра́слію*), through whose imperial decree and synodical convening in 843 A.D. the Church was restored to peace and unity, and the iconoclast hierarchy deposed.
[^376]: Odes 4 and 5 of the Matins Canon: Physical leaf p164 (Printed fol. 160). Ode 4 develops Habakkuk's prophetic vision of the divine power, celebrating the empress who adorned the churches with icons of Christ and the saints out of longing for the heavenly Kingdom. Ode 5 draws upon Isaiah's morning vigil (*Ты̀ Гд҃и, мо́й свѣ́тъ въ мі́ръ прише́лъ є҆сѝ*), praying that the Church remain forever unshaken by heretical agitation, and that God exalt the horn of Orthodox rulers who honor His image.
[^377]: Kontakion and Ikos of the Sunday of Orthodoxy: Physical leaf p165 (Printed fol. 161). The Kontakion (Tone 6, Automelon: *Неѡпи́санное Сло́во Ѻ҆ц҃ее* / *Ὁ ἀπερίγραπτος Λόγος τοῦ Πατρός*) expresses the fundamental incarnational defense of icon veneration: the uncircumscribed Word of the Father accepted bodily circumscription through His Nativity from the Theotokos, thereby restoring the fallen and defiled image of man to its primordial divine beauty. The Ikos expounds this mystery as the culmination of the prophetic visions, leading to the confession of the Holy Trinity and baptism into Christ.
[^378]: Synaxarion of the Sunday of Orthodoxy (Part 1): Physical leaf p165 (Printed fol. 161). Composed by the Byzantine ecclesiastical historian Nicephorus Callistus Xanthopoulos (c. 1256–1335 A.D.). The narrative opens with elegiac couplets praising the becoming veneration of holy images, then details the outbreak of the First Iconoclasm under Leo III the Isaurian (717–741 A.D.), his deposition of Patriarch St. Germanus I in 730 A.D., and the legendary tale that Leo was seduced into iconoclasm in his youth through a magical pact with Jewish prognosticators in Syria. It outlines the succession through Constantine V Copronymus (741–775 A.D.) and Leo IV the Khazar (775–780 A.D.), leading to the restoration of icons at the Seventh Ecumenical Council (787 A.D.) under Empress Irene and Patriarch St. Tarasius, followed by the brief reigns of Nicephorus I, Stauracius, and Michael I Rangabe.
[^379]: Synaxarion of the Sunday of Orthodoxy (Part 2 — The Miracles of 843 A.D.): Physical leaf p166 (Printed fol. 162). Xanthopoulos chronicles the Second Iconoclasm under Leo V the Armenian (813–820 A.D.), Michael II the Amorian (820–829 A.D.), and Theophilus (829–842 A.D.). Upon Theophilus's agonized deathbed illness and repentance after kissing an enkolpion, Empress Theodora convened the Orthodox hierarchy. Patriarch John the Grammarian (Jannes) was deposed, and St. Methodius I of Constantinople installed as Patriarch. The marginal gloss *панкарпенникъ* (Greek: παγκάρπιον or παγκαρпоθήκη) designates the wicker grain-basket or tomb-like confinement on the island of Antigone in which St. Methodius was imprisoned for seven years prior to his patriarchate.
[^380]: Synaxarion of the Sunday of Orthodoxy (Part 3 — The Divine Absolution and the Triumph): Physical leaf p166 (Printed fol. 162). Xanthopoulos narrates the prophetic mission of the ascetics of Mount Olympus (St. Joannicius the Great, Arsacius, and Isaiah of Nicomedia), who directed Theodora and Methodius to restore the icons. The empress required an all-night intercession throughout the first week of Lent for the soul of her deceased husband Theophilus. In parallel visions, Theodora saw Theophilus pardoned before Christ at the Brazen Gate, while Methodius saw an angel announce the emperor's pardon; when Methodius opened the scroll placed beneath the Holy Table upon which the names of heretical emperors were inscribed, Theophilus's name was miraculously blotted out. The narrative concludes with the historical first Triumph of Orthodoxy procession on March 11, 843 A.D. (the First Sunday of Lent) to the Hippodrome, establishing the annual feast.
[^381]: Odes 7, 8, and 9 of the Matins Canon: Physical leaf p167 (Printed fol. 163). Ode 7 invokes the Three Holy Children in the Babylonian furnace as types of unwavering confession against royal idolatry. Ode 8 articulates the ancestral canonical tradition: "preserving the ancestral laws of the Church, we depict icons... clearly referring the honor and veneration of the icon unto the Prototype." Ode 9 praises the restoration of the Church's adorned beauty and concludes with prayers for the Christian sovereigns and the annulment of Eve's condemnation through the Theotokos.
[^382]: Praises (Ainoi) of Matins: Physical leaf p168 (Printed fol. 164). Four Resurrection stichera and one Anatolian sticheron are followed by three prosomoia of the Triodion in Tone 4, to the melody *Thou hast given a sign* (*Да́лъ є҆сѝ зна́меніе*). The second sticheron directly refutes docetic and Manichaean Christology ("the God-fighting children of Manes", *бг҃обо́рніи дѣ́ти Ма́нентовы*), confessing that the Son of God became manifest "not in mere appearance... but in truth and by the nature of flesh," which alone validates the physical depiction of Christ's humanity.
[^383]: Idiomelon of the Day at the Praises: Physical leaf p168 (Printed fol. 164). Composed in Tone 6 (*Мѡѷсе́й во вре́мѧ воздержа́нія...*), this hymn weaves together the great Old Testament types of ascetic fasting: Moses receiving the Law on Mount Sinai (Ex. 34:28), Elijah shutting the heavens and ascending to God (1 Kings 17–19), and the Three Children conquering the fiery Babylonian furnace (Dan. 1–3), concluding with an invocation of the Trisagion hymn as the gateway to the Resurrection.
[^384]: Divine Liturgy of St. Basil the Great and Refectory Rule: Physical leaf p169 (Printed fol. 165). For the First Sunday of Lent, the Liturgy appoints the Beatitudes on 6, Ode 6 of the Triodion Canon on 4; Prokeimenon in Tone 4 (*Song of the Fathers*, Dan. 3:26–27); Epistle to the Hebrews 11:24–26, 32–12:2 (section 324 from the half, recounting the triumphs of faith among the Old Testament prophets and martyrs); Alleluia in Tone 4 (Ps. 98:6); Gospel from John 1:43–51 (section 5, the calling of Philip and Nathanael); and Communion Hymns (Ps. 148:1 and Ps. 32:1). The Trapeza rubric permits cooked food with oil (*варе́ніе со є҆ле́емъ*) and wine in moderation (two cups at dinner and two at supper), while fish remains strictly prohibited.
[^385]: First Sunday of the Great Fast at Vespers: Physical leaf p169 (Printed fol. 165). Display headpiece featuring an ornamental woodcut band. Vespers opens the second week of Lent (*Постѡ́въ ны́нѣ двоседми́чїе свѣ́тлѡ начне́мъ*), setting 10 verses at "Lord, I have cried": 4 penitential stichera of the Octoechos, 2 prosomoia by St. Joseph the Hymnographer (Tone 4, Model: *Thou hast given a sign*), 1 by St. Theodore the Studite (Tone 6, Model: *In archangelic manner* / *А҆рха́гг҃льски*), and 3 from the Menaion. Following the Entrance with the censer, the Great Prokeimenon in Tone 8 is chanted: *Thou hast given an inheritance unto them that fear Thee, O Lord* (*Да́лъ є҆сѝ достоя́ніе боя́щымся тебѐ Гд҃и*, Ps. 60:6). The rubric notes that this Great Prokeimenon alternates with *Turn not away Thy face* on every Sunday evening throughout Great Lent until the Fifth Sunday.
[^386]: Aposticha of Sunday Evening Vespers: Physical leaf p170 (Printed fol. 166). The prayer *Vouchsafe, O Lord* introduces the resumption of the Lenten weekday prostrations and bows (*покло́ны*). The Aposticha idiomelon in Tone 8 (*Прїиди́те, ѡ҆чи́стимъ себѐ...*) sets forth Christ's teaching on secretive, vainglory-free almsgiving from Matthew 6:1–4 ("lest the left hand know what the right hand does"), concluding with the petition to the Father to forgive our trespasses.
[^387]: Compline on the First Sunday of the Great Fast: Physical leaf p170 (Printed fol. 166). Display headpiece featuring an ornamental woodcut band. At Compline, a dedicated Canon to the Holy Prophets is sung in Tone 5, authored by St. Theophanes Graptos, bearing the acrostic: *Ли́къ да пле́щетъ проро́кѡвъ благото́чнѡ* (Greek: *Χορὸς δὲ κρουέτω προφητῶν εὐρύθμως* — "Let the choir of the Prophets clap tunefully"). This canon extends the prophetic commemoration from the Sunday of Orthodoxy into the opening hours of the second week of the Great Fast.
