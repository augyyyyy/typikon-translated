[^1]: Printed in Cyrillic numerals: *҂ац҃ѕ* (1000 + 900 + 6 = 1906). The typography of the Stauropegion Institute in Lviv (attached to the historical Dormition Church and Brotherhood founded in the 16th century) published standard liturgical books for the Greek Catholic Church in Galicia. Physical Leaf p1 = Unnumbered Title Page.
[^2]: Inscription dedicates this exemplar to Bishop Ivan Prasko (1914–2001), Greek Catholic Bishop for Ukrainian Catholics in Australia, New Zealand, and Oceania (consecrated 1958, Eparch of SS. Peter and Paul of Melbourne from 1982), from Fr. Ihor Shpytkovsky, dated in Melbourne, July 29, 1968. Physical Leaf p1 = Unnumbered Title Page.
[^3]: Engraving of the architectural ensemble of the Lviv Stauropegion Dormition Church (Успенська церква) on Ruska Street in Lviv, featuring the Korniakt Tower (66 meters, built 1572–1578 by architect Peter Barbon), the Chapel of the Three Hierarchs (built 1578–1591 by Peter Krasowski), and the main church (built 1591–1629 by Paulo Romano). The bottom calligraphic vyaz inscription reads: *ТВОРЕНІЕ ЦАРЕВО И СЫНА ЕГО* ("The Work of the King and His Son"), evoking both the celestial King and His Son and the royal patrons who endowed the brotherhood. Physical Leaf p2 = Full-page frontispiece plate.
[^4]: The official hierarchy dedicatory leaf dated June 26, 1906 (Decree No. 1946), during the pontificate of Pope St. Pius X (reigned 1903–1914) and the reign of Austro-Hungarian Emperor Franz Joseph I (reigned 1848–1916). Servant of God Andrey Sheptytsky served as Metropolitan of Halych and Archbishop of Lviv from 1900 to 1944. Physical Leaf p3 = Dedicatory leaf.
[^5]: Mykola Herasymovych and Roman Ilnytsky served as Senior Wardens (*старѣйшины*) of the Stauropegion Institute. Fr. Volodymyr Hurhula (1866–1938) was a prominent Greek Catholic priest, liturgist, and editor of liturgical books at the Stauropegion typography. Physical Leaf p3 = Dedicatory leaf.
[^6]: An opening prayer to Our Lord Jesus Christ traditionally printed before the liturgical corpus in Ruthenian and Slavic Triodia, beseeching divine grace, compunction, and perseverance through the upcoming holy fast. Physical Leaf p4 = Unnumbered front matter prayer leaf.

[^8]: Psalm 140 (LXX; MT Psalm 141).
[^9]: The Anatolian stichera (*Анатолієвы*) are attributed to Anatolius, Patriarch of Constantinople (449–458) or to an 8th/9th-century hymnographer of the same name.
[^10]: The three solemn penitential troparia (*Покаянія отверзи ми двери, Жизнодавче*, *На спасения стези настави мя, Богородице*, *Множества содѣланныхъ мною лютыхъ*) are chanted at Sunday Matins after Psalm 50 beginning on the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee and continuing through the Fifth Sunday of Great Lent.
[^11]: St. George of Nicomedia (9th century), chartophylax of the Great Church and metropolitan of Nicomedia, prominent Byzantine hymnographer. The Greek acrostic ΓΕΩΡΓΙΟΥ ("Of George") is formed by the initial letters of the Theotokia across the nine odes of the canon and is preserved in Church Slavonic transcription in the printed margins (*Г-Є-Ѡ-Р-Г-І-Ѡ-У*). Physical Leaf p6 = Printed Page 2.
[^12]: Nikephoros Kallistos Xanthopoulos (c. 1256–c. 1335), Byzantine ecclesiastical historian, poet, and monk of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople, author of the verse synaxaria and historical introductions for the movable festal cycle of the Triodion and Pentecostarion. Physical Leaf p8 = Printed Page 4.
[^13]: St. Cosmas the Melodist (Bishop of Maiuma, c. 675–752), foster-brother of St. John of Damascus, who first instituted canons consisting of three odes (*triodia*) for the days of Great and Holy Passion Week. The corpus was subsequently expanded by St. Theodore the Studite (759–826) and his brother St. Joseph the Studite (d. 830) to encompass the entire pre-Lenten and Lenten cycle. Physical Leaf p8 = Printed Page 4.
