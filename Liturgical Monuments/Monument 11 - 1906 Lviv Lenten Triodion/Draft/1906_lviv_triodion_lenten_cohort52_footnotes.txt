[^1410]: Exodus 15:6–7 (LXX): "Thy right hand, O Lord, is glorified in strength; Thy right hand, O Lord, hath broken the enemies; and in the multitude of Thy glory Thou hast crushed the adversaries."

[^1411]: The classical etiological explanation for the Greek name *Akathistos* (*Ἀκάθιστος*, "not-sitting"): the clergy, monastics, and people of Constantinople remained standing throughout the whole-night vigil of thanksgiving for the deliverance of the Queen of Cities in August 626 AD.

[^1412]: The Second Arab Siege of Constantinople (674–678 AD) under the Umayyad Caliph Mu'awiya I and Emperor Constantine IV Pogonatos ("the Bearded", r. 668–685 AD). The Slavonic term *Roistolom* reflects the Byzantine Greek *Rhodostolos* (literally "commander of the fleet" or "Rhodes-expedition commander"). The surviving Arab fleet was wrecked by a severe storm off the coast of Syllaeum in Pamphylia.

[^1413]: The Third Arab Siege of Constantinople (717–718 AD) under Caliph Sulayman / Umar II and general Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik, during the reign of Emperor Leo III the Isaurian (r. 717–741 AD). The chroniclers record an invading fleet of 1,800 vessels.

[^1414]: Psalm 82:2–5 (LXX) [Psalm 83:1–4 Masoretic]: "O God, who shall be like unto Thee? Hold not Thy peace, and be not still, O God. For lo, Thine enemies have made a noise; and they that hate Thee have lifted up their head... They have said, Come, and let us destroy them from being a nation"; and Philippians 2:11: "And that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."

[^1415]: Emperor Leo III formed an alliance with Khan Tervel of Bulgaria, whose armies assaulted the Arab land forces besieging Constantinople in 718 AD, inflicting over 20,000 casualties and forcing the lifting of the land siege.

[^1416]: The great defensive iron chain stretched across the Golden Horn between the walls of Constantinople and the Fortress of Galata (Kastellion), preventing hostile naval vessels from penetrating the harbor; Byzantine ships equipped with siphon tubes projector-discharged "Greek Fire" (*liquid fire*, invented by Kallinikos of Heliopolis), setting the Arab ships ablaze.

[^1417]: During the harsh winter of 717–718 AD, snow covered the ground for over a hundred days, cutting off Arab supply lines and causing devastating famine and pestilence among the besiegers, forcing many to desert or surrender to the Byzantine forces.

[^1418]: In the autumn of 718 AD, as the remnant of the Arab fleet attempted to sail through the Aegean Sea, an undersea volcanic eruption and tempest near Thera (Santorini) superheated the sea waters and showered the vessels with burning pumice and hail, boiling the pitch and sinking virtually the entire fleet, with only five (in this Slavonic account, three) vessels surviving to reach Syria.

[^1419]: Psalm 73:13 (LXX) [Psalm 74:13 Masoretic]: "Thou didst divide the sea by Thy strength; Thou didst crush the heads of the dragons in the water"; and Exodus 15:1: "Let us sing unto the Lord, for He is gloriously glorified: the horse and his rider hath He thrown into the sea."

[^1420]: Romans 12:9 ("Let love be without hypocrisy"); 1 Timothy 1:5; Galatians 5:22–23; Ephesians 4:1–6. St. John Chrysostom and early Christian commentators frequently emphasize that internal virtuous living and love without hypocrisy constitute spiritual instruments far more pleasing to God than musical instruments and vocal praise alone.

[^1421]: A classical Byzantine homiletic and rhetorical metaphor, likening the end of a long sermon or panegyric to a vessel lowering its sails and entering the calm haven of silence after traversing a vast ocean of divine wonders.

[^1422]: The *Markian Chapters* (*Главы́ Ма́рковы*, *Kaphalaia tou Markou*), attributed to Hieromonk Mark of the Monastery of St. Sabas or Bishop Mark of Otranto, provide precise combinatorial rubrics for resolving coincidences between immovable feasts of the Menaion and movable liturgical days of the Triodion and Pentecostarion.

[^1423]: The Feast of the Three Holy Hierarchs (St. Basil the Great, St. Gregory the Theologian, and St. John Chrysostom, January 30) coinciding with the Sundays of the Publican and Pharisee, the Prodigal Son, or Meatfare Sunday. The rubrics refer to the order prescribed for the First and Second Findings of the Precious Head of St. John the Baptist (February 24).

[^1424]: Rubrics for the Forefeast of the Meeting of the Lord (February 1) when it falls on the Sunday of the Publican and Pharisee, the Prodigal Son, Meatfare Sunday, or Cheesefare Sunday.

[^1425]: Meatfare Saturday is dedicated universally to the commemoration of all departed Orthodox Christians (Saturday of Souls), while Cheesefare Saturday commemorates all the holy ascetic fathers and mothers who shone forth in the monastic life.

[^1426]: Rubrics for the Great Feast of the Meeting of the Lord (*Hypapante*, February 2) coinciding with the Pre-Lenten Sundays (Publican and Pharisee, Prodigal Son, Meatfare, or Cheesefare).

[^1427]: An important liturgical provision of the Sabbaite and Ruthenian Typika: when the Great Feast of the Meeting of the Lord coincides with a Pre-Lenten Sunday, the praises (*lauds*) stichera of the Triodion are transferred to the Litia at Vespers on Saturday evening so that none of the appointed Sunday Triodion hymnography is omitted.

[^1428]: Because a Great Feast of the Lord cannot admit funeral commemorations, when the Meeting of the Lord falls on Meatfare Saturday, the entire universal Memorial Service for the Departed is transferred either to the preceding Saturday or to the preceding Thursday of Meatfare Week.

[^1429]: The Afterfeast (*Poprazdnstvo*) of the Meeting of the Lord, encompassing the days following February 2 during which the festal hymns continue to be sung alongside the daily services of the Octoechos, Menaion, and Triodion.

[^1430]: The Apodosis (*Otdanie*, Greek *Apodosis*) of the Meeting of the Lord: according to the Typikon, the apodosis cannot be celebrated on Meatfare Sunday or during Clean Week; its celebration is therefore adjusted according to the day of the week on which the feast falls, concluding at the latest on Cheesefare Sunday.
