[^1086]: The prose Synaxarion for Thursday of the Fifth Week of the Great Fast concludes its account of the Great Canon. It details the life and ecclesiastical offices of St. Andrew of Crete (Damascene and Jerusalemite), who was sent by Patriarch Theodore of Jerusalem to Constantinople for the Sixth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople III, 680–681), where he defended Orthodox christology against Monothelitism. In Constantinople, St. Andrew was ordained deacon and appointed curator of the patriarchal orphanage (orphanotrophos), later becoming Archbishop of Crete. On his return journey from Constantinople, he fell asleep in the Lord near a harbor called Hieros on the island of Mytilene (Lesbos). The text contrasts the Great Canon (numbering 250 troparia) with ordinary Byzantine canons (having ~30 troparia), and highlights the simultaneous appointment of the Life of St. Mary of Egypt by St. Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem (c. 560–638).
[^1087]: The Beatitudes with Troparia (Блаже́нны съ покло́ны / Μακαρισμοί) appointed at Matins of Thursday of the Fifth Week, chanted in Tone 6 with prostrations (metanias). The hymnographer interleaves the Gospel Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–12) with penitential strophes drawing upon the repentance of the Good Thief (Luke 23:42) and Old Testament types of Judges and early rulers. Manoah and his barren wife (Judges 13) prefigure divine promise, while Samson shorn of his hair by Delilah (Judges 16) allegorizes the soul stripped of virtuous deeds by carnal passion.
[^1088]: Old Testament types in the Beatitudes troparia: Samson conquered by Delilah (Judges 16); Barak and Deborah (Judges 4–5); Jephthah the judge (Judges 11); Jael slaying Sisera with a tent peg (Judges 4:21), a classic patristic type of the Cross defeating Satan; and Jephthah offering his daughter as a vowed sacrifice (Judges 11:30–40), allegorized as the soul immolating carnal passions before God.
[^1089]: Gideon's fleece receiving heavenly dew and his soldiers lapping water like dogs at the spring of Harod (Judges 6:36–40; 7:4–7); the condemnation of the high priest Eli for failing to discipline his sacrilegious sons Hophni and Phinehas (1 Kingdoms 2:12–36 [1 Samuel 2:12–36]); the Levite of Ephraim cutting the body of his abused concubine into twelve pieces and sending them throughout the tribes of Israel to expose the sin of the Benjaminites of Gibeah (Judges 19); and the barren Hannah praying silently in the tabernacle at Shiloh, moving only her lips, to give birth to Samuel (1 Kingdoms 1 [1 Samuel 1]).
[^1090]: Samuel raised in the tabernacle at Shiloh and dedicated to the Lord (1 Kingdoms 1–3); King David anointed with the horn of divine oil by Samuel (1 Kingdoms 16:13), urging the soul to receive the mystical anointing of penitential tears; followed by the Beatitudes Doxasticon and Theotokion confessing Christ as one divine Person (Hypostasis) in two distinct unconfused natures, echoing the Definition of Chalcedon (451).
[^1091]: Ode 7 Heirmos of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "We have sinned, we have transgressed, we have done unrighteously before Thee" (Согрѣши́хомъ, беззако́нновахомъ / Ἡμάρтомен, ἠνομήσαμεν, ἠδικήσαμεν ἐνώπιόν σου), based upon the Canticle of the Three Holy Children in the Babylonian furnace (Daniel 3:26–45 / Prayer of Azariah).
[^1092]: Saul seeking the lost asses of his father Kish and unexpectedly receiving the royal anointing from Samuel (1 Kingdoms 9–10 [1 Samuel 9–10]), warning the soul not to prefer irrational passions over the Kingdom of Christ.
[^1093]: King David's double sin of adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah the Hittite (2 Kingdoms 11 [2 Samuel 11]), contrasted with his immediate confession before the prophet Nathan and his penitential composition of Psalm 50 (LXX); Uzzah (За́нъ) struck dead for irreverently touching the Ark of the Covenant when the oxen stumbled (2 Kingdoms 6:6–7); Absalom's unnatural rebellion against his father David and the defilement of his father's concubines (2 Kingdoms 15–16); and the wicked counsel of Ahithophel overthrown by the providence of God (2 Kingdoms 15–17).
[^1094]: Solomon's exemplary wisdom corrupted in old age through his foreign wives who led his heart after strange gods (3 Kingdoms 11 [1 Kings 11]); Rehoboam provoking the division of the kingdom by despising the counsel of the elders (3 Kingdoms 12); Jeroboam leading the northern tribes into golden-calf idolatry at Bethel and Dan (3 Kingdoms 12:26–33); and Ahab's tyrannical wickedness and idolatry incited by Queen Jezebel (3 Kingdoms 16:30–33; 21).
[^1095]: Elijah calling down fire from heaven upon the two captains of fifty sent by King Ahaziah (4 Kingdoms 1 [2 Kings 1]) and slaughtering the 450 prophets of Baal at Mount Carmel (3 Kingdoms 18:40); the heaven shut from rain for three and a half years during Ahab's reign (3 Kingdoms 17:1; Luke 4:25), and the destitute widow of Zarephath (Sarepta) sustaining the prophet with her last handful of meal and cruse of oil (3 Kingdoms 17:8–24); and King Manasseh's horrific idolatry followed by heartfelt repentance in Babylonian captivity (2 Chronicles 33:11–13; Prayer of Manasseh).
[^1096]: David's penitential plea in Psalm 50:14 (LXX): "Restore unto me the joy of Thy salvation" (Возда́ждь мѝ ра́дость спасе́нїя твоегѡ̀ / Ἀπόδος μοι τὴν ἀγαλλίασιν τοῦ σωτηρίου σου); and King Hezekiah weeping upon his bed when stricken with mortal sickness, granted fifteen additional years of life through the prophet Isaiah (4 Kingdoms 20:1–6; Isaiah 38:1–5).
[^1097]: Ode 8 Heirmos of the Triodion to the Holy Apostles, Tone 8, by St. Joseph the Hymnographer: "The beginningless King of glory" (Безнача́льнаго Царя̀ сла́вы / Τὸν ἐν ὄρει ἁγίῳ δοξασθέντα), drawing upon the Canticle of the Three Holy Children (Daniel 3:57–88). The Apostles are likened to glowing coals of immaterial fire and trumpets of the Word casting down the spiritual walls of Jericho (Joshua 6).
[^1098]: Second Triodion of Ode 8 by St. Theodore the Studite (759–826), sharing the same heirmos, praising the Holy Apostles as divine architects who established the Church upon the foundation of Christ and overthrew the deception of pagan idolatry.
[^1099]: Ode 8 Heirmos of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "Him Whom the heavenly hosts glorify" (Е҆го́же во́инства небе́сная сла́вятъ / Ὃν στρατιαὶ οὐρανῶν δοξάζουσι), based upon the Song of the Three Holy Children (Daniel 3:57–88).
[^1100]: Old Testament types of ascetic ascent and judgment in Ode 8: Elijah taken up to heaven in a chariot of fire (4 Kingdoms 2:11); Elisha parting the Jordan with Elijah's mantle and receiving a double portion of his spirit (4 Kingdoms 2:9–14); the hospitable Shunammite woman receiving Elisha (4 Kingdoms 4:8–37); Gehazi struck with Naaman's leprosy for his covetous deceit (4 Kingdoms 5:20–27); King Uzziah stricken with leprosy for presumptuously burning incense upon the altar (2 Chronicles 26:16–21); and the Ninevites repenting at the preaching of Jonah (Jonah 3:5–10).
[^1101]: Jeremiah cast into the muddy cistern of Malchiah during the siege of Jerusalem and lamenting over the ruins of Zion (Jeremiah 38:6; Lamentations); Jonah fleeing to Tarshish because he foreknew God's boundless mercy would spare Nineveh (Jonah 1:3; 4:2); Daniel unharmed in the lions' den (Daniel 6); and the Three Holy Children unharmed in the fiery furnace (Daniel 3).
[^1102]: New Testament archetypes of repentance in Ode 8: the Good Thief (Luke 23:42); Peter weeping bitterly after his threefold denial (Matthew 26:75); the Publican in the temple (Luke 18:13); the sinful woman washing Christ's feet with tears (Luke 7:37–38); the Canaanite woman pleading for her demon-possessed daughter (Matthew 15:22–28); the woman with the issue of blood healed by touching Christ's hem (Matthew 9:20–22); Martha and Mary weeping at the tomb of Lazarus (John 11); and the Good Shepherd rescuing the lost sheep (Luke 15:4–7).
[^1103]: The hymnographer evokes the Dread Judgment and the fiery river (Daniel 7:9–10), praising the ascetic flight of St. Mary of Egypt, who astonished the desert elder Zosimas by appearing as an earthly angel, and imploring the intercession of St. Andrew of Crete as an initiate of repentance.
[^1104]: Ode 9 Heirmos of the Triodion to the Holy Apostles, Tone 8, by St. Joseph the Hymnographer: "Thee we confess to be truly the Mother of God" (Вои́стиннꙋ Богоро́дицꙋ тя̀ и҆сповѣ́дꙋемъ / Ἀληθῶς Θεοτόκον, σὲ ὁμολογοῦμεν), addressing the Apostles as spiritual springs of water and salt of the earth (Matthew 5:13) cleansing the soul from corrupt passions.
[^1105]: Second Triodion of Ode 9 by St. Theodore the Studite, with its Heirmos: "Thee, mediatress of heaven and earth" (Тя̀ небесѐ и҆ землѝ хода́таицꙋ / Τὴν οὐρανοῦ καὶ γῆς μεσίτην), honoring the Apostles as divine fishermen catching rational fish with the net of the Gospel (Matthew 4:19; Luke 5:10).
[^1106]: Ode 9 Heirmos of the Great Canon, Tone 6: "The childbearing of a seedless conception is ineffable" (Безсѣ́меннагѡ Зача́тїа Рождество̀ несказа́нное / Ἀσπόρου συλλήψεως, ὁ τόκος ἀνερμήνευτος), drawing upon the Canticle of the Theotokos (Magnificat, Luke 1:46–55) and the mystery of the virginal conception.
[^1107]: The opening of Ode 9 of the Great Canon presents a profound psychological and spiritual diagnosis of total human brokenness: the mind covered in sores (mind wounded), the body sickened, the spirit languishing, discourse failing, life deadened, and the end at the doors. St. Andrew then opens his final scriptural recapitulation with Moses' book of Genesis (Міробытїѐ / Κοσμογένεσις), calling the soul to examine the righteous and the sinners from the dawn of creation.
