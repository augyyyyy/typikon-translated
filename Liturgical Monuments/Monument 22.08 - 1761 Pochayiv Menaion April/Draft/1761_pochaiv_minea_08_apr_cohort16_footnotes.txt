[^180]: Reference to the prophecy of Isaiah 19:19: “In that day shall there be an altar to the Lord in the midst of the land of Egypt, and a pillar at the border thereof to the Lord.” The hymnographer poetically identifies the Holy Gospel according to Mark as this prophetic pillar in Egypt, where St. Mark founded the Church of Alexandria.
[^181]: The Slavonic rubric *судо́блаѧ* (*sudoblaya*) designates the appointed or prescribed festive ode designated by the Typikon for the Beatitudes, alternating with Ode 3 of the saint's canon.

[^182]: The printed Pochaiv text reads *ст҃ль мѣ́илсѧ є҆сѝ*, an evident typographical error or defective ligature for *свѧти́тель ꙗ҆ви́лсѧ є҆сѝ* (“thou didst appear as a hierarch”). Translated according to the clear sense of the office.
[^183]: The Pochaiv typesetter set both *сба́ти* (an erroneous partial typesetting of *сбавити* or *смягчити*) and its intended correction *оу҆ма́лити* (“to diminish”) directly side by side in the printed text (*де́рзость сба́ти оу҆ма́лити*). The redundant struck word *сбати* is omitted in translation.
[^184]: The printed text omits the initial letter *Ꙗ* in the podoben incipit (*Вї́лся* for *Ꙗ҆ви́лся є҆сѝ дне́сь* / “Thou hast appeared today”).

[^185]: According to the Life of St. Basil, Bishop of Amasea (martyred under Emperor Licinius, c. 322), after the saint was beheaded, his severed head and body were thrown separately into the Black Sea. By divine providence, both were washed ashore at Sinope, where fishermen discovered that the head had marvelously rejoined the neck with only a crimson line marking the stroke of the sword.
[^186]: St. Symeon, son of Cleopas (the brother of St. Joseph the Betrothed), succeeded St. James the Brother of the Lord as the second Bishop of Jerusalem. In extreme old age (traditionally 120 years), during the reign of Trajan, he was tortured and crucified, thus sharing the manner of Christ’s own Passion as celebrated throughout this office.
[^187]: St. Symeon of Jerusalem was martyred by crucifixion under the Roman proconsul Atticus in c. 106/107 AD; the hymnographer constantly highlights this direct physical imitation of the Passion of Christ on the Tree of the Cross.
