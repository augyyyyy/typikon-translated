[^75]: St. Theodore Trichinas (Theodore the Hairshirt-Wearer, d. c. 400), commemorated April 20, derived his epithet from the rough goat-hair garment (*trichinon*) he wore throughout his ascetic life in Thrace near Constantinople.
[^76]: The Staurotheotokion is prescribed in the Menaion when the commemoration occurs on a Wednesday or Friday during non-festal periods.
[^77]: An allegorical allusion to the carnal lusts and bondage of worldly passions, typified by Egypt in monastic ascetic literature.
[^78]: The Kontakion of St. Theodore Trichinas, sung in Tone 4 to the Podoben "Thou hast appeared today".
[^79]: An allusion to the Burning Bush of Sinai (Exodus 3:2), prefiguring the virgin birth of Christ without violation of virginity.
[^80]: The name Theodore (Greek: Θεόδωρος) translates literally as "Gift of God" (*theos* + *doron*), alluded to in the hymnographic text (`бж҃їѧ да́ра тезоимени́те`).
[^81]: St. Januarius (San Gennaro), bishop of Benevento, suffered martyrdom under Diocletian c. 305 with his companions. St. Theodore of Perge in Pamphylia was a soldier martyred under Antoninus Pius c. 138–161.
[^82]: The six companion martyrs of St. Januarius: the deacons Socius and Proculus, the lector Desiderius, Faustus, Eutychius, and Acutius.
[^83]: The Heirmos of Tone 4 Ode 1 invokes the Greek *tristatai* (Exodus 14:7, LXX: τριστάτας), the Egyptian chariot officers/captains drowned in the Red Sea, here allegorized as the tripartite faculties of the soul (*rational, irascible, concupiscent*).
[^84]: St. Januarius and his companions were thrown to wild beasts in the Flavian Amphitheatre at Pozzuoli, but the beasts refused to touch them, echoing Daniel in the lions' den (Daniel 6:16–22).
[^85]: Dioscorus, a soldier or prison guard who witnessed the miracles of St. Theodore of Perge, was converted to Christ and martyred by fire.
[^86]: According to the Acts of St. Januarius, when the judge Timotheus was struck with sudden blindness, the holy bishop Januarius prayed and restored his sight, yet the tyrant remained obstinate in his paganism.
