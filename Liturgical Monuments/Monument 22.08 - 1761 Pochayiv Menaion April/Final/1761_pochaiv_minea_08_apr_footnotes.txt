[^20]: St. Eutychius, Patriarch of Constantinople (c. 512–582; commemorated April 6), presided over the Fifth Ecumenical Council (Constantinople II, 553), was exiled under Justinian for refusing Aphthartodocetism, and was later restored.

[^24]: The Holy Apostles Herodion, Agabus, Asyncritus, Rufus, Phlegon, and Hermes (and Hermas) of the Seventy are commemorated on April 8. [^26]: St. Herodion (Rodion), a kinsman of the Apostle Paul (cf. Rom. 16:11), was consecrated the first bishop of Patras in Achaea, where he suffered martyrdom.

[^27]: St. Phlegon, of the Seventy Apostles, was bishop of Marathon in Attica.

[^28]: St. Hermes, of the Seventy Apostles, was bishop of Dalmatia.

[^29]: St. Eupsychius of Caesarea in Cappadocia (martyred c. 362 under Julian the Apostate; commemorated April 9) destroyed the pagan temple of Fortune (Tyche), for which he was tortured and beheaded.

[^30]: Slavonic acrostic: Е҆ѵѱѵ́хїа бл҃годꙋ́шнѡ пою̀ болѣ́зни. Творе́нїе І҆ѡ́сифово (Courageously I sing the sufferings of Eupsychius. The work of Joseph [the Hymnographer]).

[^31]: The Holy Martyrs Terence, Pompeius, Africanus, Maximus, Macarius, and their 36 companions were martyred at Carthage in Africa during the Decian persecution (c. 250; commemorated April 10).

[^32]: Slavonic acrostic: Мно́жество мѧ̀ спасѝ добропобѣ́дныхъ, мꙋ́ченикѡвъ. Творе́нїе І҆ѡ́сифово (Save me, O multitude of fair-victorious martyrs. The work of Joseph [the Hymnographer]).

[^33]: The Holy Hieromartyr Antipas, Bishop of Pergamum in Asia, was a disciple of the Apostle John the Theologian (cf. Rev. 2:13) and was martyred by being cast into a red-hot bronze bull under Domitian (c. 92; commemorated April 11).

[^34]: Slavonic acrostic: Свѣ́тлаго въ мꙋ́ченицѣхъ А҆нти́пꙋ чествꙋ́ю. Творе́нїе І҆ѡ́сифово (I honor Antipas, radiant among martyrs. The work of Joseph [the Hymnographer]).

[^35]: Our Venerable Father Basil the Confessor, Bishop of Parium in Mysia (commemorated April 12), opposed the Iconoclasts during the eighth-century persecutions, enduring exile and tribulations for his defense of the holy icons.

[^38]: The Holy Hieromartyr Artemon, Presbyter of Laodicea in Phrygia (commemorated April 13), ministered into extreme old age and was martyred under Diocletian (c. 303).

[^39]: In the Greek and Church Slavonic Menaion, the acrostic of the canon is alphabetical (Greek: Ἡ ἀκροστιχίς κατ᾽ ἀλφάβητον; Church Slavonic: По алфави́тꙋ), with the stanzas originally keyed to the letters of the Greek alphabet.

[^40]: Slavonic: стрѣлоно́сче ("arrow-bearer"; Greek: βελοφόρε), denoting one who bears the marks or darts of martyrdom for Christ.

[^42]: Saint Martin the Confessor, Pope of Rome (649–655, commemorated April 14), convened the Lateran Council of 649 in Rome to condemn Monothelitism, for which he was arrested by Emperor Constans II, brought to Constantinople, tortured, and exiled to Cherson in Taurica (Crimea), where he died in 655.

[^43]: The canon to Saint Martin the Confessor is the work of Joseph the Hymnographer, with the acrostic in the Greek Menaion: Μαρτῖνον ὑμνῶ τὸν σοφὸν Ῥώμης πρόεδρον. Ἰωσήφ (I hymn Martin, the wise prelate of Rome. Joseph).

[^44]: At the Lateran Council of 649, Pope Saint Martin I condemned the Monothelite leaders Pyrrhus, Sergius I, and Paul II of Constantinople, Theodore of Pharan, and Cyrus of Alexandria.

[^47]: The acrostic of the canon in the Greek Menaion is: Θείας μαθηταῖς θεῖον ἐξᾴδω μέλος. Ἰωσήφ ("Unto the divine disciples a divine song I chant. Joseph"), translated in Slavonic as: Бж҃їимъ оу҆ченикѡ́мъ бж҃твеннꙋю воспѣва́ю пѣ́снь. Творе́нїе Їѡ́сифово.

[^48]: Slavonic: і҆́дѡльскꙋю ве́щь попали́сте ("burned up the matter/stuff of idols"), corresponding to Greek ὕλην εἰδώλων.

[^49]: The Holy Virgin-Martyrs Agape, Irene, and Chionia were sisters from Aquileia (or Thessalonica) who suffered martyrdom under Diocletian (c. 304). After refusing to eat sacrificial meats and hiding sacred Christian books during the persecutions, Agape and Chionia were burned alive, while Irene was subsequently pierced by an arrow.

[^50]: The canon to the Holy Virgin-Martyrs Agape, Irene, and Chionia is attributed in the title to Joseph the Hymnographer (*Творе́нїе Їѡ́сифово*), Tone 8.

[^52]: Slavonic: `велехва́лный ѡ҆нѣмѣ̀` ("the boastful one was struck dumb"), referring to the persecuting Roman magistrate Dulcitius or the judge Sisinius.

[^53]: The printed Slavonic text reads `сла́ва та̀ ра́достнѡ` ("praising thee joyfully"), where `сла́ва` is an epigraphic error in the Pochaiv print for `сла́вѧ` (participial "glorifying/praising thee").

[^54]: Both Saint Symeon and Saint Acacius are titled in the printed rubric as `прпⷡднагѡ` ("our venerable father"). Saint Symeon of Persia was Bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon and was martyred by beheading under King Shapur II of Persia on Good Friday in 341 (or 344), along with over a thousand fellow Christians and clergy; he is therefore celebrated as a Hieromartyr. Saint Acacius was Bishop of Melitene in Armenia (5th century), an outspoken defender of Orthodoxy at the Third Ecumenical Council in Ephesus (431) against Nestorius, and renowned as a wonderworker.

[^55]: The printed Slavonic text reads `нѣ̑маѧ` ("speechless/mute things"), an apparent compositor error for `нб҃снаѧ` (*небе́снаѧ*, "heavenly things"), parallel to the antitheses: "earthly/heavenly, transient/enduring, corruptible/incorruptible" (cf. Greek τὰ οὐράνια τοῖς ἐπιγείοις).

[^56]: Slavonic: `посрами́въ свѣ́тство`, an epigraphic corruption in the Pochaiv print for `свирѣ́пство` ("ferocity/cruelty") or `совѣ́тъ` ("counsel/assembly"), referring to the deposition of Nestorius and the condemnation of his heresy at the Council of Ephesus.

[^57]: The acrostic in the Greek Menaion is: Στέφω σε τὸν μάκαρα Συмеὼν ᾄσμασιν. Ἰωσήφ ("I crown thee, the blessed Symeon, with songs. Joseph"), translated into Church Slavonic as: Вѣнча́ю блаже́ннаго тѧ̀ Сѷмеѡ́на пѣснопѣ́нїи. Творе́нїе І҆ѡ́сифово.

[^58]: Slavonic: `пре́лести мре́теченїа ... ста́до сла́вне`. The printed word `мре́теченїа` appears to be a typesetting corruption for `мꙋ́тнотече́нїѧ` ("turbid currents") or `моретече́нїѧ` ("surging currents of the sea").

[^59]: Slavonic: `Седо́мь свѧ́занъ`, an archaic form or typographical variant for `сѣди́нами свѧ́занъ` ("bound in gray-haired old age") or `сꙋдо́мъ` ("by the judgment/tribunal"); tradition records that Saint Symeon suffered martyrdom at an advanced age under King Shapur II.

[^60]: Saint Ananias was a presbyter and fellow martyr of Saint Symeon in Persia. The phrase `подвѣ́сныѧ ... мꙋ́ченики` refers to those martyrs who were suspended or hung up during their torture before their decapitation.

[^61]: According to the Life of Saint Acacius of Melitene, the river overflowing its banks threatened to flood the city. Acacius prayed, set a large stone by the riverbed as a boundary mark, and commanded the waters in Christ's name never to transgress that stone, whereupon the flood abated.

[^62]: The printed Slavonic reads `нена́гѡ бо цр҃ѧ позна̀` ("for he recognized [the servant] of the heavenly King"), where `нена́гѡ` is a contracted/abbreviated compositor form for `небе́снагѡ` (`нб҃снагѡ`). The troparion refers to the pious Byzantine emperor (Theodosius II) receiving Saint Acacius with deep reverence.

[^63]: Slavonic: `ѡ҆́брѣбаемое` (or `ѡ҆́брѣтаемое`), an epigraphic corruption for `ѡ҆бꙋрева́емое` ("storm-tossed, tempest-tossed").

[^64]: Slavonic: `по́дъ а҆ка́кїе` (or `впре́дъ` / `па́ки`). The troparion recalls the miracle in the Life of Saint Acacius where a swarm of biting flies or gnats plagued the sick; upon the saint's prayer and command, they vanished and never returned.

[^65]: Saint Symeon of Persia was martyred together with about a hundred fellow Christians, priests, and deacons (according to one tradition, 100 martyrs on Good Friday; in another expanded account, up to 1,000 or 1,150 during the week). The Slavonic term `сточи́сленное` translates the Greek ἑκατοντάριθμον ("hundred-numbered").

[^66]: The Life of Saint Acacius records that while he was praying near a marsh or river, the loud croaking of frogs disturbed the divine service. Acacius addressed the frogs, bidding them be silent and not interrupt prayer to God, and they remained mute.

[^67]: During a drought in Melitene, Saint Acacius prayed, and torrential rain fell, opening the heavens (like Elijah), and in another circumstance water miraculously gushed forth from dry rock.

[^70]: The phrase *видѣ́нїемъ и҆ дѣ́янымъ любому́дрїемъ* translates the Greek ascetic hendiadys θεωρίᾳ καὶ πράξει ('by contemplation and practical ascetic action/philosophy').
