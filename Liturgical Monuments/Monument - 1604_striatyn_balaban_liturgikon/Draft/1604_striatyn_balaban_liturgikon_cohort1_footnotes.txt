[^1]: The Striatyn Liturgikon (*Служебник*) of 1604 was printed at the private printing press established in Striatyn (near Rohatyn, Western Ukraine) by Bishop Hedeon Balaban of Lviv and his nephew Fedir Balaban, with typography directed by Pamvo Berynda. The woodcut architectural border features the Korczak heraldic arms of the Balaban family at the foot.
[^2]: The Church Slavonic title uses the collective titular formula *И́же въ ст҃ы́хъ ѿц҃ъ на́шихъ* ("Of our Fathers among the Saints") governing the three classical Byzantine liturgies: the Divine Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom, the Divine Liturgy of St. Basil the Great, and the Divine Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts (attributed to St. Gregory the Dialogist).
[^3]: Striatyn (*Стрятин*), a town in the Ruthenian Voivodeship of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, was the seat of the Balaban press which issued two monumental liturgical editions: this 1604 Liturgikon (*Sluzhebnik*) and the 1606 Euchologion (*Trebnyk*), before its typographic equipment was acquired in 1615 by Archimandrite Yelysei Pletenetsky for the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra.
[^4]: Hedeon Balaban (c. 1530–1607) was the Orthodox Bishop of Lviv, Halych, and Kamianets-Podilskyi from 1569 until his death. Although initially participating in early discussions surrounding the Union of Brest, he formally rejected the Union in 1595–1596 and remained the primary hierarch defending the Eastern Orthodox Church in the Ruthenian lands of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth.
[^5]: The Balaban family bore the Polish-Ruthenian noble coat of arms *Korczak* (*Wręby*), which blazons three horizontal silver bars (*wręby*) on a red shield, surmounted by a helmet crest depicting a silver talbot/hound sitting within a golden bowl or floating basin.
[^6]: In 1589, Patriarch Jeremias II Tranos of Constantinople visited the Ruthenian lands and bestowed upon Bishop Hedeon Balaban the personal title of Patriarchal Exarch (*Exarch of the Ecumenical Throne*), granting him supervisory authority over Orthodox ecclesiastical affairs in the Crown lands.
[^7]: The Greek loanword *каѳоли́цкіѧ* (*katholike*) in Early Modern Ruthenian Church Slavonic signifies the universal, catholic dimension of the Eastern Orthodox Church, in accordance with the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed, rather than Roman Catholicism.
[^8]: St. Gregory of Nazianzus (the Theologian), *Oration 2: Apology for His Flight to Pontus*, §35: "As food is for the body, so is the word for the soul" (ὅπεр γὰρ σώματι τροφή, τοῦτο ψυχῇ λόγος).
[^9]: Meletios I Pegas (1549–1601), Pope and Patriarch of Alexandria (1590–1601). A prominent Greek theologian and humanist educated in Venice and Padua, he maintained active epistolary relations with the Ruthenian Orthodox hierarchy, Prince Konstantyn Ostrozky, and the Lviv Dormition Brotherhood, urging the defense of Orthodoxy through education and book printing.
[^10]: Following the death of Patriarch Matthew II of Constantinople in 1597, Patriarch Meletios Pegas of Alexandria was elected Locum Tenens (Guardian) of the Ecumenical Throne of Constantinople, administering the patriarchal see from 1597 until late 1598.
[^11]: Fedir (Theodore) Yuriiovych Balaban (d. 1606), nephew of Bishop Hedeon Balaban, elder or Starosta of Vinnytsia. Together with his uncle, he financed and established the Striatyn printing press on his Striatyn estate, importing Greek and Cyrillic typographic fonts and paper.
[^12]: The printing of the 1604 Liturgikon was undertaken to provide an authoritative, uncorrupted liturgical text for Orthodox clergy in the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, counteracting textual variations and Western Romanized influences following the 1596 Union of Brest.
[^13]: Church Slavonic *зводъ* (*zvod*) refers to a collation or recension of manuscript exemplars. The Striatyn editors, led by Pamvo Berynda, collected ancient South Slavic, East Slavic, and Venetian printed Greek Liturgika to establish their liturgical text.
[^14]: Bishop Hedeon Balaban and Pamvo Berynda intended the Striatyn press to print not only liturgical texts but also grammatical, patristic, and educational treatises in Church Slavonic, Greek, and Latin. Following the Liturgikon (1604) and Trebnyk (1606), the press also prepared materials that later culminated in Berynda's famous *Lexicon Slavenorossica* (Kyiv, 1627).
[^15]: The *Instruction to Priests* (*Поученіе к іереомъ*) attributed to St. Basil the Great was a standard prefatory text in East Slavic and South Slavic Liturgika from the 14th century onward (found in manuscript Euchologia and early imprints including the 1519 Bozhidar Vuković Liturgikon and 1583 Vilnius Liturgikon), expounding pastoral vigilance, worthiness before the altar, and reverent handling of the Holy Mysteries.
[^16]: An adaptation of 2 Timothy 2:15: "Study to show thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth."
[^17]: Liturgical rubrics and patristic canons strictly require clergy to be reconciled with all persons before entering the sanctuary to celebrate the Divine Liturgy, in accordance with Matthew 5:23–24.
[^18]: Slavonic *съпрѧтоу́еши* (from *съпрѧтати*), meaning to consume the remaining Holy Mysteries and perform the ablutions of the sacred vessels after the Liturgy.
[^19]: Matthew 7:6: "Give not that which is holy unto the dogs, neither cast ye your pearls before swine."
[^20]: Alluding to the warning of Pontius Pilate in Matthew 27:24 ("See ye to it"), here invoked patristically to place the absolute burden of pastoral responsibility upon the administrating priest when taking Holy Communion to the sick in their homes.
[^21]: 1 Timothy 4:16: "Take heed unto thyself, and unto the doctrine; continue in them: for in doing this thou shalt both save thyself, and them that hear thee."
[^22]: This rubric details the traditional Byzantine rite for preparing and consecrating the Reserved Holy Sacrament (*Artos* / Holy Lamb for the sick and Viaticum) during the Vesperal Divine Liturgy of St. Basil the Great on Great and Holy Thursday.
[^23]: The rubric instructs the priest to intinct the second consecrated Lamb with the Precious Blood of Christ using the liturgical spoon (*spoonfuls applied to the soft crumb of the Lamb*) before carefully drying it for year-round reservation in the Tabernacle (*Artophorion* / *Darokhranitelnytsia*).
[^24]: The procedure for gently desiccating the intincted Eucharistic particles on Bright Tuesday using indirect, mild heat over an earthenware brazier (*скоудельница*) was standard in medieval and early modern Slavic Liturgika, preventing the damp particles from decaying or scorching.
[^25]: A distinctive pre-Nikonian and Southwestern Ruthenian rubrical provision: dividing the reserved Lamb into four designated portions and consuming them across the four principal annual fasting periods or great feasts of the liturgical year.
[^26]: The Feast of the Holy Preeminent Apostles Peter and Paul (June 29 / July 12), concluding the Apostles' Fast.
[^27]: The four great liturgical markers: Sts. Peter and Paul (end of Apostles' Fast), the Dormition of the Theotokos (August 15, concluding the Dormition Fast), the Nativity of Christ (December 25, concluding the Nativity Fast / Pylypivka), and Great and Holy Pascha, when the remaining reserved Lamb of the previous year is consumed before the new Lamb of Holy Thursday is consecrated.
[^28]: Cyrillic abbreviation *І҃НЦІ҃* representing the Titulus Crucis: *І҆исоу́съ Назарѧни́нъ Ца́рь І҆уде́йскїй* ("Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews"; Greek: ΙΝΒΙ; Latin: INRI).
[^29]: This opening rubric initiates the Diataxis (*Уставъ службы*)—the comprehensive order for the Proskomedia (Liturgy of Preparation), the vesting of priest and deacon, and the Divine Liturgy of St. John Chrysostom.
[^30]: The foundational ethical and spiritual qualification for celebrating the Eucharist, reflecting Matthew 5:23–24 and the ancient liturgical injunction that unfeigned peace with all people is the indispensable prerequisite for offering the holy oblation.
