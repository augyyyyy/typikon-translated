[^568]: The Second Canticle of Moses (Deut. 32:1–43) forms the scriptural basis for Ode 2 of the Great Canon. Unlike the other biblical canticles sung throughout the liturgical year, the Second Ode is sung exclusively during the weekdays of Great Lent because of its stern rebuke, grave penitential character, and reminder of the soul's apostasy from God.
[^569]: The patristic antithesis of the two vestments: the soul having stripped off the original garment of incorruption woven by the Creator before the Fall (Gen. 3:21), and putting on the torn tunic of shame counselled by the serpent (Gen. 3:7).
[^570]: Colometric citation of Psalm 128:3 LXX (*Ἐπὶ τὸν νῶτόν μου ἐτέκταινον οἱ ἁμαρτωλοί, ἐμάκρυναν τὴν ἀνομίαν αὐτῶν* / *Дѣлаша на хребтѣ моемъ вси началницы злыхъ*), applied here by St. Andrew of Crete to the relentless scourging of demonic passions and habitual transgression.
[^571]: The Heirmos of Ode 3 (Tone 6) is derived from the Canticle of Hannah (1 Kings / 1 Sam. 2:2 LXX: "There is none holy as the Lord, and none righteous as our God; establish Thy Church upon the rock of Thy commandments").
[^572]: The flight of Lot from the burning of Sodom unto the mountain and his refuge in the small city of Zoar (Slavonic *Сигоръ* / Gen. 19:15–22), interpreted in Byzantine ascetical literature as the soul's urgent flight from carnal conflagration into the haven of prayer.
[^573]: The Heirmos of Ode 4 (Tone 6) is drawn from the prophetic vision and Canticle of Habakkuk (Hab. 3:2 LXX: "O Lord, I have heard Thy report, and was afraid; O Lord, I considered Thy works, and was astounded").
[^574]: Jacob's ladder (Gen. 28:12) allegorized by St. Andrew of Crete as the ascetical ascent of active virtue (*дѣянїе* / *praxis*) leading unto spiritual contemplation and theological knowledge (*разумъ въ видѣнїи* / *theoria*), reflecting the ascetical theology of Evagrius Ponticus and St. Maximus the Confessor.
[^575]: Jacob's two wives (Gen. 29:16–30) interpreted allegorically: Leah representing active ascetical labor (*дѣянїе*), fruitful in virtues, and Rachel representing spiritual contemplation (*видѣнїе* / *theoria*), attained only through long and patient striving.
[^576]: The Heirmos of Ode 5 (Tone 6) is derived from the Canticle of the Prophet Isaiah (Isa. 26:9 LXX: "From night my spirit awaketh early unto Thee, O God, for Thy commandments are a light upon the earth").
[^577]: The transgression of Reuben defiling the bed of his father Jacob (Gen. 35:22; 49:4), typologically contrasted with the moral purity of Joseph who fled the solicitation of Potiphar's wife in Egypt (Gen. 39:7–12).
[^578]: Joseph cast into the cistern or pit by his brethren (Gen. 37:24) as an Old Testament archetype of Christ's descent into Hades and His three-day Burial and Resurrection.
[^579]: The Heirmos of Ode 6 (Tone 6) is drawn from the Canticle of the Prophet Jonah crying out from the belly of the sea monster (Jonah 2:3–7 LXX: "Out of the belly of Hades I cried... and Thou broughtest up my life from corruption, O Lord my God").
[^580]: The rebellion and destruction of Dathan and Abiram, swallowed alive into the pit of the earth (Num. 16:31–33), cited as a terrifying warning against spiritual rebellion and hardhearted impenitence.
[^581]: The sign given unto Moses upon Mount Horeb, whose hand became leprous as snow when drawn from his bosom and was restored whole upon being returned (Exod. 4:6–7), demonstrating God's sovereign mercy in cleansing a sin-diseased soul.
[^582]: The penitential Sessional Hymn (*Сѣдаленъ покаяненъ*) chanted after Ode 6, Tone 6, modeled on the Greek automelon *Ἡ ἐλπὶς τοῦ κόσμου* / Slavonic *Упованіе*, invoking the miraculous rescue of the sinking Apostle Peter upon the Sea of Galilee (Matt. 14:30–31).
[^583]: The Heirmos of Ode 7 (Tone 6) is drawn from the Prayer of Azariah in the Babylonian furnace (Dan. 3:28–34 LXX: "We have sinned, we have transgressed, we have done unrighteously... yet deliver us not up utterly for Thy name's sake").
[^584]: The biblical exempla of Saul who sought his father's asses and received an earthly kingdom (1 Kings / 1 Sam. 9:1–27), and King David who fell into double transgression through adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah, yet offered two-fold lamentation in Psalm 50 LXX (2 Kings / 2 Sam. 11–12).
[^585]: David composing Psalm 50 LXX as an enduring iconographic self-rebuke (*списавъ якоже на иконѣ пѣснь*) to expose his transgression before all generations.
[^586]: The Heirmos of Ode 8 (Tone 6) is derived from the Canticle of the Three Holy Children in the furnace (Dan. 3:57–88 LXX: "Him Whom the heavenly hosts glorify... hymn, bless, and exalt supremely unto all ages").
[^587]: The fiery ascent of the Prophet Elijah into heaven in a chariot of fire (4 Kings / 2 Kings 2:11), and the double portion of the spirit bestowed upon Elisha through Elijah's sheepskin mantle (melote) dividing the waters of the Jordan (4 Kings / 2 Kings 2:8–14).
[^588]: The hospitality of the Shunammite woman who built an upper room to receive the Prophet Elisha (4 Kings / 2 Kings 4:8–10), contrasted with the inhospitable soul facing exclusion from the heavenly bridal chamber (Matt. 25:10–12).
[^589]: Gehazi, servant of Elisha, smitten with Naaman's leprosy unto all generations because of deceitful covetousness and lust for silver (4 Kings / 2 Kings 5:20–27).
[^590]: The Messianic name Emmanuel ("God with us"), foretold by the Prophet Isaiah (Isa. 7:14) and fulfilled in the virginal conception of Christ (Matt. 1:23).
[^591]: The classical patristic image of the Incarnation as the weaving of Christ's human flesh within the Virgin's womb like an imperial purple mantle (*багряница*) dyed with spiritual crimson (*червленица*), popularized in the *Protoevangelium of James* and the homilies of St. Proclus of Constantinople.
[^592]: The pedagogical structure of the Great Canon, moving methodically through the Old Testament historical books from the Creation of the world (*миробытїе* / Genesis) to the Prophets as an ethical mirror for the soul.
[^593]: The transition in Ode 9 from Old Testament typologies to New Testament exemplars of salvation: Christ welcoming the penitent thief upon the cross (Luke 23:40–43), the harlot anointing His feet with tears (Luke 7:37–50), and the publican justified through humility (Luke 18:13–14).
[^594]: The forty-day fast of Christ in the Judean wilderness (Matt. 4:1–11; Luke 4:1–13) establishing the spiritual archetype and ascetical weapons of fasting and prayer to overcome demonic assault.
[^595]: The daily concluding supplicatory troparion addressed directly to the author of the Great Canon, St. Andrew of Crete, Archbishop of Gortyna (c. 660–740).
[^596]: Sabbaite Typikon rubric for concluding the Great Canon at Great Compline: both choirs unite to chant the Heirmos of Ode 9 (*Безсѣменна зачатїя*) with a full prostration to the ground, resuming the ordinary course of Great Compline from Psalm 4 (*Внегда воззвахъ*) in the Horologion (*Часословъ*), concluding with the Canon to the Theotokos and the colometric chanting of *Lord of hosts, be with us* (*Господи силъ съ нами буди*).
[^597]: The foundational rubric of the 1640 Kyiv Triodion establishing the complete liturgical structure of Clean Monday as an unchanging model, demonstration, and spiritual rule (*образъ и Показанїе и Правило Душевное*) for all subsequent weekdays of the Holy Forty Days.
[^598]: Sessional Hymn at Matins on Tuesday of the First Week, Tone 2, modeled on the Podoben *Most blessed* (*Преблагословенна еси*), commemorating Moses on Mount Sinai receiving the Law (Exod. 24:18; 34:28) and the Three Youths in Babylon triumphing over the furnace (Dan. 1:12–15; 3:19–27).
[^599]: Second Sessional Hymn at Matins, Tone 2, modeled on the Podoben *The doors of compassion* (*Милосердїя двери*), summoning the soul to bring forth the fruits of abstinence and conversion.
