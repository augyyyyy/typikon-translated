[^881]: Saint Joannicius the Great (c. 762–846) of Mount Olympus in Bithynia, Saint Arsakios, and Saint Isaiah the Recluse of Nicomedia: 9th-century monastic luminaries and confessors who provided charismatic and prophetic guidance during the final overthrow of the Iconoclast heresy and the restoration of holy icons under Empress Theodora and Patriarch Methodius I in 843.
[^882]: Early Modern Ruthenian (*проста мова*) theological vernacular formulating the dogmatic distinction between *latreia* (adoration and divine service due to God alone) and *proskynesis* (honorary, relative veneration rendered unto holy icons), defined by the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787).
[^883]: Historical gathering of prominent iconodule confessors in Hagia Sophia during the first week of Great Lent, 843: Saint Naukratios (c. 785–848), disciple and successor of Saint Theodore the Studite as abbot of the Studion; Saints Theophanes and Theodore the Branded (*Graptoi*), so called because twelve iambic verses were branded with hot irons upon their foreheads by Emperor Theophilus; Saint Michael the Syncellus of Jerusalem (c. 761–846); and Saint Euangelos (Euggelos).
[^884]: The Column of the Cross (*столпъ Крестный*), monumental landmark in the Forum of Constantine (*Forum Constantini*) in Constantinople, where Empress Theodora's visionary journey begins at dawn on Friday of the first week of Lent.
[^885]: The Bronze Gate (Chalke Gate, Greek *Χαλκῆ Πύλη*, Ruthenian *Мѣдяная брама*), monumental bronze vestibule of the Great Palace of Constantinople, historic site where Emperor Leo III the Isaurian had first ordered the destruction of the great icon of Christ the Savior (*Christos Chalkites*) in 726; here transfigured in Empress Theodora's vision as the tribunal of divine mercy where Christ pardons Emperor Theophilus.
[^886]: The pious tradition of Patriarch Methodius I writing the names of all the iconoclast emperors upon a new parchment roll (*ха́ртꙋ но́вꙋю*) and placing it beneath the Holy Table in Hagia Sophia, praying for the soul of Emperor Theophilus, whereupon an angel appeared announcing his forgiveness and Theophilus's name was found miraculously expunged from the scroll.
[^887]: The historic first Triumph of Orthodoxy procession (*Litiya*) on the First Sunday of Great Lent, March 11, 843, wherein Empress Theodora, young Emperor Michael III, Patriarch Methodius, the holy hierarchs, monastics, and people marched through the streets of Constantinople with the True Cross, holy icons, and Gospels chanting *Kyrie eleison*.
[^888]: The conciliar synod held in Constantinople in February–March 843, confirming the Seventh Ecumenical Council (787), anathemas against iconoclasts, and decreeing an annual liturgical celebration of the Triumph of Orthodoxy on the First Sunday of Great Lent.
[^889]: First Matins Canon for the Sunday of Orthodoxy, Tone 4, Ode 7: Automelic heirmos celebrating the three Persian youths descended from Abraham who were bedewed in the fiery furnace (Dan. 3:49–50).
[^890]: Ode 8, automelic heirmos *Stretching out his hands* (Dan. 6:22): celebrating the ancestral tradition and dogmas of the Church in depicting, honoring, and kissing the holy icons of Christ and the Saints.
[^891]: Foundational patristic canon of iconology citing Saint Basil the Great (*On the Holy Spirit*, XVIII, 45; PG 32:149C): "The honor rendered unto the icon passes over unto the Prototype," dogmatically affirmed at Nicaea II (787).
[^892]: Ode 9, automelic heirmos *A stone cut without hands from the unquarried mountain* (Dan. 2:34–35, 45): Christological and Marian typology of Christ the chief cornerstone born of the Virgin without human seed, uniting divine and human natures into one divine Person.
[^893]: Exapostilarion (Photogogikon) for the Sunday of Orthodoxy, Melodic model *Tone 2, Podoben: "With the disciples let us go up"* (Greek: *Τοῖς Μαθηταῖς συνέλθωμεν*, Church Slavonic: *Съ Оу҆чени́ки вни́демъ*), celebrating the cessation of iconoclast discord and the divinely ordered unity of the Church.
[^894]: Praises at Matins, Melodic model *Tone 4, Podoben: "Thou hast given a sign"* (Greek: *Ἔδωκας σημείωσιν*, Church Slavonic: *Да́лъ є҆сѝ знаме́нїе*): affirming against the docetic heresy of Manes (Manichaeism) that the Son of God was made man not in phantasm or semblance, but in genuine and visible human flesh.
[^895]: Idiomelon sticheron at the Praises, Tone 6: joining the Lenten fast to the heroic Old Testament ascetics Moses, Elijah, and the Three Youths of Babylon, petitioning Christ for worthy attainment of the Holy Resurrection.
[^896]: Pericopes for the Divine Liturgy on the First Sunday of Great Lent: Epistle to the Hebrews 11:24–26, 32–40 (the roll call of the Old Testament heroes and martyrs of faith); and Gospel according to Saint John 1:43–51 (the calling of Philip and Nathanael, Nathanael's confession, and Christ's prophecy of heaven opened and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of Man).
[^897]: Monastic Typikon dietary rule for the First Sunday of Great Lent: permitting hot cooked food with olive oil (*варе́нїе съ є҆ле́емъ*) and two cups of wine at the main meal and at supper, while strictly prohibiting the consumption of fish during the Holy Forty Days except on the Annunciation and Palm Sunday.
[^898]: Ancient liturgical office of the Synodikon of Orthodoxy (*Послѣдованїе в недѣлю Православїя на прокля́тїе є҆ретикѡ́въ*): an extraordinary service celebrated after Matins, wherein clergy and faithful processed outside to an open square or conciliar assembly to chant the anathemas against heretics and the Canon of Saint Theodore the Studite.
[^899]: Canon for the Synodikon of Orthodoxy, Tone 6, composed by Saint Theodore the Studite (759–826), abbot of the Stoudios Monastery in Constantinople and principal theologian of the second iconoclast period.
[^900]: Anathemas against the second iconoclast triumvirate under Leo V and Theophilus: "Lyzix" (Greek: *Λυζῖκος* / *Lezix*, associated with the circle of John the Grammarian); Antony I Kassymatas, iconoclast Patriarch of Constantinople (821–837); John VII the Grammarian (Morocharzanios), iconoclast Patriarch (837–843); and Theodore Melissenos.
[^901]: Typological comparison from 2 Timothy 3:8 (cf. Ex. 7:11): likening the iconoclast leaders John the Grammarian and Antony Kassymatas to Jannes and Jambres, the Egyptian sorcerers who withstood Moses before Pharaoh.
[^902]: Explicit liturgical anathema in Ode 3 against the chief figures of the second iconoclasm: Lyzikos; Patriarch John VII the Grammarian; Patriarch Antony I Kassymatas; Theodore Melissenos; and Patriarch Theodotus I Kassiteras (815–821).
[^903]: Patristic jubilation at the cessation of the second iconoclasm: the reopening of desert hermitages and monasteries, the return of exiled monks from the wilderness, and the public restoration of the veneration of the relics and icons of the holy martyrs.
[^904]: Scriptural trope from Deuteronomy 32:32–33 ("For their vine is of the vine of Sodom... their wine is the venom of serpents, and the cruel poison of asps"), typologically applied to the soul-corrupting doctrine of the iconoclasts.
[^905]: Denunciation of uncanonical, simoniacal iconoclast ordinations, condemning Patriarch John VII the Grammarian and Antony Kassymatas as spiritual successors to Simon Magus (Acts 8:18–24).
[^906]: Dogmatic confession of Ode 5: distinguishing Christian relative veneration of icons of Christ, the Theotokos, and the Saints from idolatrous worship of deaf and mute idols (*глꙋхы́мъ і҆́долѡмъ*), grounded in the historical reality of the divine Incarnation.
