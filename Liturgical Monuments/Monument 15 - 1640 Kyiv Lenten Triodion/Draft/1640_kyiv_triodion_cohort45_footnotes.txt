[^1230]: St. Gregory Palamas and the Hesychasts were vindicated against Barlaam of Calabria at the Council of Constantinople held in June 1341 under the presidency of Emperor Andronicus III Palaeologus (1328–1341), whose imperial edict confirmed the synodical decree.
[^1231]: Barlaam of Calabria asserted that the divine light of Mount Tabor was a sensible, created symbol (ktiston) that passed away, whereas St. Gregory Palamas demonstrated from the Fathers that it is the eternal, uncreated light and energy (aktiston) of the Holy Trinity.
[^1232]: St. Gregory Palamas formulated the definitive patristic distinction between the totally unknowable, unapproachable divine essence (ousia) and the eternal, uncreated, communicable divine energies (energeiai), by which mankind is deified (theosis).
[^1233]: The synaxarion verses for the Second Sunday of Great Lent, celebrating St. Gregory Palamas as the theologian of the divine and uncreated Light.
[^1234]: St. Gregory Palamas was born in Constantinople in 1296 of noble lineage; his father Constantine was an imperial senator and tutor to the future Emperor Andronicus III Palaeologus.
[^1235]: Mount Athos (the Holy Mountain), where St. Gregory entered monastic life around 1316 at approximately twenty years of age, accompanied by his brothers Macarius and Theodosius.
[^1236]: St. Nicodemus of Vatopedi (commemorated July 11), a holy hesychast elder on Mount Athos who guided St. Gregory in ascetic vigilance, fasting, and unceasing prayer of the heart.
[^1237]: The Great Lavra of St. Athanasius on Mount Athos, where St. Gregory spent three years as a monk, later withdrawing into the solitary skete of Glossia for deeper hesychastic contemplation.
[^1238]: In 1326, frequent Turkish pirate raids forced St. Gregory and his fellow monks to leave Mount Athos for Thessalonica, where he was ordained a priest at age thirty, after which he retreated to a hermitage near Veroia (Berrhoea).
[^1239]: The Hesychast Controversy extended from approximately 1337 (Barlaam's initial attacks on the monks) until the definitive Council of Blachernae and Tomos of 1351, spanning over two decades of fierce theological conflict.
[^1240]: Barlaam of Seminara (c. 1290–1348), a Greek-Calabrian monk and philosopher whose nominalist Aristotelianism led him to deny the possibility of direct experiential communion with God and to mock the hesychasts' prayer of the heart.
[^1241]: Barlaam and his partisans accused the hesychasts of polytheism and ditheism for distinguishing between the essence and uncreated energies of God, a charge the Orthodox fathers refuted by comparing it to Sabellianism and Arianism.
[^1242]: In the 1640 Kyivan text, Emperor Andronicus III Palaeologus (reigned 1328–1341) is designated as 'the fourth' (четве́ртый), counting all imperial sovereigns of that name across the Byzantine imperial registers.
[^1243]: The Council of Constantinople convened in the Church of the Holy Wisdom (Hagia Sophia) on June 10, 1341, where Barlaam was compelled to recant his writings and teachings against the hesychasts.
[^1244]: Following his condemnation at the Council of 1341, Barlaam left Constantinople for Italy, converted to the Roman Catholic faith, and was appointed Bishop of Gerace in Calabria by Pope Clement VI in 1342.
[^1245]: Gregory Akindynos (c. 1300–1348), originally a disciple of Palamas who later adopted Barlaam's anti-hesychast positions. The text plays on the Greek etymology: Akindynos ('without danger') versus Polykindynos ('full of many dangers').
[^1246]: St. Gregory Palamas was consecrated Archbishop of Thessalonica in 1347 by Patriarch Isidore I, although civil strife caused by the Zealot uprising prevented him from entering the city until 1350.
[^1247]: The three Byzantine Emperors were Andronicus III Palaeologus, John V Palaeologus, and John VI Kantakouzenos; the successive Patriarchs during the controversy included John XIV Kalekas, Isidore I, and Callistus I.
[^1248]: In 1354, while journeying by ship from Thessalonica to Constantinople to mediate peace between John V Palaeologus and John VI Kantakouzenos, St. Gregory was captured by Ottoman Turks near Gallipoli and held hostage for a year.
[^1249]: During his Ottoman captivity (1354–1355), St. Gregory engaged in famous theological disputations with Muslims and Christian renegades, defending the Incarnation, the veneration of the Cross, and holy icons, as recorded in his pastoral epistle to his flock.
[^1250]: St. Gregory Palamas served as Archbishop of Thessalonica for twelve to thirteen years (1347–1359) until his holy repose on November 14, 1359, at the age of sixty-three.
[^1251]: A reference to the comprehensive Life and Encomium of St. Gregory Palamas written by Patriarch Philotheus Kokkinos, which documents numerous post-mortem miracles and healings performed at his tomb in Thessalonica.
[^1252]: The hymnographic text articulates the core Palamite doctrine that the divine grace (charis) and divine energy (energeia) are uncreated (aktistos), flowing eternally from the divine essence and making man a partaker of the divine nature (2 Pet. 1:4).
