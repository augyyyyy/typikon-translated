[^1864]: Sticheron 21 [Greek Phi]: In the Byzantine liturgical tradition, these 24 penitential stichera by St. Andrew of Crete (sung at *Lord, I have cried* on Wednesday evening of the Fifth Week of Great Lent) form a continuous alphabetic acrostic following the 24 letters of the classical Greek alphabet from Alpha (Α) to Omega (Ω). The present sticheron corresponds to the twenty-first letter, Phi (Φ), based on the Greek opening *Φθόνῳ κατέβалεν* (*Phthonō katebalen*, "By envy cast down..."), faithfully reflected in the Church Slavonic incipit *За́вистію низложи́*.
[^1865]: Sticheron 23 [Greek Psi]: The twenty-third sticheron corresponds to the Greek letter Psi (Ψ), opening in the Greek archetype with *Ψυχὴν πρόσδεξαι* (*Psychēn prosdexai*, "Receive my soul..."). The 1640 Kyiv Triodion renders the verbal opening *По́има пріими́ мѧ*, preserving the prayer of whole-person dedication (*"soul and body entirely I place into Thy hands"*).
[^1866]: Rubric for the Theotokion: The rubric specifies chanting in a higher musical pitch (*высшимъ гласомъ*) by both choirs united in the center (*ѡ҆́ба ли́ка в̾кꙋ́пѣ*), celebrating the completion of the 24 alphabetic stichera of the Great Canon at Vespers.
[^1867]: Rubric: If the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts is not celebrated on Wednesday evening (e.g., if superseded or omitted in non-parochial monastic practice), this rubric establishes the plain Vespers order, directing that the 3 Podobni and the 24 Stichera of the Great Canon be sung at *Lord, I have cried*, followed by the idiomelon stichera of the day at the Aposticha.
[^1868]: Prokeimenon, Psalm 93:1-2 LXX: The Slavonic phrase *и҆ не ѡ҆би́ноуласѧ е҆́стъ* directly translates the Septuagint Greek *παρρησιάσατο* (*parrēsiasato*, "He acted boldly / spoke openly", from *παρρησία*), contrasting with the later Synodal text (*явися*).
[^1869]: Genesis 17:1-9: The prescribed Old Testament reading for Wednesday of the Fifth Week at Vespers, recounting God's covenant with Abram, the solemn promise of progeny, and the renaming of Abram to Abraham (*"father of a multitude of nations"*).
[^1870]: Prokeimenon, Psalm 95:1 LXX: The evening prokeimenon concluding the Genesis reading and preceding the second paremia from Proverbs.
[^1871]: Proverbs 15:20b - 16:2 LXX: The second Old Testament paremia follows the Septuagint versification, which exhibits distinct moral and sapiential maxims and structural variations from the Masoretic Hebrew text.
[^1872]: Proverbs 16:3-10 LXX: The continuation of the paremia on fol. 661. In the Septuagint, verses 3-10 emphasize the contrast between the ways of the humble and the destruction reserved for the haughty.
[^1873]: Fasting Rubric at the Trapeza: Monastic typika for Wednesday of the Fifth Week prescribe strict fasting without oil (*на масло не разрѣшаемъ*) and without wine (*вина не пиваемъ*), because of the bodily labor required for the all-night vigil of the Great Canon (*труда ради бдѣннаго, хотящаго быти*).
[^1874]: Small Compline in Cells: Small Compline is read in individual monastic cells without prostrations, incorporating the 24 stichera of St. Andrew's Canon after the Trisagion prayers, while the Menaion service is deferred to Compline.
[^1875]: Typikon Transfer Rubric: If a feast of the Menaion having a vigil (such as a major saint) coincides with Thursday of the Great Canon, its service is transferred in advance to Tuesday of the same fifth week, safeguarding the integral chanting of the Great Canon without curtailment.
[^1876]: Annunciation Transfer Rubric: In the rare liturgical occurrence of the Feast of the Annunciation (March 25) falling on Thursday of the Great Canon, the Great Canon is transferred backward to Tuesday of the fifth week, accompanied by Tuesday's triodion, while the triodion proper to the Great Canon is combined on Thursday with the Annunciation office.
[^1877]: Matins Rubric: The Matins of the Great Canon begins exceptionally early in the night ("in the fourth hour of the night", corresponding to approximately 10:00 PM or 11:00 PM). The Psalter reading is restricted to Kathisma 8 (Psalms 55-63 LXX), followed by the first section of the Life of St. Mary of Egypt by St. Sophronius of Jerusalem, read in the church prior to Psalm 50.
[^1878]: Great Canon Structure and Metanias: The rubric enumerates 250 troparia in the complete Great Canon (*Канѡнъ, .с҃: съ .н҃.*) and 750 prostrations (*а пок: .ѱ҃н.*), calculated at precisely 3 full metanias or prostrations per troparion, alternating between the right and left choirs to maintain liturgical solemnity and order.
[^1879]: Ode 1 Heirmos: Composed by St. Andrew of Crete in Tone 6, drawing its biblical theme and incipit from the first Canticle of Moses at the Red Sea (Exodus 15:1-2: *"He was a helper and protector unto me for salvation; He is my God, and I will glorify Him"*).
[^1880]: Ode 1, Troparia 4-5: St. Andrew develops the ascetic distinction between the sensory Eve of Genesis (*Еѵ́ва чꙋ́вственнаѧ*) and the inward noetic Eve (*мы́сленаѧ Еѵ́а*)—the impassioned thought in the flesh that entices the soul to illicit spiritual consumption.
[^1881]: Ode 1, Troparion 10: Evoking the creation of man from the dust of the earth (Gen. 2:7), the hymnographer addresses Christ simultaneously as the Potter/Fashioner (*зда́тель*), Creator (*Тво́рецъ*), Deliverer (*И҆зба́витель*), and Judge (*Сꙋдїѧ*).
[^1882]: Ode 1, Troparia 14-15: The Parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30-37) is allegorically interiorized: the traveler stripped and wounded by robbers is the soul overcome by sinful thoughts; the Priest and Levite signify the Old Testament Law unable to effect healing; and Christ, born of the Virgin Mary, is the compassionate Samaritan Who binds up the soul's wounds.
[^1883]: Ode 1, Troparion 20 Catchword: At the foot of fol. 664v (p. 677), the printed catchword is *Бога́-*, which corresponds to the opening of the traditional troparion of the Prodigal Son in Ode 1 (*"Богатство мое, Спасе, изнурив в блуде..."*).
[^1884]: Fol. 665r Compositor Omission: A significant codicological discrepancy occurs between fol. 664v (p. 677) and fol. 665r (p. 678). While the catchword at the foot of fol. 664v signals the troparion *Бога́-тство*, fol. 665r opens abruptly mid-phrase with *...бє́зчестныѧ, и҆ цр҃твїѧ нн҃ѣ Хв҃а ѡ҆́бщники...*, which constitutes the second half of the troparion to St. Andrew of Crete (*"Молитвами твоими нас, Андрее, избави страстей безчестных, и Царствия ныне Христова общники..."*). The 1640 Kyiv printer evidently omitted the intervening troparion of the Prodigal Son and the troparion to St. Mary of Egypt (*"Ты ми даждь светозарную благодать..."*) during quire imposition.
[^1885]: Ode 2 Heirmos 1: The First Heirmos of Ode 2 is based on the Second Biblical Canticle of Moses in Deuteronomy 32:1 (*"Give ear, O heaven, and I will speak; and let the earth hear the words of my mouth"*).
[^1886]: Ode 2, Troparion 10: Direct scriptural citation of Psalm 128:3 LXX: *"The plowers plowed upon my back; they made long their furrows"* (Slavonic: *На хребтѣ̀ мое́мъ дѣ́лаша грѣ́шницы, продолжи́ша беззако́нїе своѐ*), applied here to demonic and passionate impulses furrowing the soul.
[^1887]: Ode 2, Troparia 12-14: The coats of skin (*ко́жныѧ ри́зы*) from Genesis 3:21 are interpreted patristically as the mortal, corruptible, and passible condition of fallen human flesh, contrasting with the radiant "God-woven garment" (*Бг҃отка́ннаѧ ѡ҆де́жда*) of original innocence.
[^1888]: Ode 2, Troparion 24: Allusion to the Publican's prayer in Luke 18:13 (*"God be merciful to me a sinner"*), held up by St. Andrew as the quintessential archetype of penitent prayer.
[^1889]: Ode 2 Heirmos 2: Unlike other odes which possess a single heirmos, Ode 2 features two distinct heirmoi by St. Andrew of Crete. The second heirmos (*Ви́дите, ви́дите...*) draws from Deuteronomy 32:39 (*"See now that I, even I, am He, and there is no god beside Me"*).
[^1890]: Ode 2, Troparion 3 (to Second Heirmos): St. Andrew surveys the righteous figures of the antediluvian era—Seth (Gen. 4:26), Enos (Gen. 4:26), Enoch who walked with God and was translated (Gen. 5:24; Heb. 11:5), and Noah the preacher of righteousness (Gen. 6:9)—contrasting their piety with the soul's negligence.
