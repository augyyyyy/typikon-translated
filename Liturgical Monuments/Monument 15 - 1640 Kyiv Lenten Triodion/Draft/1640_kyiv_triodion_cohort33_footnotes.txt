[^853]: Melodic model *Tone 6, Podoben: "Having laid aside all earthly hope"* (Greek: *Ὅλην ἀποθέμενοι*, Church Slavonic *Всѐ въхло́жше* / *Всѐ ѿло́жше*), concluding the stichera on "Lord, I have cried" for the Prophets who refused to worship the creature rather than the Creator (cf. Rom. 1:25) and foretold the voluntary Passion of Christ.
[^854]: Foundational Christological argument of Orthodox iconology formulated by Saint John of Damascus (*Apologetic Treatises against those who Decry the Holy Images*) and Saint Theodore the Studite (*Antirrhetics*): although the Son of God is uncircumscribable in His divine nature (*Е҆стество́мъ неопи́санный*), by taking true human flesh in the Incarnation He voluntarily assumed all natural human properties, thereby rendering Himself visible and depictable in holy icons.
[^855]: Doxasticon at "Lord, I have cried" on the First Sunday of Great Lent, Tone 2: The shadows of the Old Law and the Tabernacle of Testimony (Heb. 9:1–11, 10:1) find their visible realization in the incarnate Image of Christ, wherein the icon is venerated relatively (*блг҃чтнѡ покланѧ́емый*) rather than adored as God (*а҆ не бг҃отвори́мый*).
[^856]: Stichera at the Litiya, Tone 2: The Old Testament prophets are commemorated on the Sunday of Orthodoxy as mediators of the New Covenant (Heb. 8:6, 9:15) who prophetically delineated the economy of salvation fulfilled in the Incarnation.
[^857]: Aposticha Doxasticon, Tone 2: Foundational patristic prooftext citing Saint Basil the Great, *On the Holy Spirit* (*De Spiritu Sancto*), XVIII, 45 (PG 32:149C): "The honor rendered unto the icon passes over unto the Prototype" (*Ἡ γὰρ τῆς εἰκόνος τιμὴ ἐπὶ τὸ πρωτότυπον διαβαίνει*), dogmatically confirmed by the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787).
[^858]: Mark's Chapter (*Ма́ркова глава̀*): A regulatory Typikon rubric governing the concurrence (*стече́нїе*) of the First Sunday of Great Lent with the First and Second Finding of the Honorable Head of Saint John the Baptist (February 24) or the Holy Forty Martyrs of Sebaste (March 9), directing that the festal service be combined according to the rubrics appointed for the Third Sunday of Lent (Sunday of the Veneration of the Holy Cross).
[^859]: Internal codicological cross-references in the 1640 Kyiv Triodion: The Troparion of the Holy Icon (*Пречи́стому Твоему́ О́бразу*) is appointed on printed fol. 307r (leaf p320); the Sunday Theotokion (*Всѧ паче смысла*) on printed fol. 282r (leaf p295); and the Polyeleos and Megalynarion rubrics on printed fol. 154v (Cheesefare Sunday, leaf p167).
[^860]: First Canon of the Sunday of Orthodoxy, Tone 4, composed by Saint Theophanes the Branded (*Ѳеофа́нъ Начерта́нный* / *Theophanes Graptos*, c. 778–845), Metropolitan of Nicaea, celebrated hymnographer and confessor under the iconoclast emperors.
[^861]: Acrostic of the First Canon of the Sunday of Orthodoxy by Saint Theophanes: *Дне́сь блг҃чтїѧ бл҃говѣ́щаннаѧ прїи́де свѣ́тлость* (Greek: *Σήμερον εὐσεβείης θεοφεγγέος ἦλθεν αἴγλη*).
[^862]: Pious Byzantine Empress Theodora (c. 815–867) and her young son Michael III (reigned as emperor 842–867), under whose imperial authority the holy icons were restored and the annual Feast of Orthodoxy was established in Hagia Sophia on the First Sunday of Lent, March 11, 843.
[^863]: Katavasia of Ode 1: Automelic heirmos of the Red Sea (*Мо́рѧ чермна́гѡ пꙋчи́нꙋ*), typologically linking Israel's dry-shod crossing and Moses' outstretched arms forming the sign of the Cross (Ex. 17:11–12) to the spiritual triumph of the Church over heretical delusion.
[^864]: Sessional Hymn (Sedalen), Melodic model *Tone 1, Podoben: "The angelic choir"* (Greek: *Χορὸς Ἀγγελικός*, Church Slavonic *Ли́къ А҆́гг҃лскїй*), automelon for Matins kathisma hymns.
[^865]: Scriptural and liturgical trope of the exaltation of the horn (1 Sam. 2:10, Ps. 131:17 / LXX 131:17): petition for divine strength and victory granted unto Orthodox Christian monarchs defending the true faith.
[^866]: Kontakion of the Sunday of Orthodoxy, Tone 8, Automelon (*Самогла́сенъ*): "The uncircumscribable Word of the Father" (*Неѡписа́нное сло́во Ѻ҆ц҃ее*), affirming that the divine Word, in taking circumscribed flesh from the Theotokos, restored the fallen and defiled image of God in man unto its original beauty.
[^867]: Ikos of the Sunday of Orthodoxy: The mystery of the divine economy (*смотре́нїа та́инство*, Greek *οἰκονομία*) foretold by the holy Prophets for those living at the culmination of the ages (cf. 1 Cor. 10:11, Eph. 1:10), celebrated in the one faith, one baptism, and the depiction of holy icons.
[^868]: Baroque Polish/Ruthenian-influenced syllabic couplet (*вірші*) heading the Synaxarion for the Sunday of Orthodoxy: contrasting the dishonorable expulsion of the holy icons under the iconoclasts with the joyful and honorable restoration of their liturgical veneration.
[^869]: Full-page woodcut engraving heading the Synaxarion of the Sunday of Orthodoxy (fol. 314r, physical leaf p327): The composition depicts the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787) and the Council of Constantinople (843), showing young Emperor Michael III and Empress Theodora beneath an architectural baldachin holding icons of Christ Pantocrator and the Hodegetria, flanked by holy hierarchs in omophoria, with the assembly of the Holy Fathers seated in synod in the lower register.
[^870]: Early Modern Ruthenian (*проста мова*) historical narrative of the Synaxarion for the First Sunday of Great Lent, recounting the historical chronicle of the Iconoclast controversy from Leo III the Isaurian (717) to the definitive restoration under Empress Theodora and Patriarch Methodius I (843).
[^871]: George Kedrenos (*Ке́дрї, ро: кн: г҃.*), 11th-century Byzantine chronicler (*Synopsis Historiarum*, PG 121–122): source for the rise of Leo III the Isaurian (originally named Konon), who began as a peasant and donkey-driver, and his opening dispute with Saint Germanus I, Patriarch of Constantinople (715–730), demanding that icons either be removed or elevated out of kissing reach.
[^872]: The forced abdication and exile of Saint Germanus I of Constantinople to his ancestral estate at Platanion in January 730, after refusing to sign the imperial decree against holy icons, and the unlawful elevation of the iconoclast syncellus Anastasios (730–754).
[^873]: Popular Byzantine legend recorded in the chronicles of Saint Theophanes the Confessor (*Chronographia*, AM 6215) and John Zonaras (*Epitome Historiarum*, XV, 2): Jewish sorcerers in Phoenicia/Isauria who allegedly foretold Leo's elevation to the imperial throne and extracted a vow that he would destroy images once established in power.
[^874]: Byzantine polemical designation of Emperor Constantine V (reigned 741–775): pejoratively surnamed *Copronymus* (Greek: *Κοπρώνυμος*, Ruthenian: *Гноєнме́нный*, "the Dung-named") because he allegedly defecated in the baptismal font as an infant; chronicling his violent persecution of monastics and the iconoclast Council of Hieria (754).
[^875]: Empress Irene (regent for her son Constantine VI, 780–790; sole empress 797–802) and Saint Tarasios, Patriarch of Constantinople (784–806), who convoked the Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787) to formulate the dogmatic definition on the veneration of holy icons.
[^876]: Outbreak of the Second Iconoclast Crisis under Emperor Leo V the Armenian (reigned 813–820): citing Kedrenos' narrative concerning the hermit of Philomelion, his disciple the monk Sabbatios, and Theodore Melissenos, who deceived the emperor by staging a prophetic encounter in the Monastery of the Dexiokrates.
[^877]: Reign of Emperor Michael II the Amorian (820–829), cited from John Zonaras' *Epitome Historiarum* (Book XV): recounting his imprisonment of Saint Methodius in a tomb on the island of Saint Andrew (Akritas) and the brutal martyrdom of Saint Euthymius, Metropolitan of Sardis, who was scourged with sinews until he died.
[^878]: Emperor Theophilus (reigned 829–842): celebrated in Byzantine historiography for his incorruptible administration of civic justice and daily inspection of markets, but notorious for the ruthless persecution of iconodules, including the torture and facial branding of Saints Theodore and Theophanes the Graptoi.
[^879]: Traditional account of Theophilus' mortal agony in early 842: suffering from severe dysentery (*бѣ́гꙋнка*), his mouth opened unnaturally wide so that his throat and internal organs were visible; Empress Theodora experienced a vision of the Mother of God, whereupon Theophilus repented, kissed an icon brought by an attendant, was healed of the gaping deformity, and died in peace.
[^880]: Deposition of the iconoclast Patriarch John VII the Grammarian (pejoratively surnamed *Jannes* after Pharaoh's magician, 2 Tim. 3:8; Ruthenian: *чаронача́лникъ*, "chief sorcerer"), the enthronement of Saint Methodius I the Confessor (March 843), and the prophetic revelation granted unto Saint Joannicius the Great on Mount Olympus in Bithynia confirming the restoration of the holy icons.
