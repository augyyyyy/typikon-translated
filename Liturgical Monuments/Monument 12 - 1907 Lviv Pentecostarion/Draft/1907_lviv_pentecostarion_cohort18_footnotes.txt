[^331]: Codicological and liturgical authorship of the Holy Saturday Canon: The rubric on fol. 153 explicitly identifies the composite authorship of the canon: the Heirmoi of Odes 1–5 were composed by Kassia the Nun (Kassiane, ca. 810–865), while the Troparia of Odes 1–5 were written by Mark the Monk, Bishop of Idrus (Hydrus/Otranto in Apulia, 10th c.); the Heirmoi and Troparia of Odes 6–9 were composed by St. Cosmas of Maiuma (ca. 675–752).
[^332]: Epigraphic alphabetic acrostic of the Holy Saturday Canon: In the Greek original and preserved in Cyrillic transliteration in the margins of the 1907 Lviv edition (*Каѝ Си́меронъ дѐ Са́ккатонъ ме́лпѡ ме́га*), the acrostic reads: *Καὶ σήμερον δὲ Σάββατον μέλπω μέγα* ("And today also, I sing the Great Sabbath"). Mark of Idrus fitted his troparia in Odes 1–5 to complete the acrostic begun by Kassia's heirmoi, while Cosmas of Maiuma included both heirmoi and troparia in the acrostic for Odes 6–9.
[^333]: Patristic Christology of the three-day death: The phrase "By the power of the better part, Thy soul was separated from the flesh" (*Си́лою лꙋ́чшагѡ... ѿ пло́ти дꙋша̀ твоѧ̀ раздѣли́сѧ*) reflects the classic teaching of St. John of Damascus (*Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* III.27): in Christ's death, His human soul was separated from His human body, yet His divine hypostasis remained inseparably united both to His soul in hades and to His body in the tomb.
[^334]: Scriptural and dogmatic typology of incorruption: "for Thy flesh saw not corruption, O Master" (*пло́ть бо твоѧ̀ и҆стлѣ́нїа не ви́дѣ, Влⷣко*), directly citing Psalm 15:10 (LXX: *οὐδὲ δώσεις τὸν ὅσιόν σου ἰδεῖν διαφθοράν*) and the Apostle Peter's Pentecost sermon (Acts 2:27, 31), asserting the bodily incorruption of Christ in the tomb prior to the Resurrection.
[^335]: Dogma of the indivisible Hypostatic Union: "even if Thy temple was dissolved at the time of the Passion, yet even so one was the hypostasis of Thy Divinity and Thy Flesh" (*а҆́ще бо и҆ разори́сѧ тво́й хра́мъ во вре́мѧ Стра́сти, но и҆ та́кѡ є҆ди́нъ бѣ̀ соста́въ Бж҃ества̀ и҆ Пло́ти твоеѧ̀*), affirming that the separation of soul and body on the Cross did not dissolve the one composite Hypostasis of the Word incarnate.
[^336]: The Synaxarion of Holy and Great Saturday: Composed by Nicephorus Callistus Xanthopoulos (ca. 1256–1335) for the Triodion, presenting the theological contemplation of the mystical Sabbath rest of Christ in the sepulchre, parallel to God resting on the seventh day of the primeval creation (Genesis 2:2-3).
[^337]: Patristic soteriology refuting the Satanic ransom: The Synaxarion decisively rejects the notion that Christ's Blood was paid as a ransom to the devil (*Но ни Кро́ви же взѧ́тъ вра́гъ на́шъ дїа́волъ... ка́кѡ бо а҆́ще не то́чїю ѿ Бг҃а, но и҆ Бг҃а є҆го̀ разбо́йникъ дїа́волъ ꙗ҆́ти и҆мѧ́ше;*), affirming with St. Gregory the Theologian (*Oration 45.22*) that the sacrifice was offered solely to God the Father for human redemption.
[^338]: Acrostic structure in Cosmas's odes: In Odes 7 and 8, the letters *Т-Ѡ-Н* and *М-Є-Л-П-Ѡ* are printed in the margins to continue the acrostic: *Σάββατον μέλπω* ("I sing the Sabbath"), demonstrating that Cosmas incorporated his Heirmoi into the structural sequence of the acrostic.
[^339]: The Heirmos *Lament Me not, O Mother* (*Не рыда́й менѐ, Ма́ти* / *Μὴ ἐποδύρου μου Μῆτερ*): The poetic dialogue between Christ in the tomb and the Virgin Mary, which served as the liturgical and iconographic foundation for the celebrated icon of "Extreme Humility" (*Ἄκρα Ταπείνωσις*) across the Byzantine and Slavic traditions.
[^340]: Sabbath typology of the Creation and Redemption: The Doxastikon of Praises (*Дне́шнїй де́нь та́йнѡ, вели́кїй Мѡѷсе́й проѡбразова́ше*) directly connects Genesis 2:2-3 with Christ's bodily Sabbath rest in the tomb, inaugurating the eternal Sabbath (*σαββατισμός*, Hebrews 4:9-10) of the New Covenant.
[^341]: Liturgical rubric for the Great Doxology and Procession: In the Ruthenian and Byzantine usages, during the singing of the solemn slow Trisagion of the Great Doxology, the priest in full vestments with the deacon and servers carries the Epitaphios (Holy Shroud) and the Holy Gospel in procession around the temple, representing the burial procession of Christ to the tomb.
[^342]: Maternal lament and Simeon's prophecy: The Funeral Sticheron (*Стїхи́ра надгро́бнаѧ: Прїиди́те, ᲂу҆блажи́мъ І҆ѡ́сифа приснопа́мѧтнаго*) incorporates the poignant lament of the Mother of God recalling the prophecy of Righteous Simeon (Luke 2:35), concluding with the threefold veneration: "We worship Thy Sufferings, O Christ, and Thy holy Resurrection!"
[^343]: Old Testament resurrection typology: The reading of Ezekiel 37:1-14 (the vision of the dry bones in the valley brought to life by the breath of the Spirit) stands as the preeminent prophetic witness of the universal bodily resurrection, chanted at the climax of Holy Saturday Matins.
[^344]: Composite liturgical Epistle lectionary: The Epistle reading (pericope 123) ingeniously fuses 1 Corinthians 5:6-8 ("Purge out therefore the old leaven... for our Pascha, Christ, is sacrificed for us") with Galatians 3:13-14 ("Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the Law, being made a curse for us"), directly linking the Paschal sacrifice with the redemption from the curse of the tree.
[^345]: Paschaltide Lenten Hours rubric: The First Hour is sung immediately in the narthex following Matins, while the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours are aggregated simply in the narthex with the Beatitudes (*съ бл҃же́нными*) prior to the solemn afternoon Vespers and Liturgy of St. Basil the Great.
