[^83]: The conclusion of the Sixth Hour prophecy on Great Tuesday: Ezekiel 1:21–28, recounting the vision of the four living creatures, the crystalline firmament, the sound of the wings like the voice of the Almighty (*Shaddai*), the sapphire throne, and the appearance of the likeness of the Glory of the Lord.
[^84]: The liturgical order of the Ninth Hour on Great Tuesday omits the customary kathisma psalmody (*стїхосло́вїе*). The service proceeds directly to the Troparion of the Ninth Hour and the Gospel reading, concluding with the Beatitudes (*Блаже́нни*) and prostrations before the Presanctified Divine Liturgy.
[^85]: The first Old Testament reading at Great Tuesday Presanctified Vespers: Exodus 2:5–10, recounting the deliverance of the infant Moses from the waters of the Nile by Pharaoh's daughter, prefiguring Christ's deliverance from the wrath of earthly rulers and the salvation of mankind through the waters of Baptism.
[^86]: The second Old Testament reading at Great Tuesday Presanctified Vespers: Job 1:13–22, describing the sudden and total loss of Job's possessions, servants, and children, and his blameless submission to divine providence (*"The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord"*).
[^87]: The Gospel reading at Great Tuesday Presanctified Vespers: Matthew 26:6–16 (section 120), recounting the anointing of Christ by the woman with the alabaster box of costly spikenard in the house of Simon the leper in Bethany, contrasted with Judas Iscariot's covenant with the chief priests to betray Him for thirty pieces of silver.
[^88]: Great Compline on Great Tuesday evening features a tri-ode (*трипѣ́снецъ*) composed by Saint Andrew of Crete (ca. 660–740), consisting of Odes 3, 8, and 9, meditating on spiritual vigilance, the suddenness of the Bridegroom's return, and the repentant anointing by the sinful woman.
[^89]: The solemn Sessional Hymn at Great Compline, Tone 1, Podoben: "The angelic choir" (*Ли́къ а҆́гг҃льскїй*), contemplating the lawless Sanhedrin—Annas, Caiaphas, Herod, and Pilate—gathering in hatred to examine and condemn the Long-suffering Lord of all.
[^90]: Matins of Holy and Great Wednesday centers on two diametrically opposed figures: the repentant harlot who poured out costly myrrh upon the Lord's feet to obtain the remission of her sins, and Judas Iscariot who, blinded by avarice, sold his Master to the chief priests for thirty pieces of silver.
[^91]: Codicological cross-reference to printed folio 37 (Physical Leaf p42 = Printed Folio 37 + 5), where the solemn Troparion of the Bridegroom (*"Behold, the Bridegroom cometh at midnight..."* / *Се́ Жени́хъ гряде́тъ*) is given in full at the opening of Passion Week on Great Monday.
[^92]: The Matins Gospel of Holy and Great Wednesday: John 12:17–50 (sections 41 to 43), recounting the testimony of the crowds regarding the raising of Lazarus, the Greeks seeking Jesus, the Father's voice from heaven, Christ's prophecy of being lifted up upon the Cross, and His solemn warning to walk while they have the Light.
[^93]: The Matins Tri-ode of Holy and Great Wednesday, composed by Saint Cosmas the Melodist, Bishop of Maiuma (ca. 675–752), consisting of Odes 3, 8, and 9, bearing the Greek acrostic *Τετράδι ψάλλω* ("On Wednesday I sing" / *Тетра́ди ѱалῶ*).
[^94]: The Kontakion of Holy and Great Wednesday, Tone 4, Podoben: "Thou Who wast lifted up upon the Cross" (*Вознесы́йсѧ*), voicing the soul's profound sorrow for having exceeded the harlot in sinfulness without offering tears, yet begging for forgiveness through silent supplication.
[^95]: The Synaxarion of Holy and Great Wednesday, composed by Nikephoros Kallistos Xanthopoulos (ca. 1256–1335): An extensive patristic treatise harmonizing the differing Gospel accounts of the women who anointed the Lord with myrrh.
[^96]: Patristic harmonization of Saint John Chrysostom (Homily 80 on Matthew): Chrysostom teaches that there were two distinct women: the repentant sinful woman of Matthew, Mark, and Luke, and Mary the sister of Lazarus in John. Nikephoros Kallistos also discusses the opinion of other patristic commentators that three distinct anointings occurred: the repentant sinner in Galilee (Luke 7), Mary of Bethany six days before Pascha (John 12), and another woman in the house of Simon the leper two days before Pascha (Matthew 26, Mark 14).
[^97]: Epiphanius of Salamis (*De Mensuris et Ponderibus*) and patristic realia: The alabaster box (*а҆лава́стръ*, Greek *ἀλάβαστρον*) was a narrow-necked glass or stone vial without handles (*вѵ́кїонъ*, Greek *βικίον*), designed to preserve volatile ointments; *nard pistic* (*на́рдъ пістико́съ*, Greek *νάρδος πιστική*) denotes genuine, pure, unadulterated spikenard.
[^98]: Patristic tradition concerning the Upper Room (*го́рница по́стлана*): Xanthopoulos distinguishes the two suburban Bethany banquets from the Last Supper, noting that the furnished upper room inside Jerusalem on Mount Zion served not only for the Mystical Supper, but also as the refuge of the Apostles after the Crucifixion, the site of the post-Resurrection appearances, the touching of Saint Thomas, and the Descent of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost.
[^99]: The Praises (Lauds) of Holy and Great Wednesday Matins: Four idiomel stichera in Tone 1, vividly contrasting the weeping harlot untying her hair and washing the Savior's feet with Judas Iscariot plotting betrayal and calculating thirty pieces of silver.
[^100]: The Aposticha of Holy and Great Wednesday Matins: Solemn idiomel stichera in Tone 6, meditating on Christ's boundless condescension in dining at the house of the Pharisee and accepting the sinful woman's tears, while Judas extends his hands to receive the price of blood.
