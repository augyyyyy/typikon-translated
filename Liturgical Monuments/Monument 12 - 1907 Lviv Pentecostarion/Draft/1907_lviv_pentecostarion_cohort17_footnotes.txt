[^309]: Theological and patristic typology: The "twofold-sprouting grain, naturally living" (*Зе́рно двоєра́сленное, є҆стественножи́зненное*), sown in the depths of the earth with tears today (John 12:24), evoking Christ's divine and human natures (*διφυὲς σπέρμα*) dying according to the flesh to yield the immortal harvest of the Resurrection.
[^310]: Liturgical funeral hymnography: The "departure songs" or funeral hymns (*пѣ̑сни и҆схо́дныѧ* / *ἐξόδιοι ᾠδαί*), blended with lamentation, chanted by the noble Joseph of Arimathea as he reverently buries the Lord in his own new rock-hewn sepulchre (Matthew 27:59-60).
[^311]: Codicological and liturgical realism: Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus anointing the sacred Body of Christ with a hundred pounds of myrrh and aloes (John 19:39-40), an act celebrated as "newly meet" or "wondrously adorned" (*новолѣ́пнѡ*), causing the whole earth to shudder in astonishment.
[^312]: Scriptural stone typology: The radical antinomy of the "smoothed stone" covering the divine "Cornerstone" (*Ка́мень оу҆глажде́нный, краеꙋго́льный покрыва́етъ Ка́мень*), directly drawing upon Isaiah 28:16, Psalm 117:22, and 1 Peter 2:6, while mortal man covers God in the tomb.
[^313]: Eucharistic vine typology: Christ as the "Life-flowing Vine" (*живото́чнаѧ Лозо̀*), rooted in John 15:1 ("I am the true Vine"), cut down from the earth upon the Cross yet pouring forth the immortal wine of salvation for the life of the world.
[^314]: Irony of the Johannine temple saying: Direct apostrophe to the Jewish authorities concerning Christ's prophetic declaration: "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19-21, Matthew 26:61), which they willfully used as an accusation to condemn Him.
[^315]: Patristic pelican typology: Christ figured forth as the pelican (*ꙗ҆́коже неѧ́сыть* / *ὥσπερ πελεκάν*) of ancient Christian bestiaries and Psalm 101:7, which pierces its own breast to revive its dead young with the outpoured life-streams of its own blood.
[^316]: Scriptural Joshua typology: Contrasting Joshua the son of Nun, who commanded the sun to stand still in the sky over Gibeon to slaughter the five kings (Joshua 10:12-13), with Jesus Christ the true Sun of Righteousness, Who concealed Himself in the tomb to overthrow the ruler of darkness and hades.
[^317]: Fulfillment of the Lucan prophecy: The Virgin Mary crying out in anguish at the foot of the Cross: "O woe! Simeon's prophecy is accomplished: for Thy sword hath passed through my heart, O Emmanuel" (Luke 2:35).
[^318]: Typology of the New Adam: The dual restoration wrought by Christ's Passion: His side pierced by the lance to heal the primeval wound of the rib whence Eve was taken, and His hands nailed to the wood of the Cross to undo the intemperate stretching forth of the hands of the first-parents toward the forbidden tree (Genesis 3:6).
[^319]: Scriptural lamentation typology: Evoking Rachel weeping for her children and refusing to be comforted because they are not (Jeremiah 31:15, Matthew 2:18), here surpassed by the choir of the disciples weeping alongside the Virgin Mother over the slain Son of God.
[^320]: Lviv Stauropegion recension variant: The imperial petition in the Trinity Troparion concluding the Second Stasis: "strengthen the sceptre of the Orthodox Emperor against adversaries" (*ски́птръ правосла́внагѡ І҆мпера́тора оу҆крѣпѝ на ра́тныѧ*), preserving the historic Austro-Hungarian state prayer for Emperor Franz Joseph I used across the Greek Catholic Metropolitanate of Galicia in the 1907 edition.
[^321]: Third Stasis of the Lamentations (*Статі́а тре́тїа*): Chanted in Tone 3 (*гла́съ г҃*), opening with the rapid, colometric stanza: "Every generation, O my Christ, offereth a hymn unto Thy Burial" (*Ро́ди всѝ пѣ́снь Погребе́нїю твоемꙋ̀ прино́сѧтъ, Хрⷭ҇тѐ мо́й*), accompanied by a complete censing of the Epitaphios and the temple.
[^322]: Typological ingratitude of Israel: Juxtaposing the divine benevolence of the Exodus—feeding Israel with heavenly manna in the desert (Exodus 16)—with their bitter return, offering Christ gall and vinegar upon the Cross (Psalm 68:22, Matthew 27:34).
[^323]: Wisdom literature citation: Quoting the imagery of Solomon from Proverbs 22:14 and 23:27 ("a deep pit is the mouth of a strange woman / the transgressor"), here applied to the deceitful and murderous mouth of Judas and the false accusers.
[^324]: Patristic and poetic imagery of Springtime: The poignant maternal lament of the Theotokos apostrophizing Christ as her "sweetest Springtime" (*Ѡ҆ сладча́йшаѧ моѧ̀ Весно̀*), symbolizing the vernal rebirth of creation withered by winter and death, now mourning the departure of its light.
[^325]: Old Testament sacrificial typology: The Virgin Mary figured forth as the Heifer (*Ю҆́ница*) lamenting over the divine Calf (*Теле́цъ*) hung upon the Tree, drawing upon the Levitical heifer sacrifices (Numbers 19) and the prophet Hosea's sacrificial language.
[^326]: Exodus cloud pillar typology: Contrasting He Who of old covered the wandering people of Israel with a protective pillar of cloud (Exodus 13:21-22) with His willing submission to be nailed in helplessness to the wood of the Cross.
[^327]: Typological contrast of the Two Josephs: Contrasting Joseph the Betrothed, who of old fled into Egypt carrying the Infant Savior to preserve Him from Herod (Matthew 2:13-14), with Joseph of Arimathea, who now buries His lifeless Body in the rock tomb.
[^328]: Culmination of Psalm 118: The final verse of the Great Kathisma (Psalm 118:176: "I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek Thy servant; for I have not forgotten Thy commandments") directly capped by the communal petition for ecclesiastical peace and redemption through the Resurrection.
[^329]: The Resurrectional Evlogetaria (*Тропари̑ по Непоро́чнахъ* / *Εὐλογητάρια*): Chanted in Tone 5 with the psalm refrain "Blessed art Thou, O Lord, teach me Thy statutes" (Psalm 118:12), marking the liturgical threshold where nocturnal burial grief gives way to the first joyful announcement of the empty tomb.
[^330]: The angelic rhetorical question: The radiant Angel sitting within the tomb rebuking the Myrrh-bearing women: "Why mingle ye myrrh with compassionate tears, O disciples?" (Luke 24:5), commanding them to dry their tears and herald the Resurrection to the Apostles.
