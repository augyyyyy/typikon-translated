[^125]: The Canon of Holy and Great Thursday, composed by Saint Cosmas the Melodist, Bishop of Maiuma (ca. 675–752), set in Tone 6. The 1907 Lviv edition features an acrostic whose Greek original (*Τῇ μακρᾷ πέμπτῃ μακρὸν ὕμνον ᾄδω*, "On Great Thursday I sing a long hymn") is marked in the margin letter by letter. The Heirmos of Ode 1 draws a classical typological parallel between the miraculous dividing of the Red Sea by Moses' rod (Exodus 14) and Christ's voluntary Passion, through which believers pass unharmed while death and corruption are overwhelmed.
[^126]: An explicit allusion to Proverbs 9:1: *"Wisdom hath builded Her house, She hath hewn out Her seven pillars; She hath killed Her beasts; She hath mingled Her wine; She hath also furnished Her table."* The Church Fathers universally interpret Holy Wisdom (*Премудрость Божия* / *Ἁγία Σοφία*) as the pre-eternal Word of God, Who took flesh in the womb of the Virgin Mary and prepared the Eucharistic banquet of the Mystical Supper.
[^127]: The Heirmos of Ode 3 meditates on the Kenosis (self-emptying) of God the Word (Philippians 2:7), Who, being rich and impassible, impoverished Himself to unite fallen humanity to the divine nature, offering Himself as the true Paschal Lamb before His historical immolation on the Cross.
[^128]: The Heirmos of Ode 4 takes its thematic imagery from the prophetic prayer of Habakkuk (Habakkuk 3:2 LXX), perceiving the divine economy of salvation as the manifestation of the Father's steadfast, unshakeable love in delivering His Only-begotten Son as the propitiation for the sins of the cosmos.
[^129]: A poetic meditation upon Christ's eschatological saying recorded in the Synoptic Gospels (Matthew 26:29; Mark 14:25; Luke 22:18): *"I will not drink henceforth of this fruit of the vine, until that day when I drink it new with you in My Father's Kingdom."* The troparion interprets this "new drink" as the eternal communion of the saints in the Kingdom of God, based upon the Patristic doctrine of theosis (deification): *"for as God I shall be with you who are gods"* (alluding to Psalm 81:6 LXX and John 10:34–35).
[^130]: The Heirmos of Ode 5 celebrates the Sacred Washing of the Feet (the Mandatum, John 13:4–15), linking the cleansing of the Apostles' feet with the prophecy of Isaiah 52:7 LXX: *"How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace."*
[^131]: The Heirmos of Ode 6 takes its imagery from Jonah's prayer from the belly of the sea monster (Jonah 2:2–7 LXX), the canonical type of Christ's descent into Hades and third-day Resurrection.
[^132]: The Kontakion of Holy and Great Thursday, composed by Saint Romanos the Melodist (fl. early 6th century), set in Tone 2, Podoben: *"Seeking the highest"* (*Вы́шнихъ и҆щя̀* / *Τὰ ἄνω ζητῶν*). It contrasts Judas receiving the Eucharistic Bread with his hands while covertly extending those same hands to receive the blood-money of the thirty pieces of silver.
[^133]: The opening verse of the Synaxarion poetically links the Washing of the Feet at the Mystical Supper with the Protoevangelium of Genesis 3:15: the heel of the New Adam trampling upon the serpent's delusion in the Garden of Eden.
[^134]: The third Synaxarion verse commemorates Christ's agony and bloody sweat in Gethsemane (Luke 22:44: *"And being in an agony He prayed more earnestly: and His sweat was as it were great drops of blood falling down to the ground"*), presenting His genuine human dread of death as a deliberate divine trap that deceived the devil.
[^135]: The Synaxarion for Holy and Great Thursday, composed in Byzantine Greek by Nikephoros Kallistos Xanthopoulos (ca. 1256–1335) and translated into Church Slavonic. It summarizes the four interconnected liturgical mysteries commemorated on this day: (1) the Mandatum or Sacred Washing of the disciples' feet; (2) the Mystical Supper and the institution of the Holy Eucharist; (3) the agony and high-priestly prayer in Gethsemane; and (4) the betrayal of Christ by Judas Iscariot.
[^136]: The Patristic tradition, cited here from Saint Athanasius the Great (ca. 296–373), identifies the anonymous man carrying a pitcher of water (Mark 14:13; Luke 22:10) as Zebedee, the father of the Apostles James and John, who prepared the Upper Room on Mount Sion. Other commentators suggested Joseph of Arimathea or Nicodemus.
[^137]: Saint John Chrysostom, in his *Homilies on the Gospel of Saint John* (Homily 70), explains that Christ intentionally began the washing of feet with Judas, offering the traitor every possible inducement to repentance, before approaching Saint Peter.
[^138]: Chrysostom (*Homilies on the Gospel of Saint Matthew*, Homily 81) observes that Christ revealed the identity of the traitor to the beloved disciple John in a whisper, lest the impetuous and fiery Peter should instantly leap up and kill Judas on the spot to defend his Master.
[^139]: The healing of the high priest's servant Malchus, whose right ear was severed by Peter's sword (Luke 22:50–51; John 18:10–11), is the final recorded miracle of physical healing wrought by Christ before His crucifixion.
[^140]: The Synaxarion addresses the complex harmonization between the Synoptic chronology (which describes the Last Supper as the Passover meal) and the Johannine chronology (John 18:28, indicating that the Jewish authorities postponed eating the Passover until Friday evening to complete Christ's trial and execution). Chrysostom argues that the Jewish leaders deliberately deferred their Passover meal in violation of the Law to carry out their murderous plot against Christ.
[^141]: The Heirmos of Ode 7 invokes the biblical narrative of the Three Holy Children (Ananias, Azarias, and Misael) in the Babylonian furnace (Daniel 3), whose dew-moistened deliverance prefigures the divine protection of the Church in the midst of worldly persecution.
[^142]: The Heirmos of Ode 8 draws upon the biblical canticle of the Three Children (*Song of the Three Holy Children*, Daniel 3:57–88 LXX), whose steadfast refusal to bow to Nebuchadnezzar's golden image prefigures the Apostles' faithful communion with Christ on Mount Sion.
[^143]: The Heirmos of Ode 9 (*Стра́нствїя Влады́чня* / *Ξενίας δεσποτικῆς*) is one of the most famous and sublime Eucharistic heirmoi in Byzantine hymnography. The Greek term *xenia* denotes both hospitality/welcoming of guests and a festive banquet, celebrating the Upper Room as the celestial chamber where believers participate in the divine life.
[^144]: The second troparion of Ode 9 interprets Proverbs 8:22 (*"The Lord created me as the beginning of His ways..."*), the key proof-text exploited by the Arians, in an orthodox Christological sense: Christ's divine nature was begotten before all ages, whereas His human nature was created in time for the work of our salvation, allowing the incarnate Word to speak in human terms.
[^145]: The third troparion of Ode 9 formulates the orthodox doctrine of the hypostatic union and the communication of idioms (*communicatio idiomatum*): Christ is truly God and truly man, without confusion or separation, working our salvation through both natures united in His single divine Hypostasis.
[^146]: The famous Exapostilarion of Great Week (*Черто́гъ тво́й ви́ждꙋ* / *Тὸν νυμφῶνά σου βλέπω*), based upon the Parable of the Ten Virgins (Matthew 25:1–13) and the Wedding Feast (Matthew 22:11–14), is chanted for the final time at Matins of Holy and Great Thursday.
[^147]: The first sticheron at the Praises (Lauds), Tone 2, vividly depicts the morning gathering of the Sanhedrin, contrasting the judicial pretensions of the mortal judges with the sovereign dignity of the divine Judge of all creation.
[^148]: The Doxastikon of the Praises (*Слава, и нынѣ*), Tone 2, directly cites the third Servant Song of Isaiah (Isaiah 50:6 LXX): *"I gave My back to scourges, and My cheeks to blows; and I turned not away My face from the shame of spittings."*
[^149]: The first Aposticha sticheron, Tone 8, meditates upon the tragic suicide of Judas (Matthew 27:3–5) and the unwitting prophecy of Caiaphas (John 11:49–50: *"It is expedient for us that one man should die for the people, and that the whole nation perish not"*).
[^150]: The second Aposticha sticheron, Tone 8, sharply contrasts Judas's hypocritical concern for the poor at Bethany (John 12:4–6) with his callous commodification of the Savior, selling Him for thirty pieces of silver—the statutory compensation for a dead slave according to Exodus 21:32.
[^151]: The fourth Aposticha sticheron, Tone 8, delivers a solemn liturgical warning to all communicants, urging them to examine their consciences and never approach the Holy Mysteries in the deceitful, treacherous spirit of Judas.
[^152]: The concluding Aposticha theotokion/sticheron, Tone 5, weaves together Christ's farewell discourses from the Gospel of Saint John: the Mandatum of humility (John 13:14–17), the definition of true love and friendship (John 15:12–15), and the allegory of the True Vine (John 15:1–8).
[^153]: The Troparion of the Prophecy at the First Hour, Tone 3, commemorates Christ's patience and forbearance when struck by the officer of the high priest (John 18:22–23).
[^154]: The Old Testament reading at the First Hour: Jeremiah 11:18–12:15 LXX. The passage contains the celebrated prophetic type of Christ as an innocent lamb led to the slaughter (*"I was like a gentle lamb led to the sacrifice"*, Jer. 11:19 LXX), followed by the prophet's perplexity at the prosperity of the wicked and God's sovereign decree of judgment and ultimate restoration.
[^155]: The remarkable phrase in Jeremiah 11:19 LXX—*"Come and let us put wood into his bread"* (*δεῦτε καὶ ἐμβάλωμεν ξύλον εἰς τὸν ἄρτον αὐτοῦ* / *прїиди́те и҆ вложи́мъ дре́во въ хлѣ́бъ є҆гѡ̀*)—is interpreted by the early Church Fathers (Saint Justin Martyr, Tertullian, Saint Cyril of Jerusalem) as a prophetic prefiguration of the Cross (*wood*) placed upon the Eucharistic Body (*bread*) of Christ.
[^156]: Codicological and typikon rubric: From Holy Thursday onward, all memorial litanies (*lity for the reposed* / *панахиди*) in the church narthex and funeral commemorations are suspended throughout Passion Week and Bright Week, resuming only on the Sunday of Saint Thomas (Antipascha), in witness to the joy of Christ's Resurrection which swallows up all death.
[^157]: On Holy and Great Thursday, the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours are read as simple "three-psalmed" offices without kathisma psalmody or prostrations, reflecting the shift toward the solemn commemoration of the Mystical Supper and Passion.
[^158]: Codicological and iconographic description of the full-width woodcut engraving on fol. 80v (Physical Leaf p88): Christ is depicted at the table of the Mystical Supper, holding the bread in His left hand and raising His right hand in blessing over the Eucharistic chalice. The rubric prescribes the tolling of the semantron at the eighth hour (approximately 2:00 PM) for the solemn celebration of Vespers united with the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great.
[^159]: The rubric prescribes that the priest vest in bright/festive liturgical vestments and perform the Proskomedia (Liturgy of Preparation) during the chanting of the introductory psalmody of Vespers, preparing the Holy Bread for both the Thursday Liturgy and the Reserved Sacrament (*запасні Дари*) consecrated for the sick throughout the coming ecclesiastical year.
[^160]: The solemn Vespers Doxastikon, Tone 6, compares Judas's ungrateful betrayal at the Mystical Supper to the rebellious murmuring of the Israelites who ate the manna in the Sinai wilderness (Psalm 77:18–31 LXX), warning of the eternal perdition that awaits those who treat holy things with contempt.
[^161]: The first Old Testament reading at Thursday Vespers: Exodus 19:10–19 LXX, recounting the terrifying theophany upon Mount Sinai, the sanctification of Israel, and God descending in fire amid thunder and trumpets, serving as the Old Covenant type of the profound sanctification required to approach the New Covenant banquet.
[^162]: The second Old Testament reading at Thursday Vespers: Job 38:1–23 and 42:1–5 LXX, recording God answering Job out of the whirlwind, rebuking mortal presumption and demonstrating the boundless wisdom, power, and transcendence of the Creator, culminating in Job's humble confession of faith.
[^163]: The third Old Testament reading at Thursday Vespers: Isaiah 50:4–11 LXX, the third "Servant Song" of Isaiah, which provides the prophetic script for Christ's voluntary submission to scourging, buffetings, and spittings during His trial.
[^164]: The rubrics at the conclusion of the Old Testament readings mark the seamless transition from Vespers into the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great: the Little Litany is intoned, followed by the chanting of the Trisagion (*Святый Боже*) and the Prokeimenon of the Apostle.
