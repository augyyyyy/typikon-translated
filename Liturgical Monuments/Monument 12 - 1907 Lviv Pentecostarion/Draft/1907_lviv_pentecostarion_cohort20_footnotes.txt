[^359]: The Eleventh Reading: from the Prophecy of Isaiah (Isaiah 61:1-9) — The Messianic Anointing of the Servant of the Lord. Proclaimed by Christ Himself in the synagogue of Nazareth (Luke 4:18-19) as fulfilled in His person, this lection heralds the Paschal transformation from mourning to jubilation ("glory instead of ashes, the anointing of joy to the mourners") and the restoration of the everlasting covenant through the holy priesthood of the new dispensation.
[^360]: The Twelfth Reading: from the Fourth Book of Kings (4 Kings 4:8-37 [2 Kings 4:8-37]) — Elisha and the Resurrection of the Shunammite's Son. The miraculous conception granted to the barren woman, followed by the bodily resurrection of her deceased child through the prophet stretching himself upon him and breathing seven times upon him, prefigures the descent of Christ into hades and His vivification of fallen Adam and all mortal flesh.
[^361]: The Thirteenth Reading: from the Prophecy of Isaiah (Isaiah 63:11–64:5) — The Prophetic Invocation of the Divine Arm and the Rendition of the Heavens. The prophet recalls the exodus of Israel through the Red Sea by the right hand of Moses, invoking the Fatherhood of God ("Thou, O Lord, art our Father, our Redeemer: from the beginning Thy name is upon us") and imploring Him to rend the heavens and descend with consuming fire upon the adversaries.
[^362]: The Fourteenth Reading: from the Prophecy of Jeremiah (Jeremiah 31:31-34) — The New Covenant Written upon the Heart. Foretelling the transition from the external Sinai covenant to the internal sacramental economy of grace, the Lord promises to write His divine laws directly upon the hearts and minds of His people, culminating in the remission of all transgressions through Christ's voluntary sacrifice.
[^363]: The Fifteenth Reading: from the Prophecy of Daniel (Daniel 3:1-56/88) — The Three Youths in the Babylonian Furnace. Chanted at the conclusion of the baptismal lections, the preservation of Ananias, Azarias, and Misael unburned amidst the raging flames by the Angel of the Lord constitutes the supreme Old Testament type of Christ's descent into hades, descending into the fiery realm of death without suffering corruption, while delivering the righteous from eternal fire.
[^364]: The Canticle of the Three Youths (*Бл҃гослови́те всѧ̑ дѣла̀ Гдⷭ҇нѧ* / Daniel 3:57-88): The ancient *Benedicite*, chanted antiphonally between reader and choir while all stand ("We stand here, and sing"), uniting all orders of the cosmos—angels, heavens, waters, celestial luminaries, elements, and the spirits of the just—in the cosmic doxology anticipating the Paschal dawn.
[^365]: The Baptismal Anthem (*Е҆ли́цы во Хрⷭ҇та̀ крести́стесѧ* / Galatians 3:27): Substituted for the Trisagion on Holy Saturday, recalling the historical celebration of the Paschal baptism of the catechumens during the 15 Old Testament readings, after which the newly illumined were led in procession into the temple clad in white robes for the Eucharistic Liturgy.
[^366]: The Epistle of Holy Saturday (Romans 6:3-11, pericope 91): St. Paul's classic theological locus on Holy Baptism as sacramental co-burial and co-resurrection with Christ, asserting that as Christ died unto sin once and liveth unto God, the baptized are dead unto sin and alive unto God in Christ Jesus.
[^367]: The Prokeimenon Anthem of Tone 7 (*Воскреснѝ Бж҃е, сꙋдѝ землѝ* / Psalm 81:8 [82:8]): Replacing the customary Alleluia, this triumphant refrain with the verses of Psalm 81 is sung while the sacred ministers divest themselves of their dark Lenten vestments in the sanctuary and vest in white and radiant robes, while the dark paraments of the holy table and church are replaced with bright white coverings.
[^368]: The Gospel of the Resurrection (Matthew 28:1-20, pericope 115): The first liturgical reading of the Resurrection of Christ, narrating the dawn at the sepulchre, the earthquake, the descent of the Angel rolling back the stone, the fear of the guards, the appearance of the Risen Christ to the women, and the universal apostolic commission on the mountain in Galilee.
[^369]: The Cherubic Hymn of Holy Saturday (*Да молчи́тъ вся́каѧ пло́ть человѣ́ча* / Tone 8): Borrowed from the ancient Jerusalem Liturgy of Saint James, this troparion replaces the standard Byzantine *Cherubikon* (*И҆́же херꙋві́мы*), bidding all earthly creation to be silent as the King of kings and Lord of lords cometh forth to be slain and given as spiritual food to the faithful, preceded by the celestial hosts.
[^370]: The Blessing of Bread and Wine at the Analoy: Because the Liturgy concluded late in the evening and the faithful remained in church for the vigil leading to midnight Paschal Matins, bread, wine, dates, and figs were blessed upon an analoy in the nave to sustain the worshippers during the reading of the Acts of the Apostles.
[^371]: Reader's Service Rubric for Vespers: When the Divine Liturgy cannot be celebrated due to dire necessity, the Typikon directs the office to conclude as standard Vespers with the Resurrection Aposticha Stichera in Tone 1 from the Oktoechos, preserving the complete hymnographic witness of the Resurrection.
[^372]: The Nocturnal Vigil and the Reading of the Acts of the Apostles: The Typikon specifies that the Liturgy must conclude at the second hour of the night (approx. 8:00 PM). The Slavonic rubric notes the Greek practice where the brethren remain continuously in church, partaking of the blessed bread and wine while the reader begins the continuous reading of the Acts of the Apostles (*Дѣя́нїа ст҃ы́хъ а҆пⷭ҇лъ*).
[^373]: The Canon of Holy Saturday Compline by Symeon Metaphrastes (the Logothete): Entitled "On the Bodily Burial of the Lord God and our Savior Jesus Christ, and on the Lamentation of the Most Holy Theotokos" in Tone 6. The rubric notes that this office and canon are absent from the Greek Triodion, which omits Compline on Holy Saturday evening, witnessing to a distinct Slavic liturgical development.
[^374]: The Lamentation of the Theotokos at the Burial of Christ: Sharing its Heirmoi with the Great Saturday Matins Canon ("He Who in ancient times concealed..."), the troparia of this canon present the maternal grief of the Virgin Mary standing before the Cross, pleading with Joseph of Arimathea to approach Pilate and bury the Body of her Son.
