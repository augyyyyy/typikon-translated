[^66]: The continuous reading of the Four Gospels (*Четвероева́нгелїе*) during Holy Week: Matthew, Mark, and Luke are read in their entirety, while John is read up to the discourse on the Passion (John 13:31). The readings are distributed across nine sessions during the Third, Sixth, and Ninth Hours of Great Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday, concluding at the Ninth Hour of Great Wednesday before the Presanctified Divine Liturgy.
[^67]: The liturgical order of the Ninth Hour on Great Monday through Wednesday omits the customary kathisma psalmody (*стїхосло́вїе*). The service proceeds directly from the three fixed Psalms to the Troparion of the Ninth Hour, followed by the Gospel proclamation from the adorned analogion in the center of the nave.
[^68]: At Great Monday Presanctified Vespers, the appointed stichera on *Lord, I have cried* reprise the solemn idiomel hymns of Monday Matins (*"The Lord, going unto His voluntary Passion..."*), emphasizing the journey toward Jerusalem and the voluntary character of Christ's sacrifice.
[^69]: The Old Testament reading from Exodus 1:1–20 at Presanctified Vespers recounts the enslavement and affliction of Israel under Pharaoh in Egypt. In patristic typology, Pharaoh figures the devil, Egypt represents the tyranny of sin, and the brick-making servitude prefigures humanity's spiritual bondage prior to Christ's redemption.
[^70]: Heliopolis (On / *Ὢν, ἥ ἐστιν Ἡλιούπολις*): Exodus 1:11 in the Septuagint explicitly witnesses three fortified treasure cities built for Pharaoh—Pithom, Raamses, and On (Heliopolis)—whereas the Masoretic Text names only Pithom and Raamses.
[^71]: The Old Testament reading from Job 1:1–12 at Presanctified Vespers presents the righteous Job as an innocent sufferer whose sudden and severe trials prefigure the unmerited sufferings, passion, and eventual vindication of Christ.
[^72]: The ancient Studite-Sabaite fasting rule prescribes xerophagy (*сꙋхоꙗде́нїе*—eating only uncooked or dried food, bread, water, and raw or salt-preserved vegetables without oil or wine) for the first three days of Passion Week, mirroring the severe ascetic discipline of the First Week of Great Lent.
[^73]: Canon 89 of the Sixth Ecumenical Council (In Trullo, 692) directs the faithful to spend the days of the saving Passion in fasting, prayer, and contrition of heart, terminating the Paschal fast at midnight of Great Saturday, in accordance with the Gospel testimonies of Matthew (28:1) and Luke (24:1).
[^74]: Great Compline on Holy Monday evening features a three-ode canon (tri-ode / *трипѣ́снецъ*) by Saint Andrew of Crete (Odes 2, 8, and 9), meditating on Christ's eschatological discourse on the Mount of Olives (Matthew 24–25).
[^75]: The Parable of the Millstones (*же́рновная при́тча*) refers to Matthew 24:41 and Luke 17:35: "Two women shall be grinding at the mill; the one shall be taken, and the other left," allegorically interpreted by the Church Fathers as distinguishing those who work with spiritual vigilance from those who labor in earthly distraction.
[^76]: Matins of Great and Holy Tuesday centers on the Parable of the Ten Virgins (Matthew 25:1–13) and the Parable of the Talents (Matthew 25:14–30), calling the soul to active vigilance, spiritual sobriety, and works of mercy before the imminent arrival of the Heavenly Bridegroom.
[^77]: Codicological cross-reference to printed folio 37 (Physical Leaf p42 = Printed Folio 37 + 5), where the Troparion of the Bridegroom (*"Behold, the Bridegroom cometh at midnight..."* / *Се́ Жени́хъ гряде́тъ*) is given in full at the opening of Passion Week on Great Monday.
[^78]: The Synaxarion for Great and Holy Tuesday, composed by Nikephoros Kallistos Xanthopoulos (ca. 1256–1335), expounds the Parable of the Ten Virgins, demonstrating that bodily virginity and ascetic rigor without the oil of mercy, compassion, and active charity cannot open the gates of the heavenly bridal chamber.
[^79]: Patristic teaching on virginity and almsgiving: The synaxarist invokes Saint Isidore of Pelusium and Saint John Chrysostom in teaching that nothing is more disgraceful than virginity overcome by avarice or stinginess toward the poor.
[^80]: The Matins Di-ode of Great Tuesday, composed by Saint Cosmas the Melodist, Bishop of Maiuma (ca. 675–752), consists of Odes 8 and 9 and carries the Greek alphabetical acrostic *Τρίτῃ τε* ("And on the Third Day"), celebrating the adornment of spiritual lamps with the oil of charity.
[^81]: The solemn Exapostilarion (Hymn of Light / *Свѣти́ленъ*) of Passion Week, *"Thy bridal chamber I behold adorned, O my Savior..."* (*Черто́гъ тво́й ви́ждꙋ, Спа́се мо́й*), chanted thrice at the conclusion of the canon while the church is in darkness or lit only by candlelight.
[^82]: The Sixth Hour prophecy on Great Tuesday: Ezekiel 1:21–28, continuing the Prophet Ezekiel's vision of the chariot-throne (*Merkavah*), the crystalline firmament, the sound of wings like the Almighty (*El Shaddai*), and the sapphire throne bearing the appearance of the Glory of the Lord.
