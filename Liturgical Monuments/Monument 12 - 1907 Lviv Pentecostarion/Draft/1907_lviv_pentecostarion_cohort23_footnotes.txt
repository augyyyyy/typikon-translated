[^409]: The solemn Paschal Gospel reading ceremony (John 1:1-17, pericope 1). In Slavic and Byzantine liturgical practice, the Prologue of Saint John is proclaimed with exceptional solemnity: concelebrating priests and deacons are stationed at various intervals along the nave from the holy table to the western doors, reading the text responsively in sections (*statiai*), often in multiple languages (such as Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and vernacular tongues), symbolizing the preaching of the Gospel unto all ends of the earth. At each verse or section, the church bells and semantra (*bilo*) are rung, culminating in a triumphant full peal at the conclusion of the reading.
[^410]: The continuous unclosing of the sanctuary doors throughout Bright Week. In Byzantine and Slavic liturgical tradition, the Royal Doors (the Great Gates) and the deacon's doors of the iconostasis remain wide open day and night continuously from the Paschal Midnight Office through Bright Saturday, even during the clergy communion, signifying the torn veil of the Temple (Matt. 27:51), the shattered gates of hades, the open tomb of Christ, and the unimpeded access of all believers into the heavenly Holy of Holies through the Resurrection.
[^411]: The blessing of Paschal foods (flesh-meats, cheese, and eggs) on the Holy and Great Sunday of Pascha. Concluding the strict fasting of Great Lent and Holy Week, this customary benediction sanctifies the festive foods brought by the faithful, drawing typological parallels from Old Testament sacrifices (Abraham's ram, Abel's lamb) and the fatted calf slain for the return of the prodigal son (Luke 15:23).
[^412]: Canonical prohibition against bringing flesh-meats into the sanctuary or church temple. The rubrist provides an extensive dogmatic and canonical instruction distinguishing between blessed festive food and the true Eucharistic Pascha—Christ Himself, the Lamb of God (1 Cor. 5:7). Citing Canon 3 of the Holy Apostles (forbidding the offering of milk, honey, meats, or fermented beverages at the holy table) and Canon 94 of the Quinisext Council in Trullo (Canon 99 in standard Greek collections, condemning the Armenian custom of preparing and eating meats within sacred precincts), the rubric strictly mandates that the blessing of foods must take place outside the temple or in the narthex.
[^413]: The Order of the Artos (Послѣ́дованїе А҆́ртоса). The Artos (Greek ἄρτος, "leavened bread") is a large loaf consecrated at the end of the Paschal Liturgy bearing the icon of the Resurrection or the sign of the Cross, symbolizing Christ as the living Bread of Life (John 6:35, 48) Who visibly remained with His disciples after His rising. In monastic typika, the Artos is carried in solemn procession to the refectory (*trapeza*) with singing and bell-ringing, elevated in place of the Panagia after the meal with the joyful exclamation "Christ is risen!", venerated by all the brethren, and returned to the church, where it rests before the iconostasis until its ceremonial distribution on Bright Saturday.
[^414]: The Paschal Order for the Burial of the Dead during Bright Week (Послѣ́дованїе погребе́нїѧ въ свѣ́тлꙋю Седми́цꙋ). If a Christian falls asleep in the Lord during Bright Week, the usual mournful funeral rites, penitential psalms, and dirges of the Trebnik are replaced by joyful Paschal hymns, the Paschal Canon, and the triumphal proclamation of Christ's victory over hades, testifying that for the faithful, death has been transformed into a Paschal passage (*transitus*) unto eternal life.
[^415]: The Paschal Agape Vespers (Вече́рнѧ любвѝ / *Vesperae Charitatis*). Celebrated on the afternoon of Pascha Sunday, the clergy vest in full festive priestly vestments. The service begins solemnly with the Paschal verses before the holy table, the Little Entrance is made with the Holy Gospel Book, and the Gospel of the Resurrection (John 20:19-25) is read facing the people, frequently in multiple languages.
[^416]: The Tone 2 Octoechos resurrectional stichera appointed for Paschal Agape Vespers. Although the Paschal Canon is chanted in Tone 1, the Octoechos cycle resumes at Paschal Vespers, beginning with Tone 2, with each subsequent day of Bright Week advancing through the tones of the Octoechos (Bright Monday: Tone 2; Bright Tuesday: Tone 3; Bright Wednesday: Tone 4; Bright Thursday: Tone 5; Bright Friday: Tone 6; Bright Saturday: Tone 8).
[^417]: The Anatolian (Eastern / Ἀνατολικά) Stichera. In the Byzantine Octoechos, these resurrectional hymns are traditionally attributed to Patriarch Anatolius of Constantinople (reigned 449–458), who composed four additional stichera for each tone to accompany the psalms at Vespers and Praises.
[^418]: The Dogmatic Theotokion (*Dogmatikon*) of Tone 2: "The shadow of the law passed away..." (Пре́йде сѣ́нь зако́ннаѧ). Attributed to Saint John of Damascus, this hymn juxtaposes the Old Testament typologies of the unburnt bush of Horeb (Exodus 3:2) and the pillar of fire (Exodus 13:21) with the mystery of the Virgin Birth and the Incarnation of Christ, the Sun of Righteousness.
[^419]: The Solemn Entrance with the Gospel at Agape Vespers. Unlike ordinary daily or Sunday Vespers where the Little Entrance is made with the censer alone, at Paschal Vespers the deacon and priest make the entrance carrying the precious Gospel Book, in anticipation of the solemn proclamation of the Resurrection Gospel.
[^420]: The Great Prokeimenon of Paschal Vespers, Tone 7: "What God is so great as our God? Thou art the God Who workest wonders!" (Ps. 76:14-15a [77:13-14a]). Chanted in the festive Grave Tone (Tone 7) with elaborate melismatic melodies, this ancient responsory is repeated on Bright Saturday evening and at Great Feasts of the Lord.
[^421]: The Agape Vespers Gospel reading: John 20:19-25 (pericope 65). Recounting the appearance of the Risen Lord Jesus to the disciples on the evening of the first day of the week, the shut doors, the greeting of peace, the exhibition of His wounds, the insufflation of the Holy Spirit with the authority to remit and retain sins, and the absence of the Apostle Thomas.
[^422]: Bright Monday (Понедѣ́льникъ свѣ́тлыѧ Седми́цы). The second day of Paschal celebration, commemorating the continuing radiance of Christ's Resurrection. In monastic usage, the priest begins Matins vested simply in mantle and epitrachelion, whereas in parochial usage, full vestments are worn throughout the week.
[^423]: Continuous Patristic Reading during Paschaltide. The rubric specifies that beginning on Bright Monday, the monastic community begins the reading of Saint John Chrysostom's *Homilies on the Gospel of Saint John* (PG 59), reading sequentially through this monumental exegetical commentary during Matins throughout the fifty days until the Sunday of All Saints.
[^424]: The Bright Monday Cross Procession (Хре́стний хід). Following Matins and the First Hour, a solemn procession is made around the monastery grounds or church precincts bearing banners, the Gospel, the Cross, the icon of the Resurrection, and the Artos, singing the Paschal Canon and reading the four Resurrection Gospels at the four sides of the church.
[^425]: The Epistle reading for Bright Monday: Acts 1:12-17, 21-26 (pericope 2). Chronicling the return of the Apostles to the upper room in Jerusalem after the Ascension and the choosing of Matthias by lot to fill the apostolic ministry vacated by Judas.
[^426]: The Gospel reading for Bright Monday: John 1:18-28 (pericope 2). The second section of the Johannine Prologue, witnessing that the Only-Begotten Son has declared the invisible God, and recording Saint John the Baptist's testimony unto the Priests and Levites: "I baptize with water, but there standeth one among you whom ye know not."
[^427]: Bright Monday Vespers (for Bright Tuesday). In accordance with the Bright Week progression, the stichera on "Lord, I have cried" shift from Tone 2 to Tone 3 of the Octoechos, celebrating Christ's victory over the grave in the third mode.
[^428]: The Dogmatic Theotokion of Tone 3: "How can we not marvel at Thy divine-human childbearing..." (Ка́кѡ не диви́мсѧ Бг҃омꙋ́жномꙋ Рождествꙋ̀ твоему̀). Composed by Saint John of Damascus, this dogmatic hymn expounds the Chalcedonian confession of the two unconfused natures in the single Person of Christ, born of the Father without mother before all ages, and born in the flesh without father from the Virgin Mother of God in the fullness of time.
[^429]: The Great Prokeimenon of Bright Monday Vespers, Tone 7: "Our God is in heaven and on earth: whatsoever He hath willed, He hath done!" (Ps. 113:11 [115:3]). Appointed with verses taken from the ancient Passover Hallel (Psalm 113 [114/115]), proclaiming the exodus of Israel from Egypt and the Jordan turning back as prophetic types of Christ's deliverance of humanity from hades.
[^430]: Bright Tuesday Matins (Во Вто́рникъ свѣ́тлыѧ Седми́цы на Оу҆́трени). The third day of Bright Week, which pairs the Paschal Canon with the resurrectional stichera of Tone 3 at the Praises, celebrating the women bearing myrrh and the confused flight of the Roman sepulchre guard.
