[^165]: The Epistle reading at the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great on Holy and Great Thursday: 1 Corinthians 11:23–32 (zacalo 149). This is the classic Pauline locus of the institution of the Holy Eucharist at the Mystical Supper, delivered by the Apostle as received directly from the Lord ("For I received of the Lord that which also I delivered unto you"). The reading articulates the essential theological and moral requirements for partaking of the Holy Mysteries, warning against receiving the Body and Blood of the Lord unworthily without discerning the Lord's Body, which incurs divine judgment and chastening.
[^166]: The celebrated Troparion of Holy and Great Thursday (*Ве́чери твоеѧ̀ Та́йныѧ дне́сь* / *Τοῦ Δείπνου σου τοῦ μυστικοῦ*), composed in the 5th or 6th century (traditionally attributed to the liturgical tradition of Antioch or Constantinople). On Holy Thursday, it replaces three distinct ordinary chants of the Divine Liturgy: (1) the Cherubic Hymn (*Херувимская пѣснь*) at the Great Entrance, where it is sung thrice in Tone 6; (2) the Communion Verse (*Прича́стенъ* / *Koinonikon*); and (3) the post-communion Thanksgiving Hymn (*"Let our mouths be filled with Thy praise, O Lord"* / *Да исполнятся уста наша*), where it is repeated continuously throughout the communion of the clergy and the faithful. In the wider Byzantine and Ruthenian tradition, it also serves as the universal prayer of personal preparation recited before receiving the Holy Mysteries at every Divine Liturgy.
[^167]: Codicological and Typikon rubrics of the Liturgy of Saint Basil the Great on Holy Thursday: At this service, an additional Holy Lamb (*Agnets*) is consecrated, saturated with the Precious Blood, dried according to the prescribed rite in the Sluzhebnik, and reverently placed in the Artophorion (Tabernacle) upon the holy table as the Reserved Sacrament (*запасні Дари*) for the communion of the sick throughout the entire subsequent year. Furthermore, the rubric specifies that the customary post-communion prostrations cease, and that the liturgical Order of the Foot-Washing is celebrated immediately following the Prayer behind the Ambo. At the monastic or parish refectory following the Liturgy, wine and oil are permitted to console the faithful before the severe fast of Great Friday.
[^168]: The Order of the Washing of Feet (*Чинъ Оумове́нїю но́гъ* / *Нιπτήρ* / *Niptēr*) on Holy and Great Thursday. An ancient ceremony attested as early as the 4th-century pilgrimage of Egeria and codified in the Byzantine imperial and monastic typika, commemorating Christ's self-emptying love in washing the feet of the twelve disciples at the Mystical Supper (John 13:1–17). In the Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church, the rite was celebrated with special episcopal solemnity in cathedral churches (such as St. George's Cathedral and the Dormition Church in Lviv), performed by the Metropolitan or Bishop vested in full episcopal vestments, assisted by twelve archpriests, priests, and deacons representing the Apostles.
[^169]: The Heirmos of Ode 5 of the Holy Thursday Matins Canon by Saint Cosmas the Melodist (*Союзомъ любве свѧзуеми апли* / *Συνδέσμῳ ἀγάπης συνδεόμενοι οἱ Ἀπόστολοι*), Tone 6, chanted as the procession of clergy, led by the Hierarch, moves from the sanctuary to the elevated dais prepared in the center of the nave for the Foot-Washing.
[^170]: The Troparia and Stichera Idiomela of the Foot-Washing in Tone 1, meditating on the supreme theological paradox of the Kenosis: the transcendent Creator, Who holds the waters in the clouds and bridles the oceans, kneeling upon the pavement to wash the dust from the feet of mortal clay, thereby conferring a spiritual ladder of ascent over the hostile demonic powers.
[^171]: The solemn Idiomel of the Foot-Washing in Tone 8 (*Дне́сь Непристу́пный существомъ* / *Σήμερον ὁ ἀπρόσιτος τῇ οὐσίᾳ*), presenting the dramatic visual and liturgical contrast between Christ's inaccessible divine essence and His assumption of the form of a bondservant, girding Himself with a linen towel and pouring water into the basin.
[^172]: The dramatic apostrophe to Judas Iscariot in Tone 8 (*Оу́не ти бы́сть Ію́до* / *Κρεῖττόν σοι ἦν Ἰούδα*), directly expanding the tragic prophecy of Christ in Matthew 26:24 and Mark 14:21: *"Woe unto that man by whom the Son of man is betrayed! It had been good for that man if he had not been born."* The hymn contrasts the unbinding of the Apostolic crown with the salvation of the repentant thief on the Cross, and reproaches Judas for hypocritically pretending grief over the precious myrrh of the sinful woman while brazenly selling the innocent Blood of the Righteous One.
[^173]: The Great Litany (*Великая ектенія*) of the Foot-Washing contains special rubrical petitions asking for the sanctification of the water by the descent and operation of the Holy Spirit, that it may serve for the cleansing of the spiritual defilements of the soul. In accordance with the diplomatic and recensional standards of the 1907 Lviv Stauropegion edition, the litany retains the official imperial supplication for Emperor Franz Joseph I of Austria-Hungary (*ѡ бл҃говѣ́рномъ І҆мпера́торѣ на́шемъ*), reflecting the historic liturgical usage of the Galician Metropolia under Austro-Hungarian jurisdiction.
[^174]: The first prayer of the Foot-Washing, read aloud by the Hierarch (*Господи и Боже преблагій*), invokes the Protoevangelium of Genesis 3:15, entreating the Lord to preserve the souls and bodies of the faithful from the deceitful serpent that lies in wait for the human heel, and granting spiritual authority to tread upon serpents and scorpions and all the power of the enemy (alluding to Psalm 90:13 LXX and Luke 10:19).
[^175]: The second prayer of the Foot-Washing, recited secretly by the Hierarch (*втай глаголемая*) while all present bow their heads. It meditates upon Christ's pedagogical inversion of worldly power (Luke 22:24–27), wherein He designated the last to be first, teaching His disciples that true greatness in the Kingdom of God consists in sacrificial service to one's brethren.
[^176]: The First Gospel Pericope of the Foot-Washing: John 13:3–11 (section 44). The liturgical text is structured as a living liturgical drama: as the protodeacon reads the sacred narrative, the Hierarch performs the physical actions simultaneously in real time—divesting himself of his episcopal vestments (omophorion, sakkos, klobuk), girding his loins with a linen towel (*lention* / *obrus*), pouring warm water into the copper basin, and kneeling on both knees before each of the twelve priests in succession, beginning on the left side, to wash their feet, dry them with the towel, and kiss them reverently.
[^177]: The dramatic dialogue between Christ and Saint Peter: When the Hierarch reaches the senior archpriest seated in the place of Saint Peter (*обдержай мѣсто Петрово*), the deacon's continuous reading pauses and the two celebrants chant the biblical exchange verbatim. When Christ insists that without this washing Peter has no part with Him, the archpriest representing Peter rises in fear and astonishment, gestures with his hands toward his feet, then elevates his hands, and finally bows his head, crying aloud: *"Lord, not my feet only, but also my hands and my head!"*
[^178]: The Second Gospel Pericope of the Foot-Washing: John 13:12–17 (section 45), chanted by the Hierarch himself from the ambo or episcopal cathedra after he has finished the foot-washing, resumed his episcopal vestments, and sat down again, delivering Christ's solemn dominical commandment of mutual brotherly service: *"For I have given you an example, that ye should do as I have done to you."*
[^179]: The concluding prayer of the Foot-Washing (*Господи Боже нашъ, по мнозѣй милости твоей излаявый себе*), followed by the sprinkling of the clergy and faithful with the blessed water from the basin. The troparion in Tone 6 (*Оумывше нозѣ* / *Νιψάμενοι τοὺς πόδας*) commemorates the Apostles departing from the Upper Room on Mount Sion to accompany the Lord across the brook Cedron to the Mount of Olives.
[^180]: Small Compline on Holy and Great Thursday, featuring the dramatic Tri-ode (*трипѣ́снецъ*) composed by Saint Andrew of Crete (ca. 660–740), containing Odes 5, 8, and 9 in Tone 8. Unlike the theological and typological canon of St. Cosmas sung at Matins, St. Andrew's hymnody is profoundly psychological and dramatic, vividly portraying the agony of Gethsemane, the deceit of Judas's honeyed kiss, the weeping of Peter, the cowardly hand-washing of Pontius Pilate, and the blood-thirsty demands of the Sanhedrin.
[^181]: Iconographic and codicological description of the full-width woodcut engraving on fol. 91r (Physical Leaf p99): The Mocking and Crowning with Thorns. Christ is depicted bound and seated, clad in a mock-regal robe and holding a reed as a mock scepter, while Roman soldiers press the crown of thorns upon His head with staves and mockingly bend the knee before Him. The engraving is signed at the bottom by the 19th-century Galician wood-engraver A. Jędrzejowski / Jewecki.
[^182]: The monumental Office of the Holy and Saving Passions of Our Lord Jesus Christ (*Послѣ́дованїе ст҃ы́хъ и҆ сп҃си́тельныхъ Страсте́й Гдⷭ҇а на́шегѡ І҆и҃са Хрⷭ҇та̀*), celebrated on Thursday evening as the Matins of Holy and Great Friday. Commonly known as the "Service of the Twelve Passion Gospels" (*Стра́сти*), this office constitutes the liturgical summit of Holy Week. Its structure consists of twelve continuous Gospel pericopes (the *Passion Gospels*) extracted from all four Evangelists, harmonizing the entire historical account of Christ's Passion from the Farewell Discourse in the Upper Room to His death on Golgotha and burial by Joseph of Arimathea.
[^183]: The celebrated Troparion of Great Friday Matins (*Егда́ сла́внїи ᲂу҆чени́цы* / *Ὅτε οἱ ἔνδοξοι μαθηταί*), Tone 8, chanted three times slowly and melodiously (*ко́сно и со сладкопѣ́нїемъ*), during which the ecclesiarch distributes lighted beeswax candles to all the clergy and worshippers in the darkened temple, symbolizing the divine light of Christ shining into the darkness of the Passion and the betrayal of Judas.
[^184]: The First Passion Gospel: John 13:31–18:1 (section 46). This is the longest of the Twelve Gospels, containing Christ's solemn Farewell Discourse and High-Priestly Prayer in the Upper Room, comforting His disciples, promising the descent of the Holy Spirit the Comforter, establishing the new commandment of love, and concluding as Christ leads the Eleven out across the brook Cedron into the Garden of Gethsemane.
[^185]: The opening troparia of Antiphon 1 in Tone 8 (*Кня́зи лю́дстїи собра́шасѧ вкꙋ́пѣ* / *Οἱ ἄρχοντες τῶν λαῶν συνήχθησαν*), based directly upon the messianic prophecy of Psalm 2:2 LXX: *"The kings of the earth stood up, and the rulers gathered themselves together, against the Lord, and against His Christ."* This initiates the cycle of fifteen Antiphons of Great Friday Matins, which alternate between the first eight Passion Gospels.
