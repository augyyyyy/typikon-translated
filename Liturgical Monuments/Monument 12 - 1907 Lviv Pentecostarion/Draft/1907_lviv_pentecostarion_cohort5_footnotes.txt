[^50]: Palm Sunday Evening dismissal order: The penitential conclusion of Palm Sunday Vespers marks the formal entrance into the severe fast and solemnity of Great and Holy Passion Week (*Страстная Седмица*), reintroducing the great prostrations with the prayer of St. Ephrem the Syrian and the penitential troparia (*"Rejoice, O Virgin Theotokos"* / *Богоро́дице Дѣ́во ра́дꙋйсѧ*).
[^51]: The Compline Tri-ode of Saint Andrew of Crete: Throughout Great Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday, Small Compline features a three-ode canon (tri-ode / *трипѣ́снецъ*) composed by Saint Andrew of Crete (ca. 660–740), Archbishop of Gortyna, meditating on the impending voluntary Passion of Christ and the typological figures of the day (Joseph the All-Comely and the Barren Fig Tree).
[^52]: The reading of the Psalter during Passion Week: In accordance with ancient Studite and Sabaite typika, the entire Psalter (all 20 kathismata) is read once through between Monday and Wednesday noon (three kathismata at Matins, and one each at the Third and Sixth Hours), ceasing completely after the Presanctified Divine Liturgy of Great Wednesday.
[^53]: The 17th Kathisma (Psalm 118 LXX / *Непоро́чны*) is reserved for Great and Holy Saturday Matins at the Burial of Christ (the Lamentations / *Погребе́нїе Хрїсто́во*), while Kathisma 18 (the Gradual Psalms 119–133 LXX) is sung at Vespers throughout Great Lent and Holy Week.
[^54]: The Bridegroom Matins (*Служба Жениха*) of Great and Holy Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday: Celebrated by anticipation on the preceding evening in parish and monastic practice. The solemn Troparion *"Behold, the Bridegroom cometh at midnight"* (*Се́ Жени́хъ гряде́тъ въ полꙋ́нощи*) derives from the Parable of the Ten Virgins (Matthew 25:1–13) and serves as the thematic anthem of the opening days of Passion Week.
[^55]: Patristic readings at Bridegroom Matins: The appointed homiletic commentaries of Saint John Chrysostom on the Gospel of Matthew (specifically Homilies 67 and 68, expounding the withering of the barren fig tree and the cleansing of the Temple).
[^56]: Saint John Chrysostom's Homily on the Withered Fig Tree (*Сло́во о и҆зсо́хшей смоко́вницѣ*), examining the mystical significance of the curse pronounced upon the leafy, fruitless tree as a prophetic judgment upon the formalist hypocrisy of the Old Covenant leadership.
[^57]: Saint John Damascene's Homily on the Withered Fig Tree and the Parable of the Wicked Vinedressers (Matthew 21:33–44).
[^58]: The Matins Tri-ode of Great Monday composed by Saint Cosmas the Melodist, Bishop of Maiuma (ca. 675–752), bearing the Greek alphabetical acrostic *Τῇ δευτέρᾳ* ("On the Second Day," i.e., Monday), spanning Odes 1, 8, and 9.
[^59]: The Synaxarion for Great and Holy Monday by Nikephoros Kallistos Xanthopoulos (ca. 1256–1335), commemorating two foundational types: the Patriarch Joseph the All-Comely (*І҆ѡ́сифъ прекра́сный*) as a prefiguration of Christ's betrayal, descent into the pit, and exaltation as Ruler and Giver of the Bread of Life; and the cursed and withered fig tree (*и҆зсо́хшая смоко́вница*) as an emblem of spiritual barrenness and hypocrisy.
[^60]: Saint Isidore of Pelusium (ca. 360–435 or 450), ascetic abbot and prolific theologian in Egypt, who in his *Epistles* (Book I, Letter 153) recorded the ancient patristic tradition identifying the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil in Paradise as a fig tree, whose leaves Adam and Eve used to sew aprons after the Fall.
[^61]: The celebrated Exapostilarion (Hymn of Light / *Свѣти́ленъ*) of Passion Week, *"Thy bridal chamber I behold adorned, O my Savior..."* (*Черто́гъ тво́й ви́ждꙋ, Спа́се мо́й*), chanted thrice by a soloist in the centre of the church while the congregation kneels, expressing the soul's yearning for the heavenly bridal banquet and its acute unworthiness.
[^62]: The proper dismissal formula for Great Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday: *"The Lord, going unto His voluntary Passion for our salvation, Christ our true God..."* (*Гряды́й Госпо́дь на во́льнꙋю Стра́сть, на́шегѡ ра́ди спасе́нїя, Христо́съ и҆́стинный Бо́гъ на́шъ...*).
[^63]: The continuous reading of the Four Gospels (*Четвероева́нгелїе*) during the Hours of Great Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday: The entirety of the four Evangelists (Matthew, Mark, and Luke complete; John read up to 13:31) is solemnly proclaimed from an adorned analogion positioned in the nave, completing the full Gospel witness before the commemorative enactment of the Lord's Passion.
[^64]: The Sixth Hour prophecy on Great Monday: Ezekiel 1:1–20, recounting the Prophet Ezekiel's vision of the divine chariot-throne (*Merkavah*) by the river Chebar, with the four winged living creatures (the Tetramorph) bearing the likenesses of a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle—patristically interpreted by Saint Irenaeus of Lyons and Saint Jerome as the four Evangelists.
[^65]: Codicological Note on the Passion Gospels Rubric: The text notes the liturgical distribution of the Four Gospels across the first three days of Great Week, preparing for the proclamation of the Twelve Passion Gospels at Great Thursday Matins.
