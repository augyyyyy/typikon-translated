And as for what pertains to the order and spiritual understanding of the prayers set forth in this book,

**[Right Page — Fol. 8r]**

I have judged good to write briefly, that there may be an answer unto heretics, and unto the Orthodox a warmer zeal for spiritual labors of prayer. According to the apostolic teaching, we are bound to pray without ceasing, as he says: *Rejoice evermore. Pray without ceasing. In every thing give thanks: for this is the will of God in Christ Jesus concerning you.* (1 Thess. 5:16–18)[^35] And to fulfill this is possible, even as Basil the Great teaches: Sometimes with the tongue, when this is possible, and especially necessary, and unto the edification of faith;[^36] but if not, then with the heart, praising God in psalms, and hymns, and spiritual songs, as it is written, singing and fulfilling prayer in the midst of work.[^37] Nevertheless, since it is commanded to pray without ceasing,

=== LEAF p12 ===

**[Left Page — Fol. 8v]**

it is not fitting to despise (as that same father said) the appointed times of prayer, which we have chosen by night, each having its own particular remembrance.[^38]

*At midnight* we begin our prayers, returning from sleep as from the darkness akin to death; and as having risen from the dead, we hymn God, preparing ourselves beforehand for the morning. And since the Midnight prayers are placed first, let no one marvel; for not without reason did the Church transmit that they should begin from thence, especially for two reasons.

The first, because the day has its beginning from midnight. Even if Chrysostom says that the day is counted from the evening according to the Hebrew tradition,[^39] yet Theophylact says that from the eighth hour of the night,

**[Right Page — Fol. 9r]**

and Saint Theodore Balsamon, Patriarch of Antioch, says that the day begins from the seventh hour of the night;[^40] which signifies nothing other than midnight. Outside philosophers also, such as Plutarch and Macrobius, testify that the day begins from midnight.[^41] And thus we do not begin the first prayers, called the Midnight Office, as from the night, but as from the light which God called day, as Moses says: *And God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night.* (Gen. 1:5)[^42] For God also created day first, as Basil in the *Hexaemeron* testifies thus: *And God said, Let there be light:* The first voice of God (he says) created the nature of light, drove away the darkness, and enlightened the world.[^43]

=== LEAF p13 ===

**[Left Page — Fol. 9v]**

And Athanasius [speaks] thus: In that God said before all things, *Let there be light*, it is manifest that the day precedes the night.[^44] And Damascene: Neither was night called first, but day; and on this account day was first, and night thereafter.[^45] And thus when we begin prayers from midnight, we consider that we have begun from the day, because midnight is the beginning of the day; and those first [prayers] we understand as proper. For it is nocturnal unto us at that time not to be weighed down with sleep, but as the day is beginning, it is meet also for our mind to be stirred toward its Creator, and equally with the sun for the mind to ascend: the one to enlighten the universe, and the eye to soar on high unto the Lord, that it may be deemed worthy of enlightenment,

**[Right Page — Fol. 10r]**

and for the adverse darkness to be driven away.

The second [reason]: Because toward midnight we are assured of the Second Coming of Christ and the general resurrection, even as the Gospel truth testifies: *At midnight,* it says, *there was a cry made, Behold, the bridegroom comes; go ye out to meet him.* (Mt. 25:6)[^46] *Behold, He comes* [signifies] the Coming; and *Go ye out* manifests our resurrection, even as the divine Chrysostom, interpreting these things, says: Either it remained in a parable, or again it shows that the resurrection takes place in the night.[^47] Furthermore, He Himself, the Firstborn from the dead, having risen at midnight, manifestly prefigured this; following whom the divine Fathers of the Sixth Ecumenical Council

**[Left Page — Fol. 10v]**

(Lk. 22:44)[^54] At midnight He was taken by the soldiers. (Jn. 18:12)[^55] At midnight He was led first unto Annas, and afterward unto Caiaphas; and whatsoever things He, the Sinless One, voluntarily endured there for the sake of our salvation.
