# PART I: ON DIVINE SERVICES IN GENERAL (Calendar, Church Books, and Liturgical Realia)

Divine services are celebrated according to feasts or according to ordinary church days from the church books, and consist of diverse parts: wherefore in this part we shall speak:

1) concerning the Menologion; 2) concerning the church books; and 3) concerning the technical terms by which the diverse psalms, hymns, and ecclesiastical chants are designated.

## CHAPTER I. § 2. On the Ecclesiastical Menologion in General

Just as in the Old Testament the people of Israel had a twofold year: (α) the civil year, which began with the month of Tishri in autumn; and (β) the ecclesiastical year, which began with the month of Nisan in spring: so also in the New Testament the Christian people have a twofold year: (α) the civil year, which begins on the first day of January; and (β) the ecclesiastical year, which begins on the 1st day of September.

The ecclesiastical year consists of: (α) Days dedicated to the worship of God, and specifically to the worship of the most important acts of God concerning the redemption of the human race, that is, Feasts of the Lord; (β) Days dedicated to the veneration of the Most Holy Virgin Mary, that is, Marian Feasts; (γ) Days dedicated to the veneration of the Saints, that is, Feasts of the Saints. But since every greater feast is already announced beforehand by holy Church, and holy Mother Church is occupied with it for a considerable time even after its celebration, therefore: (δ) the ecclesiastical year consists also of Forefeasts and Afterfeasts; (ε) Furthermore, it consists of days which holy Mother Church hath established for the repentance and sanctification of the human race, that is, fasting days; (ζ) And finally, it consists of days on which mankind entreateth God for those who have “fallen asleep in faith and in the hope of resurrection,” that is, days for the departed.

The focal point of the entire ecclesiastical year is the Resurrection of Christ, even as the Resurrection of Christ is the seal of the redemption of the human race. “And if Christ be not risen,” saith the Apostle, “then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain” (1 Cor. 15:14). And therefore His memorial is celebrated every week on Sunday, and all other feasts receive their brightness and warmth from this feast; in a word: what the sun is among the stars, that the Feast of Pascha is among the other feasts.

The sequential order, that is, the arrangement of the church year according to diverse feasts, is called the “Menologion” (*Misiatsoslov*), in Greek: “*Heortologion*” [Ἑορтоλόγιον].

Furthermore, feasts are variously divided,[^5] namely:

1. In regard to time (*ratione temporis*): (α) into those which do not fall in the Menologion at the same time or on that same day, but their celebration dependeth upon Pascha, which falleth on one of the Sundays between the 22nd day of March and the 25th day of April, and therefore these are called movable feasts; (β) into those which fall in each year in the Menologion on that same day and on that same date of the month, and therefore are called immovable feasts.
2. In regard to exterior sanctification (*ratione exterioris sanctificationis*), they are divided: (α) into holy days of obligation (*festa fori*), on which the pastor is bound to celebrate the Divine Liturgy for the people (*praeceptum missam pro populo applicare*), and the people are bound to hear the Divine Liturgy (*praeceptum missam audire*); (β) into feast days of devotion (*festa chori*), on which the pastor is bound to celebrate the Divine Liturgy for the people, but the people nevertheless are not bound to hear the Divine Liturgy; (γ) into ordinary days, on which neither the pastor is bound to celebrate the Divine Liturgy for the people, nor are the people bound to hear the Divine Liturgy.
3. In regard to the object celebrated (*ratione objecti celebrati*), they are divided: (α) into Feasts of the Lord (*cultus latriae* [worship of adoration]); (β) into Marian Feasts (*cultus hyperdouliae* [veneration of highest honour]); and (γ) into Feasts of the Saints (*cultus douliae* [veneration of honour]).
4. In regard to the liturgical rite (*ratione ritus liturgici*), into: (α) great; (β) middle; and (γ) lesser feasts. — Great feasts have Great Vespers with 10 or 8 stichera with an entrance, and sometimes with a Litiya; Matins with a Polyeleos, Gospel, and the Great Doxology. Middle feasts have Vespers with 8 stichera with an entrance, but without a Litiya; and Matins with a Polyeleos, Gospel, and the Great Doxology. Lesser feasts have Vespers with 6 stichera, but without an entrance; at Matins there is no Polyeleos, the canon is on 6, but they have the Great Doxology, and therefore are called “doxology feasts” (*doxologoumena* [δοξоλογούμενα]).

Besides feast days there are also non-festal days, on which we celebrate the memory of lesser Saints having 3 stichera at Vespers and a canon on 4 at Matins.

## § 3. On the Menologion of Movable Feasts

Pascha is the focal point of the movable feasts. It can fall from March 22 unto April 25, that is, on one of these 35 days. — The “Paschalia”[^6] is the unfailing index of the day of the Pascha of Christ and of all festal days which depend upon Pascha. — In the Paschalia, the greatest importance is held by the boundary key (*kliuch hranychnyi*) or boundary letter. The boundary keys are letters of the alphabet which show on which day and month, in what year, the Pascha of Christ will fall. — Thus the earliest day of Pascha, March 22, hath the key A, March 23 hath B, and so forth in order. The last and latest day of Pascha, April 25, hath the key Ѿ (*Ot*).

Together with the boundary key there is also found the Dominical letter (*vrutsilito*, or Dominical letters, Sunday letter), and they are seven in number. These are alphabetical letters taken as numbers to designate the 7 days of the week: A, V, H, D, E, S, Z [А, В, Г, Д, Е, Ѕ, З].

The boundary key beginneth with the Nativity of Christ and governeth throughout the entire year. And therefore, if we wish to know when in any given year the movable seasons fall, it is necessary to know beforehand what the boundary key is in that year; and knowing the key, everything that we wish to know will be unlocked by that key, if we only look in the tables at what standeth opposite the governing key. The Dominical letter (*vrutsilito*), however, doth not begin together with the boundary key; it beginneth with the 1st day of March and governeth unto the last day of February of the following year. — Therefore, the Sunday days in January and February of that second year stand in the Menologia always under the Dominical letter of the preceding year. The Menologion of movable feasts consists of: 1) the Forefeast of Pascha itself; 2) the Feast of Pascha; 3) the Afterfeast of Pascha; and 4) the 32 weeks after the Descent of the Holy Spirit, which are called “ordinary.”

I. To the Forefeast of Pascha belong: (α) The Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee, with the following week called “fast-free” (*pospolytsia*, *zahalnytsia*, *hyrtsia*), that is, when there is no fasting either on Wednesday or on Friday; (β) The Sunday of the Prodigal Son; (γ) Meatfare Saturday, which is a Saturday for the departed; (δ) Meatfare Sunday (or Sunday of the Second Coming of Jesus Christ); (ε) Cheesefare Saturday, on which is celebrated the memory of all our Venerable and God-bearing Fathers who shone forth in asceticism (*in ascesi*). On that day is celebrated the canon of St. Theodore the Studite, in which Odes 1–6 glorify those holy Fathers who were ascetics, Ode 7 the holy Women, and Odes 8 and 9 the holy Pastors of the Church; (ϛ) Cheesefare Sunday, or the Sunday of the Expulsion of the First-Created Adam from the Paradise of Delight; (η) The First Saturday of the Forty Days (*Chetyredesiatnytsia*), when we celebrate the memory of the miracle of the kolyva wrought by the holy Great-Martyr Theodore the Tyro, who died in the year 306. This miracle occurred in the year 362 in Constantinople, in the time of Julian the Apostate, who wished to turn all Christians back unto paganism, and that in such wise that he commanded that all provisions found in the marketplace should be secretly sprinkled with the blood of pagan sacrifices. But St. Theodore appeared by night unto Eudoxius, Bishop of this city, and told him that he should forbid them to buy that, and commanded that wheat, that is, grain boiled in pots, called “*kollyva*” [κολλύβα], should be given unto the needy; (ϑ) The First Sunday of the Fast, when is also celebrated the memory of the triumph of Orthodoxy over the Iconoclasts, wherefore it is also called the “Sunday of Orthodoxy”; (ι) The Second Saturday of the Forty Days, and for the departed; (κ) The Second Sunday of the Holy Great Fast. The Synod of Zamość (in the year 1720) forbade the celebration on that day of the memory of Gregory Palamas, the heretic[^7]; (λ) The Third Saturday of the Holy Fast, for the departed; (μ) The Third Sunday of the Forty Days, which is called “Veneration of the Precious Cross” (*kyriake tes stauroproskyneseos* [κυριακὴ τῆς σταυροпροσκυνήσεως]); (ν) The Fourth Saturday of the Holy Fast, for the departed; (ξ) The Fourth Sunday of the Fast, in which we celebrate the memory of our Venerable Father John of the Ladder; (ο) The Fifth Thursday of the Holy Forty Days, on which day at Matins (with us, on the evening of the 7th hour) the Great Canon of St. Andrew of Crete is sung; (ρ) The Fifth Saturday of the Holy Fast, with the Akathist to the Most Holy Virgin; (π) The Fifth Sunday of the Holy Fast, in which is celebrated the memory of our Venerable Mother Mary of Egypt; (σ) The Sixth Saturday...

...of the Holy Fast, or of Lazarus; (τ) The Sixth Sunday of the Holy Fast, or Palm Sunday (*“kyriake ton baion”* [κυριακὴ τῶν βαΐων] or *“heorte baiophoros”* [ἑορτὴ βαϊοφόρος], *festum Palmarum*, from the Greek word *“baia”* [βαΐα]—boughs of palm, olive, or willow); (υ) Great Week, with Great Thursday, Friday, and Saturday.

II. The Sunday of Pascha (from the Hebrew word *“pesach”* = *transitus* [passing over]), with the canon of St. John Damascene. Among Christians there is a twofold Pascha: (α) Pascha *“staurosimon”* [σταυρώσιμον, of the Cross], when Jesus Christ passed over from life unto death, that is, Great Friday; and (β) Pascha *“anastasimon”* [ἀναστάσιμον, of the Resurrection], when He passed over from death unto life, that is, the Bright Resurrection. This entire week is called Bright, but at the same time it is fast-free (*pospolyta*).

III. To the Afterfeast of Pascha belong: (α) The First Sunday, that is, Thomas Sunday or the Sunday of Antipascha (*anti pascha* [ἀντὶ πάσχα]), that is, in place of Pascha, because on that Sunday the Feast of Pascha is repeated for the first time. For that reason that Sunday is also called the “Sunday of Renewal,” or the “New Sunday”; (β) The Second Sunday, that is, of the holy Myrrh-Bearing Women and of holy Joseph of Arimathea with Nicodemus; (γ) The Third Sunday, that is, of the Paralytic; (δ) The Wednesday of Mid-Pentecost (that is, the 25th day from Easter Sunday, in Greek: *“he mesopentekoste”* [ἡ μεσοπεντηκοστή]). This feast is observed for 8 days; (ε) The Fourth Sunday, that is, of the Samaritan Woman; (ϛ) The Fifth Sunday, that is, of the Blind Man; (η) The Wednesday of the sixth week, on which is performed the Apodosis of Pascha; (ϑ) The Thursday of the sixth week after Pascha, that is, the Ascension of Jesus Christ, with the canon of St. John Damascene; (ι) The Sixth Sunday, that is, of the Holy and God-bearing Fathers of the First Ecumenical Council in Nicaea; (κ) The Saturday before the Descent of the Holy Spirit, which is for the departed; (λ) The Sunday of Pentecost, that is, the Descent of the Holy Spirit, with the canon of St. Cosmas. After the Divine Liturgy, at Vespers the kneeling prayers are concluded; (μ) The Monday of the Holy Spirit and the Feast of the Holy Trinity. The service of the Feast of Pentecost is celebrated throughout the entire following week, which is fast-free (*pospolyta*); and on Saturday the Apodosis of that feast takes place.

IV. There are 32 Sundays after the Descent of the Holy Spirit,[^8] of which: (α) The first is the Sunday of All Saints. On this Sunday ...the *Octoechos* beginneth with Tone 8 and the fast unto the Feast of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul, which, however, is not always of equal length, for it can last at the least 7 days, and at the most 6 weeks; (β) The Thursday of the second week is in Galicia the Feast of the Holy Eucharist; (γ) The Second Sunday after the Descent of the Holy Spirit: the Gospel for this day is “On the manifestation of Jesus Christ and the calling unto Apostleship of Peter, Andrew, James, and John”; (δ) The Friday of the third week is in Galicia the Feast of the “Compassion of the Most Holy Virgin at the Passion of Jesus Christ”; (ε) In July, on a Sunday near the 16th day of that month, we celebrate the “Memory of the Holy Fathers of the Six Ecumenical Councils,” and that in such wise: if the 16th day falleth on a Monday, Tuesday, or Wednesday, then the “Council of the Holy Fathers” is celebrated on the preceding Sunday; but if on a Thursday, Friday, or Saturday, then on the following Sunday; finally, if the 16th day falleth on a Sunday, then we celebrate the memory of the holy Fathers on that very same Sunday; (ϛ) In September, around the 14th day, are celebrated the Sunday before the Exaltation of the Precious Cross and the Sunday after the Exaltation of the Precious Cross; (η) In October, around the 11th day, is celebrated: the “Sunday of the Holy Fathers of the Seventh Ecumenical Council held in Nicaea in the year 787”;[^9] (ϑ) In December, on the penultimate Sunday before the Nativity of Christ, is celebrated the “Memory of the Holy Forefathers” (*Dominica Protopatrum*). By “Forefathers” we understand: Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. (Some understand Adam and Eve, but erroneously); (ι) The last Sunday before the Nativity of Christ is called the “Sunday of the Holy Fathers of the Old Testament” (*Dominica SS. Patrum*). By “Holy Fathers” we understand here all the God-fearing men of the Old Testament, beginning ...from Adam even unto holy Joseph, the Betrothed of Mary; (κ) On the first Sunday after the Nativity of Jesus Christ we celebrate the memory of holy Joseph, the Betrothed of Mary, David the King, and James, the Brother of the Lord according to the flesh; (λ) In January we celebrate the Sunday before Theophany and after Theophany. Mention is also made of the “Sunday of the Holy Guardian Angels” and of the “Sunday of the Holy Fathers of the Council of Florence,” but the day of these feasts is not appointed; (μ) The 32nd Sunday after the Descent of the Holy Spirit is “of Zacchaeus.”[^10]

## § 4. On the Menologion of Immovable Feasts

Of the immovable feasts we shall adduce only the most important. The focal point among these feasts is the Nativity according to the flesh of our Lord Jesus Christ, with which many of the immovable feasts stand in close connection. The Menologion of immovable feasts beginneth with the 1st day of September and endeth with the 31st day of August.

I. In September the most notable of the immovable feasts are: (α) On the 1st: The Beginning of the Indiction, that is, of the New Year. The name “Indiction” is the Latin word *indictio*, *initium et finis* [beginning and end]. It is a period consisting of 15 years, which period was divided into three parts (*lustrum*), after which the Roman Emperors decreed a tax upon their subjects in the first days of each five-year period, and collected the same in the first days of each five-year period. The Church beginneth the new year on this day either because on this day Jesus came into the Jewish synagogue and there began to preach, and having taken the book of the Prophet Isaiah, read from it that place where it is written: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me” (Isa. 61:1, *Initium indictionis ab initio ...praedicationis Jesu* [The beginning of the Indiction from the beginning of the preaching of Jesus]). Others, however, consider that the Church beginneth the new year on that day because, according to tradition, time began on that day (*Initium indictionis a principio temporis* [The beginning of the Indiction from the beginning of time]); (β) On the 6th: “Commemoration of the Miracle Wrought at Colossae by the Archistrategos Michael.” This miracle was that the pagans wished to destroy the church of the holy Archistrategos Michael by means of a river which they directed against the church; but the holy Archangel Michael appeared, and striking the rock with his staff, made a passage for the river, which even now floweth through this rock; (γ) On the 8th: The Nativity of the Most Holy Theotokos; (δ) On the 14th: The Exaltation of the Precious Cross. On the 16th: St. Josaphat; (ε) On the 26th: The Falling Asleep of the Holy Apostle and Evangelist John the Theologian; (ϛ) On the 28th: Our Venerable Father Chariton.

II. In the month of October the most notable feasts are the following: (α) On the 1st: The “Protection of the Most Holy Theotokos,” instituted in memory of how St. Andrew the Fool-for-Christ and his disciple Epiphanius, during the All-Night Vigil on October 1 in the year 1309, beheld in the air in Constantinople, in the Church of Blachernae, the Mother of God with Angels, Prophets, and Apostles, praying for the world and covering Christians with her veil. At this time the Saracens had attacked the Greeks and caused them great harm; but when the Greeks learned of this vision, they took courage and after a short time drove away the Saracens;[^11] (β) On the 6th: The holy and glorious Apostle Thomas; (γ) On the 9th: The holy Apostle James, son of Alphaeus; (δ) On the 18th: The holy Evangelist Luke; (ε) On the 23rd: The holy Apostle James, Brother of the Lord according to the flesh; (ϛ) On the 26th: The holy Great-Martyr Demetrius the Myrrh-Gusher. The Myrrh-Gusher (in Greek: *“Myroblytos”* [Μυροβλύτης] = *unguentum effundens* [pouring forth ointment], from the words *“myron”* [μύρον] = *unguentum* and *“blyo”* [βλύω] = *scaturio* [to gush forth]) is so called because from his relics flowed myrrh, that is, oil which was a medicine in certain sicknesses; (η) On the 28th: The holy Martyr Paraskeva, a Ruthenian nun and abbess, who, having left her homeland because of the Tatars, moved to Rome and died there in the year 1239, and was inscribed into the number of the Saints by Gregory X.

III. In the month of November the most notable feast days are the following: (α) On the 1st: The holy Wonderworkers and Unmercenaries Cosmas and Damian. These Saints were physicians who miraculously and not for money, but gratuitously healed people (*“Anargyros”* [Ἀνάργυρος] = unmercenary, *argento carens*, *mercedem non recipiens* [lacking silver, receiving no fee]); (β) On the 8th: The Synaxis of the Holy Archangel Michael, Guardian of Holy Mother Church ...and of the other Bodiless Powers (Synaxis = *“Synaxis”* [Σύναξις], *conventus sacer fidelium solemnitatis causa* [a holy gathering of the faithful for the purpose of a solemnity]); (γ) On the 13th: Our Holy Father John Chrysostom; (δ) On the 14th: The Holy Apostle Philip. From this day beginneth the fast unto the Nativity of Jesus Christ; (ε) On the 16th: The Holy Apostle Matthew; (ϛ) On the 21st: The Entry (*Praesentatio*) into the Temple of the All-Pure Virgin; (η) On the 30th: The Holy Apostle Andrew the First-Called (*“Protokletos”* [Πρωτόκλητος] = first-called).

IV. In the month of December the most notable feasts are the following: (α) On the 4th: The Holy Great-Martyr Barbara; (β) On the 5th: St. Sabbas; (γ) On the 6th: St. Nicholas, Bishop of Myra in Lycia; (δ) On the 9th: The Immaculate Conception (*Immaculata Conceptio*) of the Most Holy Virgin Mary; (ε) On the 13th: The Holy Martyrs Eustratios, Auxentios, Eugenios, Mardarios, and Orestes; (ϛ) On the 25th: The Nativity of Jesus Christ; (η) On the 26th: The Synaxis of the Most Holy Virgin; (ϑ) On the 27th: St. Stephen, the First Martyr and Archdeacon.

V. In January the most notable feasts are: (α) On the 1st: The Circumcision of Jesus Christ (*Circumcisio Domini*) and St. Basil the Great; (β) On the 6th: Theophany (*Theophania*); (γ) On the 7th: The Synaxis of St. John the Baptist; (δ) On the 11th: St. Theodosius, the Initiator of Common Life (*Coenobiarcha*); (ε) On the 16th: The Veneration of the Precious Chains (*catena*) of the Holy Apostle Peter; (ϛ) On the 17th: St. Anthony the Great (*Eremita*); (η) On the 20th: St. Euthymius the Great; (ϑ) On the 25th: St. Gregory the Theologian; (ι) On the 27th: The Translation of the Relics of St. John Chrysostom; (κ) On the 30th: The Three Holy Hierarchs: St. Basil the Great, St. Gregory the Theologian, and St. John Chrysostom.

VI. In February the most notable feasts are: (α) On the 2nd: The Meeting (*Occursus*) of our Lord Jesus Christ with Simeon, at the same time both the Presentation of Jesus in the Temple (*Praesentatio Domini*) and the Purification of the Blessed Virgin Mary (*Purificatio Beatae Mariae Virginis*); (β) On the 24th: The First (in Jerusalem) and Second (in Emesa) Finding of the Head of St. John the Baptist.

VII. In March these are the most notable feasts: (α) On the 9th: The 40 Martyrs; (β) On the 25th: The Annunciation (*Annuntiatio B. M. Virginis*), at the same time also the Feast of the Incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ (*Conceptio D. N. J. Christi*).

VIII. In April the most notable feasts are: (α) On the 23rd: St. George the Great-Martyr; (β) On the 25th: St. Mark the Evangelist; (γ) On the 30th: The Holy Apostle James, brother of St. John.

IX. In May the most notable feasts are: (α) On the 3rd: The Falling Asleep of St. Theodosius of the Caves; (β) On the 8th: St. John the Evangelist;

(γ) On the 9th: The Translation of the Relics of St. Nicholas from Myra to the city of Bari; (δ) On the 10th: The Holy Apostle Simon; (ε) On the 21st: St. Constantine and St. Helen; (ϛ) On the 25th: The Third Finding of the Head of St. John the Baptist (*Comanae in Cappadocia*).

X. In June the most notable feasts are: (α) On the 11th: The Holy Apostle Bartholomew; (β) On the 19th: The Holy Apostle Jude, Thaddaeus; (γ) On the 24th: The Nativity of St. John the Baptist; (δ) On the 29th: The Holy Apostles Peter and Paul; (ε) On the 30th: The Synaxis of the Holy 12 Apostles.

XI. In July the most notable feasts are: (α) On the 2nd: The Deposition of the Precious Robe of our Most Holy Lady the Theotokos in Blachernae (*Depositio vel translatio pretiosae vestis B. M. Virginis in Blachernis*); (β) On the 5th: Our Venerable Father St. Athanasius of Athos; (γ) On the 10th: St. Anthony of the Caves; on the 15th: St. Vladimir; (δ) On the 20th: The Holy Prophet Elijah; on the 24th: St. Boris and St. Gleb; (ε) On the 25th: The Dormition of St. Anna; (ϛ) On the 27th: St. Panteleimon.

XII. In August: (α) On the 1st: The Procession of the Precious Wood of the Precious and Life-Giving Cross, and the memory of the Holy 7 Maccabean Martyrs. With this day beginneth the fast unto the Dormition of the Most Holy Virgin; (β) On the 6th: The Transfiguration (*Transfiguratio*) of our Lord Jesus Christ; (γ) On the 8th: St. Stephen, King of Hungary; (δ) On the 9th: The Holy Apostle Matthias; (ε) On the 15th: The Dormition of our All-Pure Lady the Theotokos (*Obdormitio et Assumptio B. M. Virginis*); (ϛ) On the 16th: The Translation from Edessa to Constantinople of the Icon Made without Hands (*divinitus fabricata imago*) of our Lord Jesus Christ; (η) On the 29th: The Beheading (*Decollatio*) of the Head of St. John the Baptist; (ϑ) On the 31st: The Deposition of the Precious Sash of the Most Holy Theotokos (*Depositio Zonae S.Smae Deiparae*).

## § 5. Which Are the Feasts of the Lord?

The Feasts of the Lord are the following: 1. The Conception, that is, the Incarnation of our Lord Jesus Christ.[^12] 2. The Nativity of Jesus Christ. 3. The Circumcision of Jesus Christ. 4. The Presentation in the Temple or Meeting (*“Hypapante”* [Ὑπαπαντή] = *occursus*) of our Lord Jesus Christ with Simeon. 5. The Baptism of Jesus Christ. 6. The Transfiguration of Jesus Christ. 7. The Entry of Jesus Christ into Jerusalem (*Dies Palmarum*). 8. The Death of Jesus Christ, that is, the Pascha of Death. 9. The Resurrection of Jesus Christ, that is, the Joyful Pascha. 10. The Ascension of Jesus Christ. 11. The Descent of the Holy Spirit. 12. The Monday of the Holy Spirit, when at the same time the Feast of the Holy Trinity is also celebrated. 13. The Procession of the Precious Wood of the Precious and Life-Giving Cross. 14. The Exaltation of the Precious Cross. 15. The Translation from Edessa to the City of Constantine of the Icon Made without Hands of Jesus Christ.

## § 6. Which Are the Marian Feasts?

The Marian Feasts are the following: 1. The Immaculate Conception of the Most Holy Virgin. 2. The Nativity of the Most Holy Virgin. 3. The Entry into the Temple of the Most Holy Virgin. 4. The Annunciation of the Most Holy Virgin. 5. The Purification of the Most Holy Virgin.[^13] 6. The Synaxis of the Most Holy Virgin. 7. The Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos. 8. The Protection of the Most Holy Virgin. 9. The Deposition of the Precious Robe of the Most Holy Virgin. 10. The Deposition of the Precious Sash of the Most Holy Virgin.

Note: The feasts of the Annunciation and the Meeting are of a twofold nature: they are both Feasts of the Lord and Marian Feasts; nevertheless, the Annunciation is reckoned among the Marian Feasts, and the Meeting among the Feasts of the Lord.

## § 7. Which Are the Most Notable Feasts of the Saints?

Among the Saints are holy men and holy women. (α) Among men are the following choirs: Angels, Prophets, Apostles, Doctors, Hierarchs, Martyrs, Hieromartyrs, Venerables, Venerable-Martyrs, Confessors, Hieroconfessors, Venerable-Confessors. — (β) Among women: Women Martyrs, Venerable Women and Venerable-Women-Martyrs, Venerable and Righteous Virgins.

Among their feasts the most notable are the following: 1. The Synaxis of the Holy Archangel Michael and of the other Bodiless Powers. 2. The feasts of St. John the Baptist, namely: Conception, Nativity, Beheading, Synaxis, First, Second, and Third Finding of the Head of the Honorable John the Baptist. 3. The Holy Prophet Elijah. 4. The feasts of the Holy Apostles, especially the feasts of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul.

5. The feasts of the Great Hierarchs, especially St. Nicholas and the Three Hierarchs. 6. The Holy Martyrs: Stephen, Demetrius, and George. 7. The feasts of our Venerable Fathers, especially St. Anthony and St. Theodosius. 8. The Holy Unmercenaries: Cosmas and Damian. 9. The feasts of holy women: St. Anna, St. Helen, St. Paraskeva, etc.

The Synaxis of St. Michael. 5. The Entry into the Temple of the Most Holy Virgin. 6. The Day of St. Nicholas. 7. The Nativity of Jesus Christ. 8. The Synaxis of the Most Holy Theotokos. 9. The Feast of St. Stephen the Archdeacon. 10. The Circumcision. 11. Theophany. 12. The Three Hierarchs. 13. The Meeting. 14. The Annunciation. 15. Holy and Great Friday. 16. The Sunday, Monday, and Tuesday of Pascha. 17. St. George. 18. The Ascension of Jesus Christ. 19. The Sunday and Monday of the Descent of the Holy Spirit. 20. The Feast of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul. 21. The Nativity of St. John the Baptist. 22. The Feast of the Holy Prophet Elijah. 23. The Transfiguration of Jesus Christ. 24. The Feast of St. Stephen, King of Hungary. 25. The Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos. 26. The Beheading of the Honorable Head of John the Baptist.[^14]

## § 9. Which Are the Choral or Church Feasts (Festa Chori)?

According to the direction[^15] of the late Bishop of the city of Nagyvárad [Veliko-Varadin], Papp-Szilágyi, the following were at one time church feasts: 1. ## § 10. Which Are the Great Feasts?

Great feasts are divided into 3 classes: (α) To the first belongeth only Pascha, which is the “feast of feasts”; (β) To the second belong the 12 feasts: Nativity of the Theotokos, Entry, Annunciation, Dormition of the Most Holy Virgin, Nativity of Jesus Christ, Meeting, Theophany, Transfiguration, Entry into Jerusalem, Ascension, Descent of the Holy Spirit, and Exaltation of the Precious Cross; (γ) To the third class belong: The Circumcision of the Lord, Nativity and Beheading of John the Baptist, and the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul.

Great feasts are designated in the Menologion by an enclosed cross 🞣, and the titles are usually printed in red letters. They are Feasts of the Lord and Marian Feasts. All these feasts have Great Vespers with a Litiya, and Matins with a Polyeleos, Gospel, and Great Doxology; besides this they have a Forefeast and Afterfeast, that is, the divine service of those feasts is not limited to the very day of the feast, but extends unto preceding and following days. For the most part, great feasts have one day of Forefeast each; only the Feast of the Nativity of Christ hath 5 days, and the Feast of Theophany 4 days. The Circumcision and the other feasts of the 3rd class do not have a Forefeast.

The Afterfeast likewise is not always the same, but is at times longer, at times shorter. — Thus the Afterfeast consists of one day after the Annunciation of the Most Holy Theotokos; of four days after the Nativity of the Theotokos and the Entry into the Temple of the Theotokos; or of 6 days after the Nativity of Christ; or of 7 days after the Exaltation of the Precious Cross and after the Transfiguration of the Lord; or of 8 days after Theophany and after the Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos. The Feast of the Circumcision and the other feasts of the 3rd class do not have an Afterfeast.

The last day of an Afterfeast is called the “Apodosis of the feast,” because on that day the celebration of the feast itself is for the most part taken. All Apodoses are “doxology” feasts.

At Matins they have a Polyeleos, Gospel, a canon on 8, and the Great Doxology.

## § 13. What Must Be Noted Concerning Sunday, Weekday, Fasting, Fast-Free, and Requiem Days?

Sunday is dedicated to the Resurrection of Christ; of all Sundays there are 52. They are reckoned either after the Resurrection, 1st, 2nd, etc., or after the Descent of the Holy Spirit, 1st, 2nd, etc., or 1st, 2nd, etc., of the Fast.

Monday is dedicated to the Angels; Tuesday to St. John the Baptist; Wednesday and Friday to the Precious Cross; Thursday to the Apostles and St. Nicholas; Saturday to All Saints and the Departed.

On Wednesday and on Friday there is a fast; on Saturday and Sunday there is no fast even in the Great Fast itself, but only abstinence, with the exception of Great Saturday.[^29] For thus saith Apostolic Canon 66: *“Si quis clericorum deprehendatur die Dominico jejunans aut Sabbato, praeter unum solum (nempe Sabbatum Sanctum) deponatur, si vero laicus segregetur”* [“If any cleric be found fasting on the Lord’s day or on the Sabbath, except only one (namely Holy Saturday), let him be deposed, but if a layman, let him be segregated”].[^30] For Saturday is the day of creation, but Sunday is the day of redemption.

Fasts are: 1. Before the Nativity of Jesus Christ, from November 15 unto December 24 inclusive; 2. Before the Resurrection of Christ, from Cheesefare Sunday unto the Resurrection; 3. Of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul, from the Sunday of All Saints unto the feast of Peter and Paul; 4. Before the Dormition of the Most Holy Virgin Theotokos, from the 1st day of August unto the 14th; 5. On the day of the Exaltation of the Precious Cross; 6. On the day of the Beheading of the Honorable Head of St. John the Baptist.

Free or fast-free (*pospolyta*) days are: 1. The week after the Nativity of Christ unto Theophany; 2. The week of the Publican and the Pharisee; 3. Bright Week; 4. The week after the Descent of the Holy Spirit.[^31]

Requiem days: 1. The Saturday of Meatfare Sunday; 2. The Saturday before the Descent of the Holy Spirit (these days are *per excellentiam* All Souls’ Saturdays [*zadushni suboty*]); 3. The 2nd, 3rd, and 4th Saturdays of the Great Fast.

# CHAPTER II

## § 14. On Church Books in General

Church books are either public or private, that is, intended either for public or for private use. — Public books are those which are approved for public use by the Church herself; private books are smaller books taken from one or more public books for the benefit of the clergy or the faithful, and they are approved only by one or another bishop.

It is not permitted to use unapproved books or books approved by schismatics in church; so commandeth Benedict XIV of blessed memory, who in his constitution *“Etsi pastoralis”* speaketh thus: *“Quia in ecclesiasticis Graecorum libris nonnulli errores irrepsere, quibus primaevam Ecclesiae Graecae puritatem fraude potissimum ac dolo schismaticorum corruptam esse orthodoxi dolent, Ordinariorum locorum vigilantiam excitamus, ut nonnisi probatorum librorum usum admittant”* [“Because into the ecclesiastical books of the Greeks several errors have crept, whereby the orthodox grieve that the pristine purity of the Greek Church hath been corrupted chiefly by the fraud and deceit of schismatics, We arouse the vigilance of the local Ordinaries, that they admit the use of none but approved books”].[^32]

## § 15. Which of the Church Books Are Public?

1. The Horologion (in Greek: *Horologion* [Ὡроλόγιον], from the word: *hora* [ὥра] = *hora* [hour]), which is so called because the services contained therein had to be completed daily according to appointed hours (*per horas*), according to the words of the Psalmist: “Seven times a day have I praised Thee for the judgements of Thy righteousness” (Ps. 118:164 [LXX]). It containeth in itself: (α) Morning prayers for lay people; (β) The service of the Midnight Office, daily, Saturday, and Sunday; (γ) The Order of Matins, both Sunday and daily; (δ) The Hours with the Typika [*Obidnytsia*]; (ε) The Order of Vespers with Litiya; (ϛ) The service of Compline, small and great, with the Paraklesis to the Most Holy Virgin; (η) Various services, both Sunday and for the departed, prayers before Communion and after Communion, besides this also prayers for those going to sleep;

(ϑ) Sunday Troparia with Theotokia, Kontakia and Hypakoai, Prokeimena and Communion Hymns of the eight Sunday tones; likewise the daily Troparia with Kontakia and other chants of the entire week; (ι) Dismissal Theotokia in 8 tones and for all days of the week, sung after the Troparia of the Saints throughout the whole year at Vespers and at Matins at “The Lord is God” and again at the conclusion; (κ) The Menologion for the entire year according to movable[^33] and immovable feasts, in which the Troparion, Kontakion, Prokeimenon, and Communion Hymn of the feast or of the Saint, and at great feasts also the Antiphons with the Zadostoiniki [Hymns in place of *It is truly meet*], are found; (λ) Finally, there are the Indiction, Paschalion, and other rubrical directions.

2. The Psalter (in Greek: *Psalterion* [Ψαλτήριον], from the word: *psallein* [ψάλλειν] = *cantare comitante musica* [to sing to musical accompaniment]), which containeth in itself: (α) The 150 Psalms of the Old Testament. These Psalms are divided into 20 kathismata, each kathisma containing 3 antiphons [staseis], and after the antiphons are Troparia and prayers; (β) The Nine Canticles of Moses; (γ) The Exaltations [Velichaniya] sung on Feasts of the Lord, Marian Feasts, and for Great Saints,[^34] to which are also added general refrains [*prypivy*] to various Saints, and then Bulgarian refrains prescribed for movable Feasts of the Lord, Marian Feasts, and immovable Great Saints; (δ) And the canon for the departure of the soul. Besides this there is also a direction how the Psalter is to be read throughout the entire year.

3. The Triodion (in Greek: *Triodion* [Τριῴδιον], from the words: *treis ode* [τρεῖς ᾠδή] = *tres odae*, *liber trium odarum* [three odes, book of three odes]). It is called “Triodion” because the canon of daily Matins in the Great Fast for the most part consisteth not of 9, but only of three odes. Besides this, on a certain day the canon hath only two odes, and such is called a “Diodion” [*Dvopesnets*], and yet on another four, and such is called a “Tetraodion” [*Chetyrepesnets*].[^35] The Triodion containeth in itself services both Sunday and weekday, namely from the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee even unto Great Saturday; certain newer Ruthenian editions, however, have it only up to Lazarus Saturday. For Sunday services it hath: (α) Vespers stichera; (β) At Matins the stichera sung after Psalm 50 in place of “Having beheld the Resurrection of Christ,” namely at “Glory”: “Open unto me the doors of repentance,” at “Now and ever”: “Guide me into the paths of salvation”; (γ) It hath one ca- non.[^36] (δ) The exaposteilarion sung after the 9th ode. (ε) Two stichera at the Praises and at “Glory.” For weekday services it hath: (α) At Vespers three stichera and two readings, and stichera at the aposticha. (β) Small Matins with three sessional hymns and with three odes of the canon. (γ) The Triadicon exaposteilarion with “Glory,” which is read only after the 9th ode. (δ) At the end it hath a small Typikon. It must be noted that this book in the Greek-Ruthenian Church is also called the “Lenten Triodion.”

4. The Pentecostarion, that is, the book (in Greek: *Pentecostarion* [Πεντηκοστάριον], from the word: *pentekonta* [πεντήκοντα] = *quinquaginta* [fifty]). It containeth in itself all services from the Sunday of Pascha (in Ruthenian editions from Lazarus Saturday) unto the Sunday of All Saints. And that: (α) Services of Passion Week; (β) Services of Bright Week; (γ) Services from Thomas Sunday [Antipascha] unto the Sunday of All Saints. For Sunday divine services: Vespers with Sunday stichera and with stichera at the aposticha; Matins with sessional hymns, the Sunday canon with an exaposteilarion, and stichera at the Praises. For daily divine services: regular Vespers on 3 stichera, Matins with two kathismata and sessional hymns, the Sunday canon, and stichera at the aposticha.

Note: It is necessary to note that because in Galicia the services of Passion Week have been taken from the Triodion and placed beforehand in the Pentecostarion, therefore both books are collectively called the “Triodion,” dividing them into the (α) Lenten and (β) Flowery Triodion.

5. The Octoechos (in Greek: *Octoechos* [Ὀκτώηχος], from the words: *octo* [ὀκτώ] and *echos* [ἦχος] = *octo* et *sonus* [eight and tone/sound]). It beginneth on the Sunday of All Saints in the evening and is used alone until the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee, and together with the Triodion until Lazarus Saturday. It containeth in itself: Sunday and weekday services according to the 8 tones, and that in each tone: (α) The Sunday service, namely: stichera of Small and Great Vespers, both at “Lord, I have cried” and at the aposticha; furthermore, the Theotokion canon of Compline, the Trinity canon of the Midnight Office, sessional hymns, the hypakoe, the morning graduals [stepenny], a threefold Matins canon: of the Resurrection, Cross-Resurrection, and Theotokion, Sunday stichera at the Praises, and the Beatitudes at the Liturgy; (β) The Monday service: Vespers with stichera, the Theotokion canon of Compline, Matins with 3 sessional hymns and with two canons, namely: of Repentance and to the Angels, stichera at the aposticha, and the Beatitudes; (γ) The Tuesday service: It containeth in itself the stichera of Vespers, the Theotokion canon of Compline, and Matins with sessional hymns and with two canons, namely: of Repentance and to the Forerunner; (δ) The Wednesday service: It containeth in itself: evening stichera, the Theotokion canon of Compline, and Matins with sessional hymns and with two canons, namely: of the Theotokos and of the Cross; (ε) The Thursday service: It containeth in itself: evening stichera, the Theotokion canon of Compline, and Matins with sessional hymns and with two canons: to the Holy Apostles and to St. Nicholas; (ϛ) The Friday service, that is, stichera of the Cross and Stavrotheotokia at Vespers, the canon of the Theotokos at Compline, at Matins after the sessional hymns the canon: of the Cross and of the Theotokos; (η) The Saturday service: It containeth in itself: evening stichera, the Theotokion canon of Compline, and Matins with sessional hymns and with two canons: to All Saints and for the Departed. At the Praises (which the other days do not have) martyr stichera, and at the aposticha stichera for the departed. Besides these, there are found here: (ϑ) The Triadica of Gregory of Sinai used on Sunday at the Midnight Office after the Theotokion canon; (ι) Troparia after the Evlogitaria [Amomos / Psalm 118]: “The angelic council,” etc.; (κ) The 11 Matins Gospels with 11 Sunday exaposteilaria and with 11 Gospel stichera; (λ) Daily exaposteilaria sung throughout the entire week, and daily Triadica for the Holy Forty Days; (μ) A small Typikon on how the Octoechos must be used with the Menaion.

6. The Heirmologion [Chant-Book] (in Greek: *Heirmologion* [Ἑιρμολόγιον], from the word: *heirmon* [εἱρμὸν] = *melodia vel series* [melody or chain/series]). It is so called because it containeth in musical notes various church chants, especially from the Octoechos, but besides this also taken from the Menaion, from the Triodion, and from other books, which in church must be sung and not read, and according to which many other hymns are also sung. It is likewise for the most part an abridged Octoechos, but since it also hath hymns taken from other books which are set here to musical notes, therefore in this sense it is an excerpt from all the principal books. Most particularly it containeth in itself: The Heirmologisation of the Octoechos, which is divided according to the 8 tones, that is, melodies. In each tone there is set in notes: (α) How the stichera at “Lord, I have cried” are to be sung with the dogmatica and with the doxastica [slavy]; (β) Sunday Troparia with sessional hymns and graduals [stepenny]; (γ) Heirmoi of the canon with exaposteilaria; (δ) Then model hymns [prosomoia] and Bulgarian chants, etc. — With each tone there are also stichera of the great feasts which at Great Vespers are sung in place of Sunday dogmatica. After the eighth tone there are various hymns and chants taken from other books, such as: the Polyeleos [*Mnohomylostyven*], Exaltations [Velichaniya], the hymn “We who the Cherubim” [Cherubic Hymn], “All of creation rejoiceth in thee,” chants of the Liturgy of the Presanctified, and the thanksgiving hymn of St. Ambrose: “We praise Thee, O God” [*Te Deum*].

7. The Monthly Books [Menaia] (in Greek: *Menaia biblia* [Μηναῖα βιβλία] = *libri mensuales*, from the word: *men* [μὴν] = month). They consist of 12 books for the 12 months, in which are arranged the services for immovable feasts. Every Saint concerning whose life church history knoweth anything, and all church events whose memory holy Church celebrateth, have their proper services and their morning canons, so that their number reacheth up to 800.[^37] Each book containeth in itself: (α) If there be a lesser or non-festal Saint: evening stichera, further the morning canon, and Troparia of lesser Saints; (β) If there be a middle Saint, then at Vespers it hath stichera on 6, the rest as for lesser Saints; (γ) If there be a great Saint, then it containeth Great Vespers with stichera and with readings, sometimes also a Litiya — and the entire divine service is taken from the Menaion; (δ) It containeth in itself the divine service of Marian Feasts; (ε) Of Feasts of the Lord, only the divine service of immovable feasts is found here. At the end of each single book there are: (α) Sunday Theotokia of the Resurrection in 8 tones sung at “Lord, I have cried” and at the aposticha; (β) Theotokia of the 8 tones which on weekdays, when there is a “Glory” to the Saint in the Menaion, are taken at “Now and ever”; (γ) Sunday dismissal Theotokia in 8 tones sung after the Sunday Troparia at “Now and ever”; (δ) Dismissal Theotokia for weekdays in 8 tones sung after the Troparia at Vespers, and at Matins at “The Lord is God” and at the conclusion.

8. The Anthologion (in Greek: *Anthologion* [᾿Ανθολόγιον], from the word: *anthos* [ἄνθος] = *flos*, flower; or: *Tryphologion* [Τρυφολόγιον], from the word: *tryphe* [τρύφη] = *deliciae* [delights]). This book is nothing other than an abridged Menaion, which is also called the “Festal Menaion,” and that because it containeth in itself all those services of immovable feasts of the Lord, Marian feasts, and great Saints which are found also in the Menaion. Besides this, the Anthologion containeth in itself at the end of the second volume also the common services for middle and lesser Saints according to various ranks [choirs], and therefore this part is called: The General Menaion, that is, the *Obshchina*. This book containeth in itself: (a) If there be a Feast of the Lord: (α) Stichera of Small Vespers; (β) Stichera of Great Vespers with readings; (γ) Stichera at the Litiya with stichera at the aposticha; (δ) Three morning sessional hymns; (ε) The sticheron sung after Psalm 50; (ϑ) The Magnification refrain in place of “My soul doth magnify the Lord”; (η) An exaposteilarion; (ι) Stichera at the Praises; (κ) Antiphons at the Liturgy; (λ) Stichera of the Afterfeast sung at Vespers; (b) If there be a Marian Feast, then everything is found just as at Feasts of the Lord, except at Matins, for here after the 8th ode there is no magnification refrain; if there be a refrain, it is found in the Psalter; (c) If there be a great Saint, then it is one case if there be a Litiya, and another if there be no Litiya. If there be a Litiya, then it containeth in itself: (α) Small Vespers with 4 stichera at “Lord, I have cried” and with stichera at the aposticha; (β) Great Vespers with stichera at “Lord, I have cried” and readings, and with stichera at the Litiya and with stichera at the aposticha; Matins with canons, which concludeth in regular order. If there be no Litiya, then it containeth in itself: (α) Stichera of Great Vespers with readings, and with stichera at the aposticha; (β) Three morning sessional hymns; (γ) The sticheron sung after Psalm 50; (δ) Three canons: the first to the Theotokos, and the second and third to the Saint; (ε) Stichera at the Praises; (d) Finally, if there be a middle or lesser Saint, the service is taken from the General Menaion [*Obshchina*]. In the General Menaion the Saint hath: (α) Three stichera at Vespers; “Glory and now”: a Theotokion or Stavrotheotokion; (β) At Matins he hath a canon, sometimes also an exaposteilarion.

9. The Synaxarion (in Greek: *Synaxaria* [Συναξάρια], that is: *biblia*, from the word: *synago* [συνάγω] = *colligere* [to gather together]). It containeth in itself the description [lives] of the Saints. For every day holy Mother Church celebrateth the memory of some Saint whose life ought to be read at Matins. The Synaxarion, like the Menaion, hath 12 volumes. Among us there is no Synaxarion, but in place of this book parts of the Synaxarion are inserted in other books, most particularly in the Flower and Lenten Triodion.

10. The Apostol (in Greek: *Apostolos* [᾿Απόστολος] or *Apostolikon biblion* [᾿Αпоστολικὸν βιβλίον]). It containeth in itself: (α) The Acts of the Apostles, writ- ten by the Holy Evangelist Luke; (β) The General [Catholic] Epistles of the Holy Apostles: James, Peter, John, and Jude; (γ) The Epistles of the Holy Apostle Paul to various churches or persons, thus: to the Romans, 2 to the Corinthians, to the Galatians, to the Ephesians, to the Philippians, to the Colossians, 2 to the Thessalonians, 2 to Timothy, to Titus, to Philemon, and to the Hebrews. — The Apostol with respect to ecclesiastical use is divided into pericopes [*pericopae* / *zachala*], of which there are 339. The numbering by pericopes beginneth from the first chapter of the Acts of the Apostles and proceedeth through all the apostolic epistles in the cited order, even unto the end of the Epistle to the Hebrews. In the margin it is indicated when at the beginning of the reading one must begin: “In those days,” when: “Brethren,” when: “Child Titus,” or: “Child Timothy!” — At the end of the book there is found a direction when and how the appointed portions from the epistles are to be read.[^38]

11. The Gospel (in Greek: *Evangelion* [Εὐαγγέλιον], from the words: *eu* and *angelia* [εὖ et ἀγγελία] = *laetus nuntius* [glad tidings]). It containeth in itself the life and teaching of Jesus Christ set forth by the four Evangelists. The Gospels are divided into chapters and verses, but this division is scholastic; for ecclesiastical use there is the division into pericopes [*zachala*]; — and thus the Gospel of St. Matthew is divided into 115, of St. Mark into 71, of St. Luke into 114, and of St. John into 67 pericopes. At the end the Gospels have an index [rubric] showing what Gospel ought to be read and when, and that firstly according to movable feasts, beginning from the Sunday of Pascha, then according to immovable feasts, beginning from September 1, and lastly according to various occasions and needs. In order that this pericope might be more easily found, there is indicated in the text at the bottom, usually in red letters, that same designation, and therewith are added also the words with which the respective pericope is to begin, which are: “The Lord said,” “The Lord said to His disciples,” “The Lord said to the Jews that came unto Him,” or “The Lord spoke this parable,” or “At that time.” — After these words a red star is printed, directing to the first word of the pericope which must be read, and beside that word there is likewise a star.

12. The Sluzhebnik (in Greek: *Leitourgikon* [Λειτουργικόν], from the word: *leitourgein* [λειτουργεῖν] = *munus publicum obire* [to perform a public duty/office], which word is composed of *leiton* and *ergon* [λεῖτον et ἔργον] = *publicum* et *munus* [public and duty]). It containeth in itself: (α) All things necessary for the Holy Liturgy, namely: Antiphons, Troparia, Prokeimena, Epistles, and Gospels, Exaltations [Velichaniya],[^39] Communion Hymns, and dismissals of Sundays, feasts, and plain movable days, and that: beginning from the Sunday of Pascha unto the Sunday of All Saints, furthermore from the Sunday of All Saints unto the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee, and from thence unto Great Saturday; (β) The Order of the Divine Liturgies: of St. John Chrysostom, of St. Basil the Great, and of St. Gregory the Dialogist, that is, of the Presanctified; (γ) Services of the entire year for immovable feasts, and that: Feasts of the Lord, Marian Feasts, and proper to Saints; (δ) General services for other Saints having no proper services in the Menologion; (ε) Services of various intentions; (ϛ) The Order of Vespers with Litiya, the Order of the Midnight Office, both Sunday and daily, and the Order of Matins; (η) Sunday Troparia, Kontakia, and Theotokia of the eight tones; (ϑ) Daily and general Troparia to the Saints; (ι) The prayer over kolyvo (offering), and “We praise Thee, O God.”

13. The Trebnik [Euchologion] (in Greek: *Euchologion* [Εὐχολόγιον], from the word: *euche* [εὐχὴ] = *praecatio* [prayer]). It is called in the Old Slavonic language “Trebnik” from its principal content, that is, from the Holy Mysteries [Sacraments], which are indispensably necessary [*potrebny*] to men, or also because daily necessities can be satisfied through these services. It containeth in itself: (α) The rites of the seven Holy Mysteries. It must be noted that the priest will find also in the Trebnik those rules which he must observe during the administration of the Holy Mysteries; (β) Services of every kind of ecclesiastical Consecrations and Blessings, such as: the Order of Burials, the Order of Parastas, the Order of the Consecration and Blessing of water, church, cemetery, bells, vestments, vessels, objects, fruits, herbs, etc.; (γ) Various prayers, such as: that concluded at the beginning of the Holy Forty Days, for the blessing of palms, of meats, over the sick, in time of fair weather and drought, etc.; (δ) The Order of the installation of a newly appointed pastor, further the rubric on the Divine Liturgy celebrated by a priest for the first time, and then a second time after 50 years; (ε) At the end there is also the profession of faith which a priest, pastor, professor of theology, canon, and bishop must profess.

14. The Pontifical Sluzhebnik [Archieratikon], that is, *Archieratikon* [᾿Αρχιερατικόν], which containeth in itself the services celebrated by a Bishop.

15. The Typikon (in Greek: *Typikon* [Τυπικὸν], from the word: *typos* [τύπος] = *forma*, *regula* [form, rule]) is the rubrical order, or guide, for how the divine services of the entire year are to be celebrated from all the above-mentioned books. The Typikon which is used among us[^40] was published for the Eparchy of Przemyśl; it is divided into the following parts: (α) On the ecclesiastical rule in general; (β) On the order of sacred ministrations, that is, how Small and Great Vespers, Compline, the Midnight Office, Matins, the Hours, the Typika [*Obidnytsia*], and the Liturgy are celebrated in general; (γ) Special directions for the most notable immovable feasts in the order of the Menologion, beginning from September 1; (δ) Special directions for movable feasts, Sundays, and weekdays from the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee even unto the Sunday of All Saints; (ε) Directions on the Polyeleos, the ordinary Katavasia, the hymn “My soul doth magnify the Lord,” on the Typika [*Izobrazitelni*] and Antiphons, and on dismissals with supplementary notes; (ϛ) Finally, it containeth six pillars [stólpy] with the incipits of the Gospel pillars taken from the Indiction, and a brief Paschalion direction. The Typikon was compiled on the foundation of the church books, to which it frequently referreth, and in which explanation must be sought if anything in the Typikon should be stated incompletely or not entirely clearly.

## § 17. Which of the Church Books Are Private?

(α) The Molitvoslov, or better said, the “Priestly Horologion,” for it containeth in itself everything that the Horologion containeth, but besides this priestly needs are also satisfied here; (β) The Izbornik of needful ecclesiastical rites and services taken from the Horologion, Leitourgikon, Octoechos, Menaion, and Triodion; (γ) The “Sunday Octoechos,” which containeth in itself only the Sunday service; (δ) The “Strastnik” [Passion Book], that is, the book containing the Gospels that are read in Passion Week; (ε) The “Hlasopesnets,” containing in itself the most frequently used chants of the Heirmologion and other church hymns; (ϛ) The “Bohohlasnyk” (in Greek: *hymnologion* [ὑμνολόγιον]), containing in itself church hymns in musical notes;[^41] (η) The “Akathistnik,” which containeth in itself 20 Akathists.

## § 18. How Are Public Books Divided?

Public books are divided in accordance with church rites into 3 parts, and that into: (α) Choral, (β) Liturgical, and (γ) Euchological books (*libri chorales, liturgici et euchologici*).[^42] Choral books contain in themselves pre-liturgical services, and these are: the Horologion, Psalter, Triodion, Pentecostarion, Octoechos, Menaia (or the Anthologion with the General Menaion), and the Synaxarion. Liturgical books contain the services of the Holy Liturgies, and these are: the Leitourgikon, Apostol, and Gospel. Lastly, euchological books contain in themselves the rites of the Holy Mysteries and other ecclesiastical Consecrations and Blessings; to these belongeth: the Trebnik.

## § 19. What Must Be Noted Concerning Church Books?

1. The Psalter is so divided in the services that in each week the entire Psalter is to be read through once, and in the Great Fast in each week (with the exception of Passion Week) twice; — in Passion Week, however, when it is prescribed to read through all 4 Gospels, the entire Psalter is read in the first three days only once, namely all kathismata with the exception of the first three and the 17th, while the 18th kathisma at that time is read three times, namely each day at Vespers. On other days, just as also in Bright Week, the Psalter is omitted.

2. Of the choral books, some are used only for movable feasts or days; such are: the Triodion, Pentecostarion, and Octoechos; others for immovable feasts or days; these are: the Menaion, or Anthologion with the General Menaion; and lastly, certain books both for movable feasts or days and for immovable feasts or days; such are the Horologion and Psalter.

3. Because each day we celebrate together a twofold feast or day, that is, movable and immovable, therefore in a single service those two feasts or days are combined, and in accordance with this, in a single Horologion there are combined from the Sunday of All Saints unto the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee the Octoechos with the Menaion; in the fast, the Triodion with the Octoechos and with the Menaion; and during the time of the Pentecostarion, the Flower Triodion with the Menaion.

4. The Lenten Triodion is joined with the Octoechos in such wise that: (α) On the days of Cheesefare Week there are taken from the Octoechos at Vespers the stichera at “Lord, I have cried,” and at Matins the sessional hymns after the first stichology, exaposteilaria, and those odes of the canon which are not in the Triodion; (β) On the Sundays of the Great Fast, besides the Triodion, the Octoechos is also taken, with the exception of Palm Sunday; (γ) On the weekdays of the Forty Days there are taken from the Octoechos at Matins the sessional hymns after the 1st stichology, but the canon of the Octoechos is superseded by the Triodion [three odes] and this canon on weekdays is entirely omitted. In a word, concerning the Octoechos we can say this: that the chanting of the Octoechos, regarding weekdays, beginneth on Monday after the Sunday of All Saints and in regular order concludeth on Friday before Meatfare Saturday, and exceptionally endeth on Friday before Lazarus Saturday; regarding Sundays, however, it beginneth on the second Sunday after the Descent of the Holy Spirit and continueth unto the 5th Sunday of the Great Fast inclusively. Because parts of the Octoechos are printed at the end of the Menaion, or in the Menaion itself, we can say that in the Great Fast the Octoechos is taken only on Sundays, and on weekdays is omitted. But besides this, the Lenten Triodion is combined also with the Menaion, namely: on all weekdays of the Forty Days there are taken, besides the Triodion, at Vespers from the Menaion 3 or 4 stichera at “Lord, I have cried,” and at Matins the canon of the occurring Saint, which, with slight exceptions indicated in the Triodion, is sung in its entirety.

5. The Octoechos is omitted: (α) From Lazarus Saturday even unto the Sunday of All Saints inclusively, with the exception of certain parts which are taken from the Octoechos and transferred to the Flower Triodion and printed here; (β) From December 20 unto January 14, with the exception of a single Sunday; (γ) On great Feasts of the Lord, on whatever day they fall, even on a Sunday; (δ) On great Marian Feasts, if they fall on a weekday; (if a Marian Feast falleth on a Sunday, the Sunday service is taken from the Octoechos, and the feast from the Menaion); (ε) Likewise also during Forefeasts and at the Apodoses (excepting Sundays) of great feasts; (ϛ) And on the feasts of Great Saints (excepting Sundays) the Octoechos is omitted.

6. The Menaion is omitted: (α) On all Sundays of the Lenten Triodion, beginning from the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee unto Palm Sunday inclusively; (β) On Wednesday and Friday of the 3rd week and on Thursday of the 5th week of the Great Fast; (γ) On Saturdays, namely Meatfare, Cheesefare, of the 1st and 5th weeks, and on Lazarus Saturday; (δ) On all days of Passion Week.

Note: But if on those days there should occur a Great Saint, or the Patron Saint of the church, or the Feast of the Meeting and the Annunciation, their service is taken even on the cited days. Nevertheless, here too there are limitations, thus: the service of the “40 Martyrs” is not taken if those Saints occur on Thursday or Saturday of the fifth week; the service of St. John the Forerunner is not taken on Meatfare Saturday if it falleth on February 24; only the service of the Meeting is always taken on its proper day, and the service of the Annunciation is celebrated even in Passion Week; the service of a Great or Patron Saint in Passion Week is taken in the first days, but in the latter days is transferred to Bright Week on Monday.

7. The Flower Triodion is taken together with the Menaion from Thomas Sunday even unto the Sunday of All Saints, and that usually so that at Vespers there are taken at “Lord, I have cried” 3 stichera from the Menaion, and the canon on 4. Nevertheless, during this time the Menaion is omitted: (α) On all days of Bright Week; (β) On all Sundays of Pentecost; (γ) On the day of Mid-Pentecost and its Apodosis; (δ) On the day of the Apodosis of Pascha; (ε) On the day of Ascension and its Apodosis; (ϛ) On the Saturday before Pentecost; (η) On the feast of the Descent of the Holy Spirit on Sunday and on Monday; (ϑ) On the Sunday of All Saints. Nevertheless, although the services of middle Saints are transferred on these days, those of Great Saints, and especially of Patron Saints of the church, are celebrated on their proper days, even though they should occur on the cited days.

Note: The services of Lesser and Middle Saints which are omitted from Lazarus Saturday unto Thomas Sunday are taken anticipatively [*anticipative*] from Monday of the 2nd week of the Great Fast at Compline.

8. The singing of the eight tones of the Octoechos in the course of 8 weeks is called a “pillar” [*stolp*]. Of pillars throughout one year there are 6; they begin at an appointed time: the 1st pillar beginneth on the 1st Sunday of the Fast of the Apostles [Peter and Paul]; the 2nd on the days after the Prophet Elijah; the 3rd after the Exaltation of the Precious Cross; the 4th during the Fast of the Nativity of the Lord; the 5th after Theophany; and the 6th during the Holy Great Fast. — If anyone wisheth to know on which day of each year each single pillar falleth: let him first look up from the Indiction the Key-letter [*klyuch hranychnyi*], that is, the boundary letter, and thereafter let him look under this letter in the Paschalion, and there it is accurately stated on what day each pillar beginneth.

## § 20. From Whom Do the Church Books Originate?

In the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd centuries Christians probably had of church books only the Holy Scripture of the Old and New Testaments and the Psalter.

But in the 4th century, when the Christian faith was not only free, but also dominant: in the Church of Christ there probably was already the Horologion [Book of the Hours], the Leitourgikon [Sluzhebnik], and the Euchologion [Trebnik]. Those books were probably compiled by the great Hierarchs of the 4th century, especially by St. Basil the Great and St. John Chrysostom.

In the 5th century lived St. Anatolius († 458), Patriarch of Constantinople, who composed a multitude of stichera,[^43] and St. Romanus the Deacon († 500), the Melodist, who composed a multitude of kontakia, that is, short poetic hymns.

In the 6th century lived St. Sabbas († 532), Archimandrite of the “Great Lavra” on the torrent Cedron near Jerusalem, who composed a multitude of chants and canticles and one church order, which is now known under the name of the “Typikon of St. Sabbas.” — Besides this, he is also held to be the first compiler of the Menaion. He systematically compiled the Menaion according to oral tradition, which he received from his teachers St. Chariton (who lived in the time of Constantine), St. Euthymius († 473), and St. Theoctistus († 467),[^44] and prescribed it with obligation for his monks.

In the 7th century lived St. Sophronius († 638), Patriarch of Jerusalem, to whom they attribute the compilation of the Triodion and the enlargement of the Menaion; furthermore St. Andrew, Archbishop of Crete († 712), compiler especially of the Great Canon of Repentance and the canon for the Wednesday of Mid-Pentecost.

In the 8th century lived St. John Damascene († 760), compiler of the Octoechos and enlarger of the Menaion; and St. Cosmas, at first a monk in the “Great Lavra” of Jerusalem, but later Bishop of Maiuma († 781). He composed, among others, the canon for the Descent of the Holy Spirit.

In the 9th century lived St. Theodore the Studite († 826), Archimandrite of the Monastery of Studium at Constantinople, and his brother Joseph († 845), Bishop of Thessalonica, who composed many canons and canticles. Some also attribute to them the compilation or enlargement of the Triodion.

Finally, in this same 9th century St. Theophanes, Archbishop of Nicaea († 858), and Joseph the Hymnographer, a monk in Constantinople († 883), compiled the services of Selected Saints, that is, they enlarged the Menaion.[^45]

# CHAPTER III.
## On Liturgical Expressions and Terminology in the Church Books
## § 21. On Liturgical Technical Terms in General

By technical terms we call those names by which we designate the psalms and poems, readings, liturgical actions and melodies, hymns, chants and canticles, parts and entire services used in our rite. — Those designations were received by the respective parts of our rite either according to text, or melody, or composition, or usage, or purpose, or according to their meaning. Thus the kontakion received this name from the text, for it is a short poem (*kontakion* [κοντάκιον] == *breviculum*); psalms are called antiphons from their melody; the polyeleos received this name from its composition, for therein the word *eleos* [ἔлеος] == *mercy* is found many times; the refrain [*pripiv*] is so called from its usage, for it is chanted before a troparion; the prokimenon is so named from its purpose, for by it the faithful are reminded of the reading of Holy Scripture; while the parastas is so called according to its meaning (*parastasis* [παράστασις] == *intercessio*), for by it we intercede for the departed.

## § 22. On Liturgical Technical Terms in Particular

1. **Order** [*Chyn*] (in Greek: *diataxis* [διάταξις]) is used as the order or disposition of some divine action (*ordo seu dispositio officii divini*).

2. **Service** [*Sluzhba*] (in Greek: *Akolouthia* [᾿Ακολουθία] == *ipsum officium*) is nothing other than the action itself arranged in an ordered sequence (*ipsum officium ordinatum denotat*).

3. **Psalms** are hymns taken from the Old Testament and are called psalms (in Greek: *psalmos* [ψάλμος]) from the word *psallein* [ψάλλειν], for the Hebrews, when singing them, accompanied them with music. Of the psalms the most renowned are: (α) Psalm 103 (“Bless the Lord, O my soul”), which is called the introductory psalm (in Greek: *prooimiakos* [προοιμιακὸς] == *prooimialis*), for with it the church rule beginneth; (β) Psalm 140 (“Lord, I have cried”), called the lucernarium psalm (in Greek: *ho epilychnios* [ὁ ἐπιλύχνιος] == *lucernarius*), and that because at the evening sacrifice of old that psalm was stichologized by the light of that candle which the deacon brought out and placed before the royal doors, or because at Vespers it is stichologized before the lighting of the evening sacrifice; (γ) Psalms 3, 37, 62, 87, 102, and 142 are called the “Six Psalms” [Hexapsalmos] (in Greek: *hexapsalmos* [ἑξάψαλμος], from the words *hex* [ἕξ] and *psalmos* [ψάλμος]); (δ) Psalm 118 (“Blessed are the undefiled”), called the blameless psalm (in Greek: *ho amomos* [ὁ ἄμωμος]), and that because therein is described the blessedness of the blameless man; (ε) Psalm 135 (“O give thanks unto the Lord”), called the polyeleos psalm (in Greek: *ho polyeleos* [ὁ πολυέλεος]), and this name deriveth from the fact that in those verses of the psalm the word “mercy” is frequently repeated (in Greek: *eleos* [ἔλεος]); (ϛ) Psalms 119–133 are called gradual psalms (in Greek: *anabathmoi* [ἀναβαθμοί] == *psalmi graduales*), and that because in the Psalter before Psalm 119 we find the inscription: “A Song of Ascents.” The Holy Fathers say that the Hebrews sang those psalms when they returned from the Babylonian captivity; others say that they sang them during prayer pilgrimages to Jerusalem at the 3 great feasts. Besides this, some also say that those psalms were sung by the Levites at the Feast of Tabernacles while standing upon the 15 steps which were placed in the temple between the court of the men and the court of the women. Some also call those psalms also the psalms “unto the Lord” (*ta pros Kyrion* [τὰ πρὸς Κύριον]), and that because the first of them beginneth with this phrase: “unto the Lord”; (η) Psalms 148, 149, and 150 are called the laudatory psalms, or the psalms at the Praises [Lauds] (*hoi ainoi* [οἱ αἶνοι]), from the word *aineite* [αἰνεῖτε] == “praise ye,” which is repeated in the verses of these psalms; (ϑ) Psalm 102 (“Bless the Lord, O my soul”) and Psalm 145 (“Praise the Lord, O my soul”) are called the Typika psalms (*hoi ton typikon psalmoi* [οἱ τῶν τυπικῶν ψαλμοί]), for they proclaim the prefiguration (*typon* [τύπον] == *figuram*) of the Messiah coming into the world. They are stichologized at the Typika [Obyednytsia].

4. **Kathisma** (from the Greek word *kathizo* [κατίζω] == to sit) signifieth “a sitting.” The Psalter, as was said, containeth 20 kathismata. A kathisma was used in church either by reading or by chanting (*recitando vel cantando*). The reading of the Psalter was called “stichology” (*stichologia* [στηχολογία]), while the chanting was called “psalmody.” — When they only read (*aneu melous* [ἄνευ μέλους]) the Psalter, they divided the kathisma into 3 staseis (*staseis* [στασεῖς] == *stationes*), that is, at three places during the reading they rested. But when they sang (*meta melous* [μετὰ μέλους]), they divided the kathisma into 3 melodies. They read in such a manner that either one read with a single voice, or responsively [in two choirs]. But they chanted the kathisma in such a manner that: (α) either all sang together (*symphonia* [συμφωνία]), or (β) two choirs sang antiphonally (*antiphonia* [ἀνтиφωνία]), or (γ) two sang antiphonally and someone besides this was the leader (*hypophonia* [ὑποφωνία]),[^46] that is, the precentor (*hypoboleus* [ὑποβολεύς]) sang alone, and the others responded to him, either by repeating the final words of the psalm, or with another verse.

5. **Antiphon** (in Greek: *antiphonon* [ἀντιφωνῶν]) signifieth nothing other than “responsive word” (*vox opposita, seu ejusdem vocis oppositio vel rectius ejusdem sententiae ad singulos versus psalmi repetitio*), that is: a corresponding and counter melody, or the repetition of a single divine thought throughout the verses of a psalm. Of the psalms, those which were chanted with a corresponding and antiphonal melody are called “antiphonal psalms,” or simply “Antiphons.” An antiphon in a nar- rower sense consisteth of 2 or 3 verses of a Psalm with “Glory” and “Now,” and of one refrain. Antiphonal psalms are the following: (α) Gradual psalms; (β) “Typika psalms” sung at the Typika [Obyednytsia]; (γ) “Liturgical psalms,” and those antiphons are either daily or festal. Daily antiphons are: Psalm 91 (“It is good to give praise unto the Lord”), thereafter Psalm 92 (“The Lord reigneth”), and Psalm 94 (“Come, let us rejoice in the Lord”). Of feasts, only the Feasts of Pascha, Ascension, Descent of the Holy Spirit, Transfiguration, Exaltation, Nativity of Jesus Christ, Theophany, and Meeting of our Lord in Jerusalem have proper antiphons. On Sunday and on other feasts it would be proper to sing the antiphonal psalms of the Typika, that is, Psalm 102 (“Bless the Lord, O my soul”), Psalm 145 (“Praise the Lord, O my soul”), and instead of the 3rd “antiphonal psalm” it would be proper to sing stichera composed on the individual verses of the evangelical Beatitudes spoken by Jesus Christ upon the mount in Matthew chapter 5, and therefore those are named “stichera on the Beatitudes.” But among us instead of those, Sunday antiphons are sung on Sundays.

6. **Paroemia** (in Greek: *paroimia* [παροιμία] == *parabola, proverbium*). Paroemias are called readings from the Holy Scripture taken for the most part from the Old Testament and containing within themselves either prophecies concerning the event commemorated on that day, or explaining the event of the occurring feast, or containing praise thereof. But readings taken from the New Testament are called “Epistles” [*Apostles*] or “Gospels.” Paroemias are read: (α) At Great Vespers on Feasts of the Lord, of the Mother of God, and of Great Saints, usually 3; (β) In the Great Fast at Vespers, especially those combined with the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts,[^47] only 2; (γ) At Vespers combined with the Liturgy of St. Basil the Great, for example, on the Eves of the Nativity, of Theophany, and on Great Saturday, more; (δ) Besides this, they are also read at the Royal Hours, that is, on the Eve of the Nativity, of Theophany, and on Great Friday, but there at a single hour only one paroemia is taken.

7. **The Canticles of Moses**. Concerning these canticles it is necessary to note that they are taken from various books of the Holy Scripture of the Old and New Testaments, namely: (α) Ode 1 is taken from Exodus 15:1–19; this canticle is that which, after passing through the Red Sea, Moses and the children of Israel sang thus: “Let us sing unto the Lord, for He hath been gloriously glorified”; (β) Ode 2 is taken from the Book of Deuteronomy, chapter 32, which Moses cried aloud at the end of his life, when he had led the people through the wilderness, tactics: “Give heed, O heaven, and I will speak”; (γ) Ode 3 is taken from 1 Kings [1 Samuel], chapter 2, and is the canticle of Hannah, mother of the Prophet Samuel, which she thus proclaimed: “My heart is established in the Lord”; (δ) Ode 4 is taken from the prophecy of Habakkuk, chapter 3, who having seen God coming forth from the shaded mountain, cried aloud thus: “Lord, I have heard Thy report, and was afraid”; (ε) Ode 5 is taken from the book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 26, who having foretold the birth of Emmanuel from the Virgin, cried aloud thus: “Early in the morning my spirit seeketh Thee, O God”; (ϛ) Ode 6 is taken from the prophecy of Jonah, chapter 2:1–7, which he cried aloud when after his three-day stay in the belly of the whale he reached dry land, thus: “I cried in mine affliction unto the Lord God”; (η) Ode 7 is taken from the Book of Daniel, chapter 3, which the Three Youths cried aloud in the Babylonian furnace, saying thus: “Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers”; (ϑ) Ode 8 is likewise taken thence and containeth the thanksgiving canticle of those same youths delivered from the fiery furnace, which they cried aloud thus: “Bless the Lord, all ye works of the Lord; sing and exalt Him above all forever”; (ι) Ode 9 is taken from the Gospel of St. Luke, chapter 1:68–79, which the Prophet Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, cried aloud when his son was born, thus: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel,” etc. At the beginning of this 9th Ode is found also the Canticle of the Theotokos in Luke, chapter 1:46, which she, bearing in her womb the Saviour, cried aloud thus: “My soul doth magnify the Lord, and my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour.” — These Canticles of Moses are very important, for they are the foundation for the canons of the Sunday service. — Now they are read before the odes of the canon only in the Great Fast. Besides this, Ode 1 and Ode 8 are also sung on Great Saturday at Vespers between the 15 paroemias with beautiful hypophonia. Furthermore, it is necessary to note that when there was not yet a Sunday canon in the Church of Christ, these canticles were probably used.

8. **A Verse** [*Stikh*] (in Greek: *stichos* [στίχος] == *versiculus*) is a short chant, taken either from the psalms, or from the Holy Scripture of the New or Old Testament, proclaimed before the stichera according to the melody of the stichera. Verses are either taken, or given [prescribed], or antiphonal. (α) Taken verses are verses from the evening psalms at “Lord, I have cried” and from the psalms at the Praises [Lauds] at Matins and from the Beatitudes of Jesus Christ;[^48] (β) Given verses are according to the dogmatic significance of the feasts and rites at Vespers, at the aposticha, at Compline, at the Litiya, at Matins at the aposticha, and at the Royal Hours, and on various occasions;[^49] (γ) Besides these there are also antiphonal verses, of which the most renowned are those verses which are sung at Matins after the Polyeleos at the exaltations [megalynaria], where after the verses there is for the most part the refrain “We magnify thee,” furthermore at “My soul doth magnify the Lord,” where “More honorable than the Cherubim” is sung, or at the Liturgy at the antiphons, where after the verses “By the prayers of the Mother of God” or “Save us, O Son of God” is repeated.

9. **The Prokimenon** (in Greek: *prokeimenon distichon* [προκείμενον δίσтиχον] == *praevium distichon*, from the word *prokeimai* [πρόκειμαι] == *propositus sum vel positus sum ante*) is a two-fold verse, which is taken for the most part from the psalms, and is set forth before the reading of Holy Scripture and remindeth the faithful of Holy Scripture, or of some extraordinary chant, the subject of which it containeth within itself in brief (*complexive*). Prokimena regarding their usage are: (α) Vespers prokimena, which are used at Vespers both great and small; (β) Matins prokimena, which are taken only at great Matins; (γ) Liturgical prokimena, which are used at Liturgies; (δ) Finally, prokimena according to various services and needs. — Both movable and immovable feasts have their proper prokimena. Nevertheless, of the prokimena prescribed for movable feasts the most renowned are: the daily prokimena used at Vespers throughout the entire week;[^50] furthermore the Sunday prokimena sung at Matins according to the 8 tones, and the Sunday prokimena used at Liturgies according to the 8 tones.[^51] — If in the church books at evening divine services we find this phrase: “Prokimenon of the day” (*prokeimenon tes hemeras* [προκείμενον τῆς ἡμέρας]), then under this we always understand the movable day, that is, Monday, Tuesday, etc., whose prokimenon we take. As regards melody, prokimena are divided into: (α) Great, and (β) Small. Great prokimena are sung with a longer melody, while small ones are sung with a shorter melody.

10. **Stichera** (in Greek: *sticheron* [стиχηρὸν] == *versus*) are strophes of a single poem composed according to dogmatic significance or according to the propriety of the feast, before which a short verse is sung, and that in such wise that the subject of this verse is explained, amplified, or interpreted in the sticheron. Stichera according to liturgical usage are stichera: (α) At Vespers at “Lord, I have cried” and at the Aposticha; (β) At Compline; (γ) At the Litiya; (δ) At Matins at the Praises [Lauds] and at the Aposticha; (ε) At the Royal Hours; and (ϛ) At the Typika [Obyednytsia]. As regards melody they are sung according to the 8 tones, and the tones of the stichera are designated in the church books. Of stichera according to their dogmatic significance the most renowned are: (α) Resurrectional (*anastasima* [ἀναστάσιμα]), in which the Resurrection of Christ is glorified; (β) Cross-resurrectional (*stauroanastasima* [σταυροαναστάσιма]), in which the Cross and Resurrection of Christ are glorified; (γ) Triadica (*triadika* [тριαδικὰ]), composed in honor of the Holy Trinity; (δ) Theotokia (*theotokia* [θεοτόκια]), composed in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos; (ε) Stavrotheotokia (*staurotheotokia* [σταυροθεοτόκια]), in honor of the compassions [sorrows] of the Mother of God; (ϛ) Martyria (*martyrika* [μαρτυρικὰ]), composed in honor of the holy martyrs; (η) Stichera of Pascha, composed in honor of the Feast of Pascha; (ϑ) Stichera of the departed (*nekrosima* [νεκρώσιма]), wherein supplications are set forth for the deceased; (ι) Eastern stichera (*anatolika* [ἀνατολικὰ]), which it is necessary to chant before the rising of the sun. (κ) Besides these, most especially worthy of note are the so-called “Evangelical Stichera,” of which there are 11 and which are sung at Sunday Matins (at the Praises [Lauds], and that at “Glory”), and which are composed according to the 11 Matins Gospels, being in union with them as regards their subject matter.

11. **An Automelon [Idiomelon]** (in Greek: *idiomelon* [ἰδιόμελον]) is such a sticheron that was not chanted according to the melody of either a troparion or a heirmos, but had its own proper and solemn melody (*idion echon melon* [ἴδιον ἔχον μέλος]), and therefore was not chanted with a verse, but without it. Of the automela [idiomela] the most renowned are those whose melody in the 8 tones was probably found for the first time in Bulgaria, and therefore they called them “Bulgarian” [*Bolgar*] chants.

12. **Prosomoia** [Model-stichera], that is, stichera (*stichera prosomoia* [στιχηρὰ προσόμοια]), are such original stichera after whose pattern other stichera are metrically composed, and according to whose melody others also are sung.

13. **A Troparion** (in Greek: *troparion* [троπάριον]). Some derive this name from the text, and others from the melody. Of those who explain that name from the text, that is, according to its meaning, there are some who derive this name from the Greek word *tropos* [τρόπος] == a manner of living (*modus vivendi*), for in troparia is contained the manner of life of a saint, or a description of the feast. Others again derive it from the Greek word *tropaios* [τρόπαιος] == a trophy of victory (*signum victoriae*), because in troparia the victory of a saint is glorified, or the triumph of a feast. Others again derive it in a metaphorical sense from the word *tropos* [τρόπος] == an image (*imago, figura*), and that because in troparia a saint is represented under various figures, such as: a ray, a star, etc. And finally, some derive it from the word *trepein* [тρέπειν] == to turn (*vertere*), because a troparion hath the aim to incline the faithful unto such a life as is represented to us in troparia. Those who explain it according to melody likewise derive it from *trepein* [тρέπειν] == to turn, because those poems with respect to melody are turned in various manners, that is, unto the tones, unto prosomoia, and unto other models. Moreover, troparia are either short hymns or strophes of a single long poem which they sang in church without accompaniment of music. And according to this, they are divided into two parts, into “independent” and into “subordinate.” 1) A troparion as independent is a short hymn chanted in church, wherein is expressed the essence of the day, of the feast, or of the intention, or the virtue-filled life of a saint is described. One may also in this sense call a troparion a principal or ruling chant (*canticum principale vel regens*). Independent troparia have 8 melodies (tones), and a single troparion hath only one, and not two melodies, unto which it is necessary each time to turn, as many times as the troparion is sung. Independent troparia are given both on movable feasts and on immovable feasts, both on Sundays and on weekdays; besides this, diverse intentions also have their proper troparia. Of troparia the most renowned are: the 8 Sunday troparia in the 8 tones chanted throughout 8 weeks. Every single troparion is joined with a Theotokion troparion, because according to the custom accepted in the Greek Church we at all prayers magnify the Mother of God and entrust ourselves unto her mighty intercession. Besides this, independent troparia are joined with a kontakion, an ikos, a synaxarion, and with a hypakoe.
2) Subordinate troparia are parts of a single poem; such are:
(α) Strophes of the odes of a canon, which after the pattern of the first strophe (that is, the heirmos) are metrically composed, and according to the melody of the first strophe (that is, the heirmos) it is necessary to chant them;[^52]
(β) Sessional hymns, or kathisma-troparia, subordinate unto the kathismata, because at the beginning of Matins, between the kathismata, they are chanted or read, and therefore are called sessional troparia (*versus sedendarii*), for when they read a kathisma and chanted those troparia, it was permitted to sit;[^53]
(γ) Exaposteilaria [troparia photagogika], subordinate unto the Sunday Matins Gospels (τροπάρια φωтаγώγικα), of which there are 11, just as there are 11 Gospels and 11 Evangelical Stichera;
(δ) Dismissal Theotokia (τροπάρια ἀπολυτίκια), by which a conclusion is set unto a service or unto some part of divine worship.

14. **A Kontakion** (in Greek: *kontakion* [κοντάкιον] == *breviculum*, from *kontos* [κονтὸς] == *brevis*). It is so called either from the text, for it is a short hymn (*hymnus brevis*), or from the melody (*en konto* [ἐν κονтῷ] == *breviter*), for it was necessary to chant it in a short melody (*brevis modulus*). Kontakia are in union with independent troparia and serve for the fulfillment of the thoughts composed in troparia.

15. **An Ikos** (in Greek: *oikos* [οἶкоς] == *domus vel imago*) is a hymn chanted in church which expresseth the glory and significance of a feast or the praise of a saint more extensively than a kontakion and in figures. An ikos is the counterpart of a kontakion, for a kontakion describeth a feast for us in brief, while an ikos describeth it in its fullness (*amplius et diffusius*). An ikos always followeth after a kontakion and is never read without a kontakion.

16. **Hypakoe** (in Greek: *hypakoe* [ὑπακοὴ], from the word *hypakouo* [ὑπακούω] == to give heed, to hear, *aures praebere ad audiendum verbum vel respondere*) is a hymn worthy of wonder (in Greek: *oidion thaumaston* [ᾠδιον θαυμαστὸν]), which when the deacon chanted as an idiomelon (lit. 'samohlasno'), all the people listened unto it with God-fearing quietude; yet others say also that the deacon chanted only half, and the people half. The Sundays according to the 8 tones, and furthermore also certain feasts, have their proper hypakoe, which now among us is only read at Matins after the sessional hymns and at Little Compline. The hypakoe, although it is appointed in the place of the sessional hymns, when it is permitted to sit, nevertheless is to be sung and heard while standing.

17. **Canon** (from the Greek word *kanon* [κανών] == *regula*) in general is the longest church poem composed according to a determined order, consisting of 9 odes. A canon consisteth of 9 odes for this reason, namely, that by them men may be likened unto the 9 angelic choirs, who unceasingly glorify God. The canon is divided by little ektenias and other chants into three chief sections in honor of the three chief orders[^54] of angels, and that in such wise that after the 3rd Ode a “sessional hymn” is taken, after the 6th a “kontakion” and “ikos,” and after the 9th the “Exaposteilarion.” Canons according to their dogmatic significance are: (α) Resurrectional (*anastasimoi* [ἀναστάσιμοι]), (β) Cross (*staurosimoi* [σταυρόσιμοι]), (γ) Cross-resurrectional (*stauroanastasimoi* [σταυροαναστάσιμοι]), (δ) Triadica (*triadikoi* [τριάδικοι]), (ε) Theotokia (*theotokioi* [θεοτόκιοι]), (ϛ) Cross-Theotokia (*staurotheotokioi* [σταυροθεοτόκιοι]), (η) Dogmatic (*dogmatikoi* [δογμάτικοι]), (ϑ) Of the departed (*nekrosimoi* [νекρόσιμοι]), (ι) Supplicatory (*hiketerioi* [ἱκετήριοι]), and diverse ones according to Feasts of the Lord, of the Mother of God, and of Saints.

18. **An Ode** (in Greek: *oide* [ᾠδὴ]) is a part of a canon and is a longer poem consisting of several troparia similar to one another (that is, strophes) and chanted with a living voice, and not by means of any musical instruments. The first troparion of an ode is called the “Heirmos,” and before it a refrain is not sung; before the 2nd and 3rd a refrain is sung, etc.; before the penultimate troparion “Glory” is sung, and before the last “Now and.” The concluding troparion is called the “Katavasia”; and before it likewise a refrain is not sung.

19. **The Heirmos** (in Greek: *heirmos* [εἱρμός] == *tractus*, a link or connection) is the first troparion of an ode, which is independent in itself both in metrical composition and in melodic chanting, but with respect unto the other subsequent troparia it hath a connective function.

For it uniteth, bindeth, and gathereth these troparia into a single ode[^55] with respect to metrical composition and with respect to melodic chanting, as their prototype.

20. **A Refrain** [*Pripiv*] is a verse which is sung before the troparia subordinate unto the heirmos. The refrain is diverse according to the dogmatic significance of the ode. There are refrains: (α) Resurrectional, for example: “Glory, O Lord, to Thy holy resurrection!”; (β) Cross-resurrectional, for example: “Glory, O Lord, to Thy cross and resurrection!”; (γ) Theotokia, for example: “Most Holy Lady Theotokos, save us!”; (δ) Unto the Holy Apostles, for example: “Holy Apostle of Christ, pray unto God for us”; (ε) Unto Holy Hierarchs, for example: “Holy Father and Hierarch, pray unto God for us”; (ϛ) Unto Venerable Fathers, for example: “Holy Venerable Father, pray unto God for us”; (η) Unto Venerable Women, for example: “Venerable Mother, pray unto God for us!”; (ϑ) Unto Martyrs, for example: “Holy Martyr, pray unto God for us!” etc.

21. **Katavasia** (from the Greek word *katabaino* [καταβαίνω] == descent) is the last troparion of an ode, which when they chanted, both choirs came forth into the midst of the church, and there sang this troparion. A katavasia is either: (α) Ordinary, that is, it is the heirmos itself repeated at the end of the ode; or (β) Prescribed, that is, appointed for a certain season, and such are the heirmoi of festal canons. For example, the prescribed katavasia after the Apodosis of the Exaltation of the Precious Cross until November 21 is: “I shall open my mouth”; from November 21 until January 1 it is: “Christ is born.”

Note 1. The Sunday canon was composed on the foundation of the 9 Canticles of Moses of the Old Testament by St. John Damascene, namely: (α) The Heirmos of the First Ode was composed on the foundation of the thanksgiving canticle sung by Miriam, sister of Moses, after the passage of the Hebrews through the Red Sea (Exod. 15:1–19); (β) The Heirmos of the Second Ode, which is taken only in days of penance, that is, in the Great Fast, but at other times is omitted, was composed on the foundation of the canticle of Moses at the time of the march of the Hebrews through the wilderness: “Give heed, O heaven, and I will speak” (Deut. 32:1–44);[^56] (γ) The Heirmos of the Third Ode was taken from the canticle of Hannah, mother of the Prophet Samuel: “My heart is established in the Lord” (1 Kings [1 Samuel] 2); (δ) The Heirmos of the Fourth Ode was taken from the canticle of the Prophet Habakkuk, who having seen God coming forth from the shaded mountain, cried aloud: “Lord, I have heard Thy report, and was afraid” (Hab. 3:2–20); (ε) The Heirmos of the Fifth Ode was taken from the book of the Prophet Isaiah, who foretold the birth of Emmanuel from the Virgin: “Early in the morning my spirit seeketh Thee, O God” (Isa. 26:9–20); (ϛ) The Heirmos of the Sixth Ode was taken from the words of the Prophet Jonah, who after his three-day stay in the belly of the whale reached dry land: “I cried in mine affliction unto the Lord God” (Jonah 2:1–7); (η) The Heirmos of the Seventh Ode was taken from the canticle of the Three Youths in the Babylonian furnace sung by them: “Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers” (Dan. 3:57–72); (ϑ) The Heirmos of the Eighth Ode was taken from the thanksgiving canticle of those same Three Youths which they sang when delivered from the furnace, saying: “Bless the Lord, all ye works of the Lord; sing and exalt Him above all forever” (Dan. 3:57–72); (ι) The Heirmos of the Ninth Ode deriveth its thoughts from the thanksgiving canticle of the Prophet Zechariah, father of St. John the Baptist: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel” (Luke 1:57–79). It is necessary to note that in the canon at the Eighth Ode instead of “Glory” and instead of “Now and” the Trinitarian refrain is read: “Let us bless the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, the Lord,” etc.; while before the 8th Katavasia there is sung: “We praise, we bless, we worship the Lord.” After the 8th Katavasia, but before the 9th Heirmos at Matins the Priest proclaimeth: “The Theotokos and Mother of Light let us magnify in hymns,” whereupon there is sung the Canticle of the Theotokos: “My soul doth magnify the Lord,” or the magnification of the feast. After the canon at Matins on weekdays there is sung: “It is truly right.”

Note 2. Since at Matins Sunday hath three canons, namely: Resurrectional, Cross-resurrectional, and Theotokion; Monday two: Unto the Angels and of Repentance; Tuesday two: Unto the Forerunner and of Repentance; Wednesday and Friday: Unto the Cross and unto the Theotokos; Thursday: Unto the Apostles and Nicholas; and Saturday: Unto All Saints and of the Departed; unto which sometimes a 3rd or even a 4th is added if a celebrated Saint is kept: therefore the Typikon always accurately indicateth how many troparia must be taken for the individual odes, and that in these words: the canon on 8, on 12, on 14, or on 16, that is, each single ode consisteth of 8, 12, 14, or 16 troparia, of which the first troparion is the heirmos, and the last the katavasia. Furthermore, the Typikon will also show how many troparia are taken from one, how many from another, how many from the third, and how many from the fourth canon out of the multiple canons falling upon a single day; for example, the Sunday canon on 6, the Cross-resurrectional is omitted, the Theotokion on 4, and unto the Saint on 4 — all together it is taken on 14; which rubric only signifieth that from the odes of the Resurrectional canon it is necessary to take 6, from the Theotokion 4 troparia, and from the canon of the Saint likewise 4; the heirmos will be taken of Sunday, and the katavasia the prescribed one: and all the troparia will be 14.

22. **The Synaxarion** (in Greek: *synaxarion* [συναξάριον]) is a short compendium of the history of a feast or of the life of a saint. The synaxarion is read at Matins in the canon after the 6th Ode following the kontakion and ikos. It originateth from the Greek word *synaxis* [συνάξις] == assembly.

23. At Matins in the canon after the 9th Ode the **Exaposteilarion** (lit. 'Svetilen') or “Poslatelen” (in Greek: *exapostilarion* [ἐξаποστειλάριον], from *exapostello* [ἐξαποστέλλω] == I send forth) is taken.

Some say that the exaposteilarion is the very same thing as the hymn of light (lit. 'Svetilen'), that is, it is a hymn containing within itself a supplication for the sending down of heavenly light, or a supplication for the enlightenment of our mind through heavenly light.

Others, however, assert that the exaposteilarion and the hymn of light (lit. 'Svetilen') are not one and the same, for in the exaposteilarion the sending forth of the disciples and Apostles of Christ unto the Gentiles is glorified, while in the hymns of light (lit. 'Svetilen') the presence of heavenly light and that the intelligible light may shine forth is entreated. Besides this, there are also those who derive this designation from the circumstance that in ancient times for the singing of these hymns they sent forth the reader into the midst of the church.

24. **Magnification** [*Velichaniye*] is a laudatory chant (*canticum laudativum*) consisting of a single refrain, which in chanting is for the most part repeatedly sung after certain verses of a psalm. Magnifications are taken: (α) At Matins after the Polyeleos; (β) At Matins in the canon before the 9th Ode; (γ) At the Liturgy instead of “It is truly right” (and for this reason they are also called “Zadostoinyky”); and (δ) At dismissals instead of “More honorable.” — As regards the Magnifications sung after the Polyeleos, concerning these it is necessary to note that they consist of a single given refrain which is repeated after the verses, and at the end at “Alleluia” the “Bulgarian refrain” is taken if there be one, but if there be no Bulgarian refrain, then the given refrain is repeated. — In the Matins canon before the 9th Ode there is usually taken the ordinary Magnification “More honorable than the Cherubim,” in which this refrain is repeated after the verses of the Canticle of the Theotokos: “My soul doth magnify the Lord”; but this ordinary magnification nevertheless is omitted on Feasts of the Lord and of the Mother of God, for then at the exclamation of the Priest: “The Theotokos and Mother of Light,” there is sung the magnification of the feast, consisting of a single refrain with the heirmos of the ninth ode. — This given magnification is taken also at the Liturgy instead of “It is truly right.”[^57]

25. **The Polyeleos** (in Greek: *polyeleos* [πολύελεος], from *polys* [πολύς] and *eleos* [ἔλεος]) is a chant consisting of selected verses taken from two psalms of the 19th Kathisma (“Praise ye the name of the Lord” and “O give thanks unto the Lord”),[^58] in which chant the word “mercy” (*eleos* [ἔλεος]) is frequently repeated throughout those verses. Others derive this name from *polys* [πολύς] == many and *elaion* [ἔλαιον] == oil, and that because in ancient times when they chanted this canticle in church, they lighted many lamps filled with oil.[^59] — The Polyeleos is ordinarily taken at Great Matins on Feasts of the Lord, of the Mother of God, and of Great Saints, and that instead of the 3rd Kathisma, when it is joined with the magnification. — On Sunday it is not taken, except from September 22 until December 20, and furthermore from January 14 until Cheesefare Sunday, when there are usually appended also the Bulgarian refrains in the Heirmologion: “Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit: Glory to Thee, O Holy Trinity. Now and: Rejoice, thou who art full of grace. Alleluia (thrice), glory to Thee, O God: Most Pure Theotokos Mary,” and immediately “The Angelic Council”; while on Cheesefare Sunday, Meatfare Sunday, and the Sunday of the Prodigal Son, unto the two psalms of the 19th Kathisma there is appended also a 3rd psalm, that is, Psalm 136: “By the rivers of Babylon,” which is sung with a solemn Alleluia, while the Bulgarian refrains are omitted.

26. The Sunday troparia: “The Angelic Council,” with the refrain: “Blessed art Thou, O Lord: teach me Thy statutes,” are called “troparia after the Blameless,” and that because they are sung only at Sunday Matins instead of the 3rd sessional hymn, and that after the 3rd Kathisma (that is, the 17th), or better said, after Psalm 118 (“Blessed are the undefiled”), which psalm is called the “blameless” psalm.[^60]

27. **Graduals** (lit. 'Stepenna', Slavonic: *Stepenny*) are stichera composed by St. John Damascene on the basis of the gradual psalms (119–133)[^61] in the 8 tones. Each single tone hath its own gradual stichera, which are chanted not with verses, but without verses in antiphony, and according to this they are also divided into 3 antiphons. Each antiphon hath three stichera, and the third sticheron of each antiphon is composed in honor of the Holy Trinity. (Among us they are only read, except on feast days with a Litiya, when from the graduals of Tone 4 the 1st antiphon is sung: “From my youth.”)

28. **Theotokion** (in Greek: *Theotokion* [Θеοτόκιον]) is a poem composed in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos and chanted with a living voice in church. Theotokia are divided into: (α) Theotokia-troparia, of which some are “independent” (such are the troparia composed for Feasts of the Mother of God); while others are “subordinate” (such are all troparia on Sunday and on other days chanted at “Now” with the resurrectional troparion or with the heirmos.[^62] (β) Theotokia-stichera, which are taken at Vespers at “Lord, I have cried” at “Now,” at the aposticha at “Now,” at the Litiya at “Now,” at Matins at the Praises [Lauds] and at the aposticha at “Now.” Of these Theotokia-stichera the most renowned are those Sunday ones which are sung at “Lord, I have cried” at “Now” and at the aposticha at “Now,” for of these the former are called “Dogmata,” and the latter “Dogmatika,” because they always contain within themselves the dogma relating unto the incarnation of the Son of God.[^63] (γ) There are Theotokion-canons, which are sung on Feasts of the Mother of God at Matins; and besides this at Compline there is a canon of the Theotokos, while at Sunday Matins the third canon likewise is in honor of the Theotokos.

29. **Ektenia** [Litany] (from the Greek word *ektenes* [ἐκτενὴς] and *euche* [εὐχὴ] == *oratio prolixa, protensa, sine intermissione fusa*) is a series of joined supplications or petitions (*concatenata precum series*), upon the conclusion of which, for the fulfillment of these petitions, we entreat intercession from the Most Holy Virgin and from the Saints; and at the end, as a seal of the suppliant petitions, the Priest loudly uttereth the exclamation in honor of the Holy Trinity, firmly confident that all this shall be unto the honor, glory, and worship of God. An ektenia is: (α) The Peace Ektenia [Great Litany] (“In peace let us pray unto the Lord”); (β) The Insistent [Augmented Litany] (“Have mercy on us, O God”); (γ) The Supplicatory [Litany of Fervent Supplication] (“Let us complete our prayer unto the Lord”); (δ) The Little Ektenia (“Again and again in peace let us pray unto the Lord”); (ε) The Litiya Ektenia (“O God, save Thy people”); (ϛ) At the Midnight Office (“Furthermore we pray that this city... may be preserved”); (η) For the Departed; (ϑ) Ektenia of the Catechumens (“Pray, ye catechumens”); (ι) Ektenia for the Enlightened in the Great Fast at the Liturgy of the Presanctified; (κ) And ektenias for diverse needs, as for every petition, over the sick, etc., etc.

30. **A Bow** (in Greek: *metanoia* [μετάνοια] == *poenitentia*) is an outward sign of repentance, reverence, or greeting. A bow in church is twofold: small and great. (α) A small bow (*metanoia mikra* [μετάνοια μικρὰ]) is such a sign whereby a man inclineth only the head and torso, but not the knees (*choris gonyklisias* [χωрὶς γονυκλισίας]). A great bow (*metanoia megale* [μετάνοια μεγάλη]) is such a sign wherein a man inclineth not only the head and torso, but also the knees, and toucheth the earth with his hands and head. Great bows occur on Wednesday and on Friday of Cheesefare Week, furthermore on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday of all weeks of the Great Fast, and cease on Great Wednesday at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts.[^64]

31. **Litiya** signifieth either a procession (*processio*), or a prayer in procession (*supplicatio in processione*). In the ancient times of the Church such processions (that is, litiyas) were in use in Constantinople, where on determined days the Patriarch and the Emperor went forth annually with a procession into the public square (*forum*) and there offered supplication for the salvation of the entire city. But besides these determined days, such processions occurred also upon the appearance of any calamity, for example, an earthquake, hail, famine.... Now, however, by a litiya we understand a prayer ordinarily concluded in procession, which taketh place at Vespers in the narthex, or before the ambo, and wherein we entreat God that He may deliver us from famine, pestilence, etc., that He may bless us and multiply for us the fruits of the earth; and it is joined with the blessing of loaves, wheat, wine, and oil. — Litiya is joined with Vespers,[^65] while with Great Compline it is concluded only on the Nativity of Christ, on Theophany, and on the Annunciation, yet only then if this feast falleth on a fast day in the Great Fast.

32. **The Akathist** (in Greek: *hymnos akathistos* [ὕμνος ἀκάθιστος], from the word *a* [ἀ] and *kathizo* [καθίζω]; *a* is privative, *kathizo* == to sit, or it is a hymn lacking sitting, without sitting) is a thanksgiving poem composed in honor of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Most Holy Theotokos, or of some Saint, consisting of 24 shorter or longer strophes with an antecedent initial troparion, which poem they sang not sitting, but standing. The shorter troparia ending always with “Alleluia” are called kontakia, while the longer ones ending always with “Rejoice, O Bride unwedded” or otherwise are called “ikoi.” Moreover, the entire poem is divided into four staseis (*staseis* [στάσεις]). Of the akathists the most renowned are: (α) The Akathist of the Annunciation, which is taken at Matins on the 5th Saturday of the Holy Great Fast; and (β) The Akathist of the Dormition, which among us is celebrated on the eve of the Forefeast of the Dormition in the evening.

33. **The Paraklesis** (in Greek: *paraklesis* [παράκλησις], from the word *parakaleo* [παρακαλέω] == *evoco auxilii gratia*, I call upon for help) is a service composed unto the Most Holy Theotokos, whereby the people have recourse unto the Most Holy Theotokos, asking her help (*officium impetratorium*), — consisting after the likeness of Matins of a canon, Gospel, and other laudatory stichera.

34. **The Parastas** (from the Greek word *parastasis* [παράστασις] == *intercessio, commendatio*) is a service joined with kolyvo, whereby we intercede for the departed. Kolyvo, which we present in remembrance and instead of the departed person himself, is prepared from boiled wheat, sweetened with honey, and adorned with diverse sweet fruits, and that because: wheat signifieth the body of the deceased, which although it hath died and is buried in the earth, nevertheless in truth shall rise from the grave at the Second Coming of Christ. The sweetness of honey signifieth that although the souls of the Saints already now enjoy divine joys, yet after the resurrection of the body they shall perfectly enjoy the sweetness of divine gladness. The adornment of diverse fruits signifieth the radiant splendor of the beauty of the holy bodies. The parastas consisteth of a canon, which psalms precede, while a Little Panakhyda followeth.

35. **The Dismissal** (in Greek: *apolysis* [ἀπόλυσις] == *dimissio*) is the concluding prayer of any rite (*completi officii conclusio*). In dismissals we call upon the Most Holy Virgin and the Saints (*invocatio B. Virginis et Sanctorum*) that they may intercede for us before God (*intercessio B. Virginis et Sanctorum*), so that God through the prayers of the Most Holy Virgin and the Saints may bless and save us. A dismissal with respect to its composition is threefold: great, small, and least. (α) The Great Dismissal is performed thus: “Wisdom. Bless. He Who is blessed, Christ our God, is and was and shall be, always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages. Most Holy Theotokos, save us,” etc.

(β) The Small Dismissal: “Wisdom. More honorable than the Cherubim... Glory to Thee, O Christ God, our hope, glory to Thee,” etc. (γ) The Least Dismissal beginneth only from “Glory to Thee, O Christ our God.” The Great Dismissal is taken at Vespers and at Matins, and that then if at Vespers there is an entrance, and at Matins the Great Doxology. The Small Dismissal is taken at Vespers and at Matins then if at Vespers there is no entrance, and at Matins there is no Great Doxology. The Least Dismissal is used at the celebration of the Holy Mysteries and at other rites. With respect to prescription, dismissals are: daily, festal, and for diverse intentions. — (α) Of the daily dismissals the most renowned is first: the Sunday dismissal, which beginneth thus: “May He Who rose from the dead, Christ our true God...” etc., in which are also the dismissals prescribed for the other days of the week,[^66] which all begin thus: “May Christ our true God...” (β) Of festal dismissals the most renowned are: the dismissals given for Feasts of the Lord, which begin thus, for example: “May He Who was born in a cave in Bethlehem of Judea and lay in a manger for our salvation, Christ our true God...” etc., or: “May He Who deigned to be baptized in the Jordan for our salvation, Christ our true God...” etc., etc., — or: “May He Who deigned to recline in the arms of righteous Simeon for our salvation, Christ our true God...” etc. The dismissal of the Feast of Pascha is called the “Dismissal with the Cross.” It is taken in Bright Week, on the Sundays of the Paschal season, on Mid-Pentecost Wednesday, and on the Apodosis of Pascha at the Liturgy, — and is concluded thus: The Priest: “Wisdom”; the Choir: “Bless,” etc.; the Priest: “May Christ Who rose from the dead, by death trampling down death, and to those in the tombs bestowing life, our true God...” etc.; the Choir: “Amen.” After this the Priest, turning unto the people with the Cross, signing them thrice with the sign of the Cross, saith: “Christ is risen!” And the people say: “Indeed He is risen!” and thus the blessing taketh place. On Feasts of the Mother of God the daily dismissal is taken, only at the name of the Most Holy Virgin in the dismissal these words are inserted, for example: “whose Entry into the Temple we today celebrate with a bright feast,” or: “whose honorable Dormition we today celebrate with a bright feast,” etc. On Feasts of Saints we likewise take the daily dismissal, and the Saint is only commemorated or...

thus: “whose solemn feast we brightly celebrate,” or “whose feast we today accomplish,” or “whose memory we today venerate,” etc., according as the Saint whose day we celebrate is either a great, or middle, or little feast. (γ) Of the dismissals for diverse intentions, however, the most renowned is that for the departed, which beginneth thus: “May He Who hath dominion over the quick and the dead, Christ our true God...” etc., and in which after the invocation of the Saints the names of the departed are also commemorated. Finally concerning dismissals it is also necessary to note that therein that Saint, male or female, unto whom the church is dedicated, is always commemorated by these words: “whose all-honorable temple this is.” — Within the afterfeast, when the Oktoechos is not taken, the festal dismissal is taken, except on Sunday. Within the feast of Pascha, however, the “Dismissal without the Cross” is taken, thus: The Priest: “Wisdom.” The Choir: “Shine, shine,” etc. The Priest: “May He Who rose from the dead...” etc., and thus: “Blessing.” If, however, the Apodosis of a feast falleth on Sunday, we say the Sunday dismissal.

§. 23.

What Is Further Necessary to Note Concerning These Designations?

(α) The evening chant “Gladsome Light” is called the “Lucernarium Hymn” (*hymnos epilychnios* [ὕμνος ἐπιλύχνιος] == *hymnus lucernarius*). This hymn, as St. Basil saith,[^67] the Holy Martyr Athenogenes († 169) composed; nevertheless it is customarily attributed unto St. Sophronius, Patriarch of Jerusalem, probably for this reason, that he introduced it into church use in his province around the year 634.
(β) A twofold doxology (*doxologia* [δοξολογία]) do we have: little and great. The Little Doxology is: “Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit,” etc.; the Great, however, is “Glory to God in the highest.” This hymn is called also the Angelic Hymn (*hymnus angelicus*). — Here, nevertheless, it is also necessary to note that in church books, when the hymn “Glory to God in the highest” is only to be read, then this is called the “Little Doxology,” whereas the singing of this hymn is called the “Great Doxology.”
(γ) The hymn “Holy God” is called the “Trisagion” (*hymnos trisagios* [ὕμνος τρισάγιος]). It came into church use during the time of St. Proclus, Patriarch of Constantinople († 446), and Emperor Theodosius II (408–450).[^68]
(δ) The hymn “Let us who mystically represent the Cherubim” is called the “Cherubic Hymn” (*hymnus Cherubicus*).[^69]
(ε) “Holy, Holy, Holy” is called the “Triumphal Hymn” (*hymnos epinikios* [ὕμνος ἐπινίκιος] == *hymnus victoriae et triumphi*).
(ϛ) Antidoron (in Greek: *antidoron* [ἀντίδωρον], from *anti* and *hagia dora* [ἀντὶ et ἅγια δῶρα]) are particles of the blessed prosphora wherefrom the Lamb was taken for the sacred action, which instead of the holy gifts, instead of Holy Communion, it is necessary to give unto those who did not participate in the Holy Mysteries.[^70] Pope St. Pius I, Universal Pontiff, instituted the distribution of the antidoron.[^71]
