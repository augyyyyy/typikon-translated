16. **Hypakoe** (in Greek: *hypakoe* [ὑπακοὴ], from the word *hypakouo* [ὑπακούω] == to give heed, to hear, *aures praebere ad audiendum verbum vel respondere*) is a hymn worthy of wonder (in Greek: *oidion thaumaston* [ᾠδιον θαυμαστὸν]), which when the deacon chanted as an idiomelon (lit. 'samohlasno'), all the people listened unto it with God-fearing quietude; yet others say also that the deacon chanted only half, and the people half. The Sundays according to the 8 tones, and furthermore also certain feasts, have their proper hypakoe, which now among us is only read at Matins after the sessional hymns and at Little Compline. The hypakoe, although it is appointed in the place of the sessional hymns, when it is permitted to sit, nevertheless is to be sung and heard while standing.

17. **Canon** (from the Greek word *kanon* [κανών] == *regula*) in general is the longest church poem composed according to a determined order, consisting of 9 odes. A canon consists of 9 odes for this reason, namely, that by them men may be likened unto the 9 angelic choirs, who unceasingly glorify God. The canon is divided by little ektenias and other chants into three chief sections in honor of the three chief orders[^54] of angels, and that in such wise that after the 3rd Ode a “sessional hymn” is taken, after the 6th a “kontakion” and “ikos,” and after the 9th the “Exaposteilarion.” Canons according to their dogmatic significance are: (α) Resurrectional (*anastasimoi* [ἀναστάσιμοι]), (β) Cross (*staurosimoi* [σταυρόσιμοι]), (γ) Cross-resurrectional (*stauroanastasimoi* [σταυροαναστάσιμοι]), (δ) Triadica (*triadikoi* [τριάδικοι]), (ε) Theotokia (*theotokioi* [θεοτόκιοι]), (ϛ) Cross-Theotokia (*staurotheotokioi* [σταυροθεοτόκιοι]), (η) Dogmatic (*dogmatikoi* [δογμάτικοι]), (ϑ) Of the departed (*nekrosimoi* [νекρόσιμοι]), (ι) Supplicatory (*hiketerioi* [ἱκετήριοι]), and diverse ones according to Feasts of the Lord, of the Mother of God, and of Saints.

18. **An Ode** (in Greek: *oide* [ᾠδὴ]) is a part of a canon and is a longer poem consisting of several troparia similar to one another (that is, strophes) and chanted with a living voice, and not by means of any musical instruments. The first troparion of an ode is called the “Heirmos,” and before it a refrain is not sung; before the 2nd and 3rd a refrain is sung, etc.; before the penultimate troparion “Glory” (*Сла́ва*) is sung, and before the last “Now and.” The concluding troparion is called the “Katavasia”; and before it likewise a refrain is not sung.

19. **The Heirmos** (in Greek: *heirmos* [εἱρμός] == *tractus*, a link or connection) is the first troparion of an ode, which is independent in itself both in metrical composition and in melodic chanting, but with respect unto the other subsequent troparia it hath a connective function.

=== LEAF p72 ===
[Book Page 48]

For it unites, binds, and gathers these troparia into a single ode[^55] with respect to metrical composition and with respect to melodic chanting, as their prototype.

20. **A Refrain** [*Pripiv*] is a verse which is sung before the troparia subordinate unto the heirmos. The refrain is diverse according to the dogmatic significance of the ode. There are refrains: (α) Resurrectional, for example: “Glory, O Lord, to Thy holy resurrection!”; (β) Cross-resurrectional, for example: “Glory, O Lord, to Thy cross and resurrection!”; (γ) Theotokia, for example: “Most Holy Lady Theotokos, save us!”; (δ) Unto the Holy Apostles, for example: “Holy Apostle of Christ, pray unto God for us”; (ε) Unto Holy Hierarchs, for example: “Holy Father and Hierarch, pray unto God for us”; (ϛ) Unto Venerable Fathers, for example: “Holy Venerable Father, pray unto God for us”; (η) Unto Venerable Women, for example: “Venerable Mother, pray unto God for us!”; (ϑ) Unto Martyrs, for example: “Holy Martyr, pray unto God for us!” etc.

21. **Katavasia** (from the Greek word *katabaino* [καταβαίνω] == descent) is the last troparion of an ode, which when they chanted, both choirs came forth into the midst of the church, and there sang this troparion. A katavasia is either: (α) Ordinary, that is, it is the heirmos itself repeated at the end of the ode; or (β) Prescribed, that is, appointed for a certain season, and such are the heirmoi of festal canons. For example, the prescribed katavasia after the Apodosis of the Exaltation of the Precious Cross until November 21 is: “I shall open my mouth” (*Отверзу*); from November 21 until January 1 it is: “Christ is born.”

Note 1. The Sunday canon was composed on the foundation of the 9 Canticles of Moses of the Old Testament by St. John Damascene, namely: (α) The Heirmos of the First Ode was composed on the foundation of the thanksgiving canticle sung by Miriam, sister of Moses, after the passage of the Hebrews through the Red Sea (Exod. 15:1–19); (β) The Heirmos of the Second Ode, which is taken only in days of penance, that is, in the Great Fast, but at other times is omitted, was composed on the foundation of the canticle of Moses at the time of the march of the Hebrews through the wilderness: “Give heed, O heaven, and I will speak” (Deut. 32:1–44);[^56] (γ) The Heirmos of the Third Ode was taken from the canticle of Hannah,

=== LEAF p73 ===
[Book Page 49]

mother of the Prophet Samuel: “My heart is established in the Lord” (1 Kings [1 Samuel] 2); (δ) The Heirmos of the Fourth Ode was taken from the canticle of the Prophet Habakkuk, who having seen God coming forth from the shaded mountain, cried aloud: “Lord, I have heard Thy report, and was afraid” (Hab. 3:2–20); (ε) The Heirmos of the Fifth Ode was taken from the book of the Prophet Isaiah, who foretold the birth of Emmanuel from the Virgin: “Early in the morning my spirit seeks Thee, O God” (Isa. 26:9–20); (ϛ) The Heirmos of the Sixth Ode was taken from the words of the Prophet Jonah, who after his three-day stay in the belly of the whale reached dry land: “I cried in mine affliction unto the Lord God” (Jonah 2:1–7); (η) The Heirmos of the Seventh Ode was taken from the canticle of the Three Youths in the Babylonian furnace sung by them: “Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers” (Dan. 3:57–72); (ϑ) The Heirmos of the Eighth Ode was taken from the thanksgiving canticle of those same Three Youths which they sang when delivered from the furnace, saying: “Bless the Lord, all ye works of the Lord; sing and exalt Him above all forever” (Dan. 3:57–72); (ι) The Heirmos of the Ninth Ode derives its thoughts from the thanksgiving canticle of the Prophet Zechariah, father of St. John the Baptist: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel” (Luke 1:57–79). It is necessary to note that in the canon at the Eighth Ode instead of “Glory” (*Сла́ва*) and instead of “Now and” the Trinitarian refrain is read: “Let us bless the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, the Lord,” etc.; while before the 8th Katavasia there is sung: “We praise, we bless, we worship the Lord.” After the 8th Katavasia, but before the 9th Heirmos at Matins the Priest proclaims: “The Theotokos and Mother of Light let us magnify in hymns,” whereupon there is sung the Canticle of the Theotokos: “My soul doth magnify the Lord,” or the magnification of the feast. After the canon at Matins on weekdays there is sung: “It is truly right.”

Note 2. Since at Matins Sunday hath three canons, namely: Resurrectional, Cross-resurrectional, and Theotokion; Monday two: Unto the Angels and of Repentance; Tuesday two: Unto the Forerunner and of Repentance; Wednesday and Friday: Unto the Cross and unto the Theotokos; Thursday: Unto the Apostles and Nicholas; and Saturday: Unto All Saints and of the Departed; unto which sometimes a 3rd or even a 4th is added if a celebrated Saint is kept: therefore the Typikon always accurately indicates how many troparia must be taken for the individual odes, and that in these words: the canon on 8, on 12, on 14, or on 16, that is, each single ode consists of 8, 12, 14, or 16 troparia, of which the first troparion is the heirmos, and the last the katavasia. Furthermore, the Typikon will also show how many troparia

22. **The Synaxarion** (in Greek: *synaxarion* [συναξάριον]) is a short compendium of the history of a feast or of the life of a saint. The synaxarion is read at Matins in the canon after the 6th Ode following the kontakion and ikos. It originates from the Greek word *synaxis* [συνάξις] == assembly.

23. At Matins in the canon after the 9th Ode the **Exaposteilarion** (lit. 'Svetilen') or “Poslatelen” (in Greek: *exapostilarion* [ἐξаποστειλάριον], from *exapostello* [ἐξαποστέλλω] == I send forth) is taken.

Some say that the exaposteilarion is the very same thing as the hymn of light (lit. 'Svetilen'), that is, it is a hymn containing within itself a supplication for the sending down of heavenly light, or a supplication for the enlightenment of our mind through heavenly light.

Others, however, assert that the exaposteilarion and the hymn of light (lit. 'Svetilen') are not one and the same, for in the exaposteilarion the sending forth of the disciples and Apostles of Christ unto the Gentiles is glorified, while in the hymns of light (lit. 'Svetilen') the presence of heavenly light and that the intelligible light may shine forth is entreated. Besides this, there are also those who derive this designation from the circumstance that in ancient times for the singing of these hymns they sent forth the reader into the midst of the church.

24. **Magnification** [*Velichaniye*] is a laudatory chant (*canticum laudativum*) consisting of a single refrain, which in chanting is for the most part repeatedly sung after certain verses of a psalm. Magnifications are taken: (α) At Matins after the Polyeleos; (β) At Matins in the canon before the 9th Ode; (γ) At the Liturgy instead of “It is truly right” (*Достойно есть*) (and for this reason they are also called “Zadostoinyky”); and (δ) At dismissals instead of “More honorable.” — As regards the Magnifications sung after the Polyeleos, concerning these it is necessary to note that they consist of a single given refrain which is repeated after the verses, and at the end at “Alleluia” the “Bulgarian refrain” is taken if there be one, but if there be no Bulgarian refrain, then the given refrain is repeated. — In the Matins canon before the 9th Ode there is usually taken the ordinary Magnification “More honorable than the Cherubim,” in which this refrain is repeated after the verses of the Canticle of the Theotokos: “My soul doth magnify the Lord” (*Вели́читъ дꙋша̀ моя̀ Го́спода...*); but this ordinary magnification nevertheless is omitted on Feasts of the Lord

=== LEAF p75 ===
[Book Page 51]

and of the Mother of God, for then at the exclamation of the Priest: “The Theotokos and Mother of Light,” there is sung the magnification of the feast, consisting of a single refrain with the heirmos of the ninth ode. — This given magnification is taken also at the Liturgy instead of “It is truly right.”[^57]

25. **The Polyeleos** (in Greek: *polyeleos* [πολύελεος], from *polys* [πολύς] and *eleos* [ἔλεος]) is a chant consisting of selected verses taken from two psalms of the 19th Kathisma (“Praise ye the name of the Lord” and “O give thanks unto the Lord”),[^58] in which chant the word “mercy” (*eleos* [ἔλεος]) is frequently repeated throughout those verses. Others derive this name from *polys* [πολύς] == many and *elaion* [ἔλαιον] == oil, and that because in ancient times when they chanted this canticle in church, they lighted many lamps filled with oil.[^59] — The Polyeleos is ordinarily taken at Great Matins on Feasts of the Lord, of the Mother of God, and of Great Saints, and that instead of the 3rd Kathisma, when it is joined with the magnification. — On Sunday it is not taken, except from September 22 until December 20, and furthermore from January 14 until Cheesefare Sunday, when there are usually appended also the Bulgarian refrains in the Heirmologion: “Glory to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit: Glory to Thee, O Holy Trinity. Now and: Rejoice, thou who art full of grace. Alleluia (thrice), glory to Thee, O God: Most Pure Theotokos Mary,” and immediately “The Angelic Council” (*Аггельскій соборъ*); while on Cheesefare Sunday, Meatfare Sunday, and the Sunday of the Prodigal Son, unto the two psalms of the 19th Kathisma there is appended also a 3rd psalm, that is, Psalm 136: “By the rivers of Babylon,” which is sung with a solemn Alleluia, while the Bulgarian refrains are omitted.

The Sunday troparia: “The Angelic Council,” with the refrain: “Blessed art Thou, O Lord: teach me Thy statutes,” are called “troparia after the Blameless,” and that because they are sung only at Sunday Matins instead of the 3rd sessional hymn, and that after the 3rd Kathisma (that is, the 17th), or better said, after Psalm 118 (“Blessed are the undefiled”), which psalm is called the “blameless” psalm.[^60]

27. **Graduals** (lit. 'Stepenna', Slavonic: *Stepenny*) are stichera composed by St. John Damascene on the basis of the gradual psalms (119–133)[^61] in the 8 tones. Each single tone hath its own gradual stichera, which are chanted not with verses, but without verses in antiphony, and according to this they are also divided into 3 antiphons. Each antiphon hath three stichera, and the third sticheron of each antiphon is composed in honor of the Holy Trinity. (Among us they are only read, except on feast days with a Litiya, when from the graduals of Tone 4 the 1st antiphon is sung: “From my youth.”)

28. **Theotokion** (in Greek: *Theotokion* [Θеοτόκιον]) is a poem composed in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos and chanted with a living voice in church. Theotokia are divided into: (α) Theotokia-troparia, of which some are “independent” (such are the troparia composed for Feasts of the Mother of God); while others are “subordinate” (such are all troparia on Sunday and on other days

chanted at “Now” with the resurrectional troparion or with the heirmos.[^62] (β) Theotokia-stichera, which are taken at Vespers at “Lord, I have cried” (*Господи воззвахъ*) at “Now,” at the aposticha at “Now,” at the Litiya at “Now,” at Matins at the Praises [Lauds] and at the aposticha at “Now.” Of these Theotokia-stichera the most renowned are those Sunday ones which are sung at “Lord, I have cried” (*Господи воззвахъ*) at “Now” and at the aposticha at “Now,” for of these the former are called “Dogmata,” and the latter “Dogmatika,” because they always contain within themselves the dogma relating unto the incarnation of the Son of God.[^63] (γ) There are Theotokion-canons, which are sung on Feasts of the Mother of God at Matins; and besides this at Compline there is a canon of the Theotokos, while at Sunday Matins the third canon likewise is in honor of the Theotokos.

29. **Ektenia** [Litany] (from the Greek word *ektenes* [ἐκτενὴς] and *euche* [εὐχὴ] == *oratio prolixa, protensa, sine intermissione fusa*) is a series of joined supplications or petitions (*concatenata precum series*), upon the conclusion of which, for the fulfillment of these petitions, we entreat intercession from the Most Holy Virgin and from the Saints; and at the end, as a seal of the suppliant petitions, the Priest loudly utters the exclamation in honor of the Holy Trinity, firmly confident that all this shall be unto the honor, glory, and worship of God. An ektenia is: (α) The Peace Ektenia [Great Litany] (“In peace let us pray unto the Lord”); (β) The Insistent [Augmented Litany] (“Have mercy on us, O God” (*Помилуй насъ Боже*)); (γ) The Supplicatory [Litany of Fervent Supplication] (“Let us complete our prayer unto the Lord” (*И҆спо́лнимъ моли́твꙋ на́шꙋ...*)); (δ) The Little Ektenia (“Again and again in peace let us pray unto the Lord”); (ε) The Litiya Ektenia (“O God, save Thy people”); (ϛ) At the Midnight Office (“Furthermore we pray that this city... may be preserved”); (η) For the Departed; (ϑ) Ektenia of the Catechumens (“Pray, ye catechumens”); (ι) Ektenia for the Enlightened in the Great Fast at the Liturgy of the Presanctified; (κ) And ektenias for diverse needs, as for every petition, over the sick, etc., etc.

30. **A Bow** (in Greek: *metanoia* [μετάνοια] == *poenitentia*) is an outward sign of repentance, reverence, or greeting. A bow in church is twofold: small and great. (α) A small bow (*metanoia mikra* [μετάνοια μικρὰ]) is such a sign whereby a man inclines only the head and torso, but not the knees (*choris gonyklisias* [χωрὶς γονυκλισίας]). A great bow (*metanoia megale* [μετάνοια μεγάλη]) is such a sign wherein a man inclines not only the head and torso, but also the knees, and touches the earth with his hands and head. Great bows occur on Wednesday and on Friday of Cheesefare Week, furthermore on Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday of all weeks of the Great Fast, and cease on Great Wednesday at the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts.[^64]

31. **Litiya** signifies either a procession (*processio*), or a prayer in procession (*supplicatio in processione*). In the ancient times of the Church such processions (that is, litiyas) were in use in Constantinople, where on determined days the Patriarch and the Emperor went forth annually with a procession into the public square (*forum*) and there offered supplication for the salvation of the entire city. But besides these determined days, such processions occurred also upon the appearance of any calamity, for example, an earthquake, hail, famine.... Now, however, by a litiya we understand a prayer ordinarily concluded in procession, which takes place at Vespers in the narthex, or before the ambo, and wherein we entreat God that He may deliver us from famine, pestilence, etc., that He may bless us and multiply for us the fruits of the earth; and it is joined with the blessing of loaves, wheat, wine, and oil. — Litiya is joined with Vespers,[^65] while with Great Compline it is concluded only on the Nativity of Christ, on Theophany, and on the Annunciation, yet only then if this feast falls on a fast day in the Great Fast.

32. **The Akathist** (in Greek: *hymnos akathistos* [ὕμνος ἀκάθιστος], from the word *a* [ἀ] and *kathizo* [καθίζω]; *a* is privative, *kathizo* == to sit, or it is a hymn lacking sitting, without sitting) is a thanksgiving poem composed in honor of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Most Holy Theotokos, or of some Saint, consisting of

=== LEAF p79 ===
[Book Page 55]

24 shorter or longer strophes with an antecedent initial troparion, which poem they sang not sitting, but standing. The shorter troparia ending always with “Alleluia” are called kontakia, while the longer ones ending always with “Rejoice, O Bride unwedded” or otherwise are called “ikoi.” Moreover, the entire poem is divided into four staseis (*staseis* [στάσεις]). Of the akathists the most renowned are: (α) The Akathist of the Annunciation, which is taken at Matins on the 5th Saturday of the Holy Great Fast; and (β) The Akathist of the Dormition, which among us is celebrated on the eve of the Forefeast of the Dormition in the evening.

33. **The Paraklesis** (in Greek: *paraklesis* [παράκλησις], from the word *parakaleo* [παρακαλέω] == *evoco auxilii gratia*, I call upon for help) is a service composed unto the Most Holy Theotokos, whereby the people have recourse unto the Most Holy Theotokos, asking her help (*officium impetratorium*), — consisting after the likeness of Matins of a canon, Gospel, and other laudatory stichera.

34. **The Parastas** (from the Greek word *parastasis* [παράστασις] == *intercessio, commendatio*) is a service joined with kolyvo, whereby we intercede for the departed. Kolyvo, which we present in remembrance and instead of the departed person himself, is prepared from boiled wheat, sweetened with honey, and adorned with diverse sweet fruits, and that because: wheat signifies the body of the deceased, which although it hath died and is buried in the earth, nevertheless in truth shall rise from the grave at the Second Coming of Christ. The sweetness of honey signifies that although the souls of the Saints already now enjoy divine joys, yet after the resurrection of the body they shall perfectly enjoy the sweetness of divine gladness. The adornment of diverse fruits signifies the radiant splendor of the beauty of the holy bodies. The parastas consists of a canon, which psalms precede, while a Little Panakhyda follows.

35. **The Dismissal** (in Greek: *apolysis* [ἀπόλυσις] == *dimissio*) is the concluding prayer of any rite (*completi officii conclusio*). In dismissals we call upon the Most Holy Virgin and the Saints (*invocatio B. Virginis et Sanctorum*) that they may intercede for us before God (*intercessio B. Virginis et Sanctorum*), so that God through the prayers of the Most Holy Virgin and the Saints may bless and save us. A dismissal with respect to its composition is threefold: great, small, and least. (α) The Great Dismissal is performed thus: “Wisdom. Bless. He Who is blessed, Christ our God, is and was and shall be, always, now and forever, and unto the ages of ages. Most Holy Theotokos, save us,” etc.

[Book Page 56]

(β) The Small Dismissal: “Wisdom. More honorable than the Cherubim... Glory to Thee, O Christ God, our hope, glory to Thee,” etc. (γ) The Least Dismissal begins only from “Glory to Thee, O Christ our God.” The Great Dismissal is taken at Vespers and at Matins, and that then if at Vespers there is an entrance, and at Matins the Great Doxology. The Small Dismissal is taken at Vespers and at Matins then if at Vespers there is no entrance, and at Matins there is no Great Doxology. The Least Dismissal is used at the celebration of the Holy Mysteries and at other rites. With respect to prescription, dismissals are: daily, festal, and for diverse intentions. — (α) Of the daily dismissals the most renowned is first: the Sunday dismissal, which begins thus: “May He Who rose from the dead, Christ our true God...” etc., in which are also the dismissals prescribed for the other days of the week,[^66] which all begin thus: “May Christ our true God...” (β) Of festal dismissals the most renowned are: the dismissals given for Feasts of the Lord, which begin thus, for example: “May He Who was born in a cave in Bethlehem of Judea and lay in a manger for our salvation, Christ our true God...” etc., or: “May He Who deigned to be baptized in the Jordan for our salvation, Christ our true God...” etc., etc., — or: “May He Who deigned to recline in the arms of righteous Simeon for our salvation, Christ our true God...” etc. The dismissal of the Feast of Pascha is called the “Dismissal with the Cross.” It is taken in Bright Week, on the Sundays of the Paschal season, on Mid-Pentecost Wednesday, and on the Apodosis of Pascha at the Liturgy, — and is concluded thus: The Priest: “Wisdom”; the Choir: “Bless,” etc.; the Priest: “May Christ Who rose from the dead, by death trampling down death, and to those in the tombs bestowing life, our true God...” etc.; the Choir: “Amen.” After this the Priest, turning unto the people with the Cross, signing them thrice with the sign of the Cross, saith: “Christ is risen!” And the people say: “Indeed He is risen!” and thus the blessing takes place.
