§. 215.

What the Priest Performeth on the First Day of the Holy Great Fast.[^320]

On this day (that is, on the First Monday of the Great Fast), after the divine service of the day, the Priest instructeth his spiritual children how they ought to prepare themselves through the holy Fast for the Passions of Christ. §. 216.

What is the Dread Prayer?

The dread prayer is a service over those who suffer from demons. It is the adjuration of the devil. It consisteth of Psalms 142, 22, 26, 64, and 50, further of the canon to Jesus Christ, to the Most Pure Virgin, to the Bodiless Powers, to the Apostles, and to All Saints, and of the exorcistic prayers of Saint Basil the Great and Saint John Chrysostom.[^321]

§. 219.

What is Necessary to Note Concerning the Color of Church Vestments?

In the Western-Latin Church the color of church vestments is strictly regulated, and it is fivefold: white, red [crimson], green, violet, and black;[^328] further, vestments of diverse colors may be used freely only if one of these five colors is the fundamental color; moreover, it is most precisely

<!-- LEAF: p308 -->

(p. 284)

determined also which color of vestments it behoveth to use on individual days and at various divine services.
Among us in the Eastern-Greek Church, however, regarding the color of church vestments there is no precise arrangement. The ecclesiastical books speak of bright, red [crimson], and black vestments.
Bright vestments[^329] are used on festive days, red [crimson] on fasting days, and black on days of commemoration for the departed.[^330]
Furthermore, the color of vestments hath its symbolic significance: for white vestments are a symbol of innocence, sanctity, and joy; red vestments are a symbol of sorrow and repentance;[^331] while black vestments are a sign of grief. And on this account it is not fitting to use vestments of diverse color, or of such color as is not accepted by the Church or doth not correspond with the mystery of the feast. For if the fatherland hath its colors, then let the Church also have its colors, as is evident.

§. 220.

What is Necessary to Know Concerning the Ecclesiastical Language?

The language of divine services is twofold: $\alpha$) symbolic (*képnyelv* [symbolic language]), and $\beta$) verbal (*szóbeli nyelv* [spoken language]).[^332]
In order that a man may understand the symbolic language of divine services, he must first learn what essence or what symbolic meaning each individual service hath according to its theological sense, and therein each individual liturgical action.
As for the verbal language, concerning it we indicate this: that it must be, according to the common law of each church (*juxta jus commune*), more or less distinct from the common and ordinarily spoken tongue (*köznyelv* [vernacular]), and unchangeable, that is, a dead language (*holt nyelv* [dead language]).

<!-- LEAF: p309 -->

(p. 285)

The difference between the Eastern and Western Churches — regarding the discipline of the ecclesiastical language — is this: that the Western Church, according to its particular law (*juxta jus privatum*), useth only one[^333] dead language in the Divine Liturgy, whereas the Eastern Church according to its particular law (*juxta jus privatum*) useth more.[^334]
That an ecclesiastical language properly established is not permitted to be altered or uprooted, and that in the Eastern Churches it must also be a dead language, is attested by Benedict XIV of blessed memory, who thus writeth: “Constantem, firmamque esse disciplinam, ne Missae idioma mutetur, et si mutet lingua vernacula; sed eo sermone Missa celebretur, quo celebrata est ab initio, etiam si illa lingua exoleverit apud vulgus, ejusque peritiam viri docti dumtaxat habeant.”[^335]
More especially doth Benedict XIV of blessed memory forbid that in the Eastern Churches an already consecrated and dead language should be cast out and uprooted on the ground that the people no longer understand it because they now speak another living language; for thus he writeth: “Sed non in eo controversiae cardo vertitur. Illud quaeritur, an si, quae vernacula fuit, vulgari alio sermone in populum invecto doctorum dumtaxat virorum facta fuerit lingua, divina Officia et Missa in novum vulgi sermonem convertenda sint. Id vero est, a quo semper abhorruit et abhorret Catholica Ecclesia.”[^336]

§. 221.

Brief Explanation Concerning the Paschalion.[^337]

According to the statute of the Ecumenical Council of Nicaea (held in the year 325), the Feast of Pascha may occur on the days from March 22 unto April 25; for this Council decided “to celebrate the Pascha of Christ on the first Sunday following the full moon which occurreth either on the very day of the vernal equinox (March 21), or the first after it.” — If this full moon should occur on the very day of the vern-

<!-- LEAF: p310 -->

(p. 286)

al equinox (March 21) on a Saturday, then the Pascha of Christ may be celebrated on the following Sunday (March 22), for the Jews will celebrate their Passover on Saturday. — But if this full moon should occur on the very day of the vernal equinox on a Sunday, then, in order not to celebrate Pascha together with the Jews on that same Sunday, the Pascha of Christ is postponed unto the following Sunday. — The latest possible day of the Pascha of Christ is the 25th day of April, because the March full moon may occur before the equinox, which according to our Julian Menologion is March 20; on this account one must await the full moon after March 21 after the passage of 28 days; yet even then, if this 28th day should occur on a Sunday, then on account of the Jewish Passover the Christian Pascha is to be celebrated on the following Sunday after the passage of 7 days, and thus after the passage of 35 days, that is, the 25th of April.
These 35 paschal days — between March 22 and April 25 — are designated with the letters of the Cyrillic alphabet with the addition of the letter “Ѫ”[^338] and they are called the “paschal letters” or “boundary letters.” By means of these 35 letters the Paschalion is composed.[^339] And therefore if we wish to know when in any given year the Pascha of Christ and the movable seasons fall, we must first know what the “boundary key,” that is, the “paschal letter” is in that year; and knowing this letter, everything that we wish to know will be unlocked by this key, if only we look into the table. (See Table I.) For example: in the year 1891 the boundary key will be “ь”, therefore the Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee will be February 10; Pascha: April 21; the Descent of the Holy Spirit: June 9.
Besides this table, there are also other tables in which without any labor we can find the boundary key, for in these it is already given. (See Table II.) For example: for the year 1891 the boundary key is “ь”, for the year 1893: “ж”.
The question ariseth: how can this boundary key be found?
