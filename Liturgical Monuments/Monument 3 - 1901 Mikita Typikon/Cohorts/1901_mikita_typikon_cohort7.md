## § 20. From Whom Do the Church Books Originate?

In the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd centuries Christians probably had of church books only the Holy Scripture of the Old and New Testaments and the Psalter.

But in the 4th century, when the Christian faith was not only free, but also dominant: in the Church of Christ there probably was already the Horologion [Book of the Hours], the Leitourgikon [Sluzhebnik], and the Euchologion [Trebnik]. Those books were probably compiled by the great Hierarchs of the 4th century, especially by St. Basil the Great and St. John Chrysostom.

In the 5th century lived St. Anatolius († 458), Patriarch of Constantinople, who composed a multitude of stichera,[^43] and St. Romanus the Deacon († 500), the Melodist, who composed a multitude of kontakia, that is, short poetic hymns.

In the 6th century lived St. Sabbas († 532), Archimandrite of the “Great Lavra” on the torrent Cedron near Jerusalem, who composed a multitude of chants and canticles and one church order, which is now known under the name of the “Typikon of St. Sabbas.” — Besides this, he is also held to be the first compiler of the Menaion. He systematically compiled the Menaion according to oral tradition, which he received from his teachers St. Chariton (who lived in the time of Constantine), St. Euthymius († 473), and St. Theoctistus († 467),[^44] and prescribed it with obligation for his monks.

In the 7th century lived St. Sophronius († 638), Patriarch of Jerusalem, to whom they attribute the compilation of the Triodion and the enlargement of the Menaion; furthermore St. Andrew, Archbishop of Crete († 712), compiler especially of the Great Canon of Repentance and the canon for the Wednesday of Mid-Pentecost.

<!-- LEAF: p62 -->
In the 8th century lived St. John Damascene († 760), compiler of the Octoechos and enlarger of the Menaion; and St. Cosmas, at first a monk in the “Great Lavra” of Jerusalem, but later Bishop of Maiuma († 781). He composed, among others, the canon for the Descent of the Holy Spirit.

In the 9th century lived St. Theodore the Studite († 826), Archimandrite of the Monastery of Studium at Constantinople, and his brother Joseph († 845), Bishop of Thessalonica, who composed many canons and canticles. Some also attribute to them the compilation or enlargement of the Triodion.

Finally, in this same 9th century St. Theophanes, Archbishop of Nicaea († 858), and Joseph the Hymnographer, a monk in Constantinople († 883), compiled the services of Selected Saints, that is, they enlarged the Menaion.[^45]

# CHAPTER III.
## On Liturgical Expressions and Terminology in the Church Books
## § 21. On Liturgical Technical Terms in General

By technical terms we call those names by which we designate the psalms and poems, readings, liturgical actions and melodies, hymns, chants and canticles, parts and entire services used in our rite. — Those designations were received by the respective parts of our rite either according to text, or melody, or composition, or usage, or purpose, or according to their meaning. Thus the kontakion received this name from the text, for it is a short poem (*kontakion* [κοντάκιον] == *breviculum*); psalms are called antiphons from their melody; the polyeleos received this name from its composition, for therein the word *eleos* [ἔлеος] == *mercy* is found many times; the refrain [*pripiv*] is so called from its usage, for it is chanted before a troparion; the prokimenon is so named from its purpose, for by it the faithful are reminded of the reading of Holy Scripture; while the parastas is so called according to its meaning (*parastasis* [παράστασις] == *intercessio*), for by it we intercede for the departed.

## § 22. On Liturgical Technical Terms in Particular

1. **Order** [*Chyn*] (in Greek: *diataxis* [διάταξις]) is used as the order or disposition of some divine action (*ordo seu dispositio officii divini*).

2. **Service** [*Sluzhba*] (in Greek: *Akolouthia* [᾿Ακολουθία] == *ipsum officium*) is nothing other than the action itself arranged in an ordered sequence (*ipsum officium ordinatum denotat*).

3. **Psalms** are hymns taken from the Old Testament and are called psalms (in Greek: *psalmos* [ψάλμος]) from the word *psallein* [ψάλλειν], for the Hebrews, when singing them, accompanied them with music. Of the psalms the most renowned are: (α) Psalm 103 (“Bless the Lord, O my soul”), which is called the introductory psalm (in Greek: *prooimiakos* [προοιμιακὸς] == *prooimialis*), for with it the church rule beginneth; (β) Psalm 140 (“Lord, I have cried”), called the lucernarium psalm (in Greek: *ho epilychnios* [ὁ ἐπιλύχνιος] == *lucernarius*), and that because at the evening sacrifice of old that psalm was stichologized by the light of that candle which the deacon brought out and placed before the royal doors, or because at Vespers it is stichologized before the lighting of the evening sacrifice; (γ) Psalms 3, 37, 62, 87, 102, and 142 are called the “Six Psalms” [Hexapsalmos] (in Greek: *hexapsalmos* [ἑξάψαλμος], from the words *hex* [ἕξ] and *psalmos* [ψάλμος]); (δ) Psalm 118 (“Blessed are the undefiled”), called the blameless psalm (in Greek: *ho amomos* [ὁ ἄμωμος]), and that because therein is described the blessedness of the blameless man; (ε) Psalm 135 (“O give thanks unto the Lord”), called the polyeleos psalm (in Greek: *ho polyeleos* [ὁ πολυέλεος]), and this name deriveth from the fact that in those verses of the psalm the word “mercy” is frequently repeated (in Greek: *eleos* [ἔλεος]); (ϛ) Psalms 119–133 are called gradual psalms (in Greek: *anabathmoi* [ἀναβαθμοί] == *psalmi graduales*), and that because in the Psalter before Psalm 119 we find the inscription: “A Song of Ascents.” The Holy Fathers say that the Hebrews sang those psalms when they returned from the Babylonian captivity; others say that they sang them during prayer pilgrimages to Jerusalem at the 3 great feasts. Besides this, some also say that those psalms were sung by the Levites at the Feast of Tabernacles while standing upon the 15 steps which were placed in the temple between the court of the men and the court of the women. Some also

<!-- LEAF: p64 -->
call those psalms also the psalms “unto the Lord” (*ta pros Kyrion* [τὰ πρὸς Κύριον]), and that because the first of them beginneth with this phrase: “unto the Lord”; (η) Psalms 148, 149, and 150 are called the laudatory psalms, or the psalms at the Praises [Lauds] (*hoi ainoi* [οἱ αἶνοι]), from the word *aineite* [αἰνεῖτε] == “praise ye,” which is repeated in the verses of these psalms; (ϑ) Psalm 102 (“Bless the Lord, O my soul”) and Psalm 145 (“Praise the Lord, O my soul”) are called the Typika psalms (*hoi ton typikon psalmoi* [οἱ τῶν τυπικῶν ψαλμοί]), for they proclaim the prefiguration (*typon* [τύπον] == *figuram*) of the Messiah coming into the world. They are stichologized at the Typika [Obyednytsia].

4. **Kathisma** (from the Greek word *kathizo* [κατίζω] == to sit) signifieth “a sitting.” The Psalter, as was said, containeth 20 kathismata. A kathisma was used in church either by reading or by chanting (*recitando vel cantando*). The reading of the Psalter was called “stichology” (*stichologia* [στηχολογία]), while the chanting was called “psalmody.” — When they only read (*aneu melous* [ἄνευ μέλους]) the Psalter, they divided the kathisma into 3 staseis (*staseis* [στασεῖς] == *stationes*), that is, at three places during the reading they rested. But when they sang (*meta melous* [μετὰ μέλους]), they divided the kathisma into 3 melodies. They read in such a manner that either one read with a single voice, or responsively [in two choirs]. But they chanted the kathisma in such a manner that: (α) either all sang together (*symphonia* [συμφωνία]), or (β) two choirs sang antiphonally (*antiphonia* [ἀνтиφωνία]), or (γ) two sang antiphonally and someone besides this was the leader (*hypophonia* [ὑποφωνία]),[^46] that is, the precentor (*hypoboleus* [ὑποβολεύς]) sang alone, and the others responded to him, either by repeating the final words of the psalm, or with another verse.

5. **Antiphon** (in Greek: *antiphonon* [ἀντιφωνῶν]) signifieth nothing other than “responsive word” (*vox opposita, seu ejusdem vocis oppositio vel rectius ejusdem sententiae ad singulos versus psalmi repetitio*), that is: a corresponding and counter melody, or the repetition of a single divine thought throughout the verses of a psalm. Of the psalms, those which were chanted with a corresponding and antiphonal melody are called “antiphonal psalms,” or simply “Antiphons.” An antiphon in a nar-

<!-- LEAF: p65 -->
rower sense consisteth of 2 or 3 verses of a Psalm with “Glory” and “Now,” and of one refrain. Antiphonal psalms are the following: (α) Gradual psalms; (β) “Typika psalms” sung at the Typika [Obyednytsia]; (γ) “Liturgical psalms,” and those antiphons are either daily or festal. Daily antiphons are: Psalm 91 (“It is good to give praise unto the Lord”), thereafter Psalm 92 (“The Lord reigneth”), and Psalm 94 (“Come, let us rejoice in the Lord”). Of feasts, only the Feasts of Pascha, Ascension, Descent of the Holy Spirit, Transfiguration, Exaltation, Nativity of Jesus Christ, Theophany, and Meeting of our Lord in Jerusalem have proper antiphons. On Sunday and on other feasts it would be proper to sing the antiphonal psalms of the Typika, that is, Psalm 102 (“Bless the Lord, O my soul”), Psalm 145 (“Praise the Lord, O my soul”), and instead of the 3rd “antiphonal psalm” it would be proper to sing stichera composed on the individual verses of the evangelical Beatitudes spoken by Jesus Christ upon the mount in Matthew chapter 5, and therefore those are named “stichera on the Beatitudes.” But among us instead of those, Sunday antiphons are sung on Sundays.

6. **Paroemia** (in Greek: *paroimia* [παροιμία] == *parabola, proverbium*). Paroemias are called readings from the Holy Scripture taken for the most part from the Old Testament and containing within themselves either prophecies concerning the event commemorated on that day, or explaining the event of the occurring feast, or containing praise thereof. But readings taken from the New Testament are called “Epistles” [*Apostles*] or “Gospels.” Paroemias are read: (α) At Great Vespers on Feasts of the Lord, of the Mother of God, and of Great Saints, usually 3; (β) In the Great Fast at Vespers, especially those combined with the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts,[^47] only 2; (γ) At Vespers combined with the Liturgy of St. Basil the Great, for example, on the Eves of the Nativity, of Theophany, and on Great Saturday, more; (δ) Besides this, they are also read at the Royal Hours, that is, on the Eve of the Nativity, of Theophany, and on Great Friday, but there at a single hour only one paroemia is taken.

7. **The Canticles of Moses**. Concerning these canticles it is necessary to note that they are taken from various books of the Holy Scripture of the Old and New Testaments,

<!-- LEAF: p66 -->
namely: (α) Ode 1 is taken from Exodus 15:1–19; this canticle is that which, after passing through the Red Sea, Moses and the children of Israel sang thus: “Let us sing unto the Lord, for He hath been gloriously glorified”; (β) Ode 2 is taken from the Book of Deuteronomy, chapter 32, which Moses cried aloud at the end of his life, when he had led the people through the wilderness, tactics: “Give heed, O heaven, and I will speak”; (γ) Ode 3 is taken from 1 Kings [1 Samuel], chapter 2, and is the canticle of Hannah, mother of the Prophet Samuel, which she thus proclaimed: “My heart is established in the Lord”; (δ) Ode 4 is taken from the prophecy of Habakkuk, chapter 3, who having seen God coming forth from the shaded mountain, cried aloud thus: “Lord, I have heard Thy report, and was afraid”; (ε) Ode 5 is taken from the book of the Prophet Isaiah, chapter 26, who having foretold the birth of Emmanuel from the Virgin, cried aloud thus: “Early in the morning my spirit seeketh Thee, O God”; (ϛ) Ode 6 is taken from the prophecy of Jonah, chapter 2:1–7, which he cried aloud when after his three-day stay in the belly of the whale he reached dry land, thus: “I cried in mine affliction unto the Lord God”; (η) Ode 7 is taken from the Book of Daniel, chapter 3, which the Three Youths cried aloud in the Babylonian furnace, saying thus: “Blessed art Thou, O Lord God of our fathers”; (ϑ) Ode 8 is likewise taken thence and containeth the thanksgiving canticle of those same youths delivered from the fiery furnace, which they cried aloud thus: “Bless the Lord, all ye works of the Lord; sing and exalt Him above all forever”; (ι) Ode 9 is taken from the Gospel of St. Luke, chapter 1:68–79, which the Prophet Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, cried aloud when his son was born, thus: “Blessed be the Lord God of Israel,” etc. At the beginning of this 9th Ode is found also the Canticle of the Theotokos in Luke, chapter 1:46, which she, bearing in her womb the Saviour, cried aloud thus: “My soul doth magnify the Lord, and my spirit hath rejoiced in God my Saviour.” — These Canticles of Moses are very important, for they are the foundation for the canons of the Sunday service. — Now they are read before the odes of the canon only in the Great Fast. Besides this, Ode 1 and Ode 8 are also sung on Great Saturday at Vespers between the 15 paroemias with beautiful hypophonia. Furthermore, it is necessary to note that when there was not yet a Sunday canon in the Church of Christ, these canticles were probably used.

8. **A Verse** [*Stikh*] (in Greek: *stichos* [στίχος] == *versiculus*) is a short chant, taken either from the psalms, or from the Holy Scripture of the New or Old Testament, proclaimed before the stichera according to the melody of the

<!-- LEAF: p67 -->
stichera. Verses are either taken, or given [prescribed], or antiphonal. (α) Taken verses are verses from the evening psalms at “Lord, I have cried” and from the psalms at the Praises [Lauds] at Matins and from the Beatitudes of Jesus Christ;[^48] (β) Given verses are according to the dogmatic significance of the feasts and rites at Vespers, at the aposticha, at Compline, at the Litiya, at Matins at the aposticha, and at the Royal Hours, and on various occasions;[^49] (γ) Besides these there are also antiphonal verses, of which the most renowned are those verses which are sung at Matins after the Polyeleos at the exaltations [megalynaria], where after the verses there is for the most part the refrain “We magnify thee,” furthermore at “My soul doth magnify the Lord,” where “More honorable than the Cherubim” is sung, or at the Liturgy at the antiphons, where after the verses “By the prayers of the Mother of God” or “Save us, O Son of God” is repeated.

9. **The Prokimenon** (in Greek: *prokeimenon distichon* [προκείμενον δίσтиχον] == *praevium distichon*, from the word *prokeimai* [πρόκειμαι] == *propositus sum vel positus sum ante*) is a two-fold verse, which is taken for the most part from the psalms, and is set forth before the reading of Holy Scripture and remindeth the faithful of Holy Scripture, or of some extraordinary chant, the subject of which it containeth within itself in brief (*complexive*). Prokimena regarding their usage are: (α) Vespers prokimena, which are used at Vespers both great and small; (β) Matins prokimena, which are taken only at great Matins; (γ) Liturgical prokimena, which are used at Liturgies; (δ) Finally, prokimena according to various services and needs. — Both movable and immovable feasts have their proper prokimena. Nevertheless, of the prokimena

<!-- LEAF: p68 -->
prescribed for movable feasts the most renowned are: the daily prokimena used at Vespers throughout the entire week;[^50] furthermore the Sunday prokimena sung at Matins according to the 8 tones, and the Sunday prokimena used at Liturgies according to the 8 tones.[^51] — If in the church books at evening divine services we find this phrase: “Prokimenon of the day” (*prokeimenon tes hemeras* [προκείμενον τῆς ἡμέρας]), then under this we always understand the movable day, that is, Monday, Tuesday, etc., whose prokimenon we take. As regards melody, prokimena are divided into: (α) Great, and (β) Small. Great prokimena are sung with a longer melody, while small ones are sung with a shorter melody.

10. **Stichera** (in Greek: *sticheron* [стиχηρὸν] == *versus*) are strophes of a single poem composed according to dogmatic significance or according to the propriety of the feast, before which a short verse is sung, and that in such wise that the subject of this verse is explained, amplified, or interpreted in the sticheron. Stichera according to liturgical usage are stichera: (α) At Vespers at “Lord, I have cried” and at the Aposticha; (β) At Compline; (γ) At the Litiya; (δ) At Matins at the Praises [Lauds] and at the Aposticha; (ε) At the Royal Hours; and (ϛ) At the Typika [Obyednytsia]. As regards melody they are sung according to the 8 tones, and the tones of the stichera are designated in the church books. Of stichera according to their dogmatic significance the most renowned are: (α) Resurrectional (*anastasima* [ἀναστάσιμα]), in which the Resurrection of Christ is glorified; (β) Cross-resurrectional (*stauroanastasima* [σταυροαναστάσιма]), in which the Cross and Resurrection of Christ are glorified; (γ) Triadica (*triadika* [тριαδικὰ]), composed in honor of the Holy Trinity; (δ) Theotokia (*theotokia* [θεοτόκια]), composed in honor of the Most Holy Theotokos; (ε) Stavrotheotokia (*staurotheotokia* [σταυροθεοτόκια]), in honor of the compassions [sorrows] of the Mother of God; (ϛ) Martyria (*martyrika* [μαρτυρικὰ]), composed in honor of the holy martyrs; (η) Stichera of Pascha, composed in honor of the Feast of Pascha; (ϑ) Stichera of the departed (*nekrosima* [νεκρώσιма]), wherein supplications are set forth for the deceased; (ι) Eastern stichera (*anatolika* [ἀνατολικὰ]), which it is necessary to chant before the rising of the sun. (κ) Besides these, most especially worthy of note are the so-called “Evangelical Stichera,” of which there are 11 and which are sung at Sunday Matins (at the Praises [Lauds], and that at “Glory”), and which are composed according to the 11 Matins Gospels, being in union with them as regards their subject matter.

<!-- LEAF: p69 -->
11. **An Automelon [Idiomelon]** (in Greek: *idiomelon* [ἰδιόμελον]) is such a sticheron that was not chanted according to the melody of either a troparion or a heirmos, but had its own proper and solemn melody (*idion echon melon* [ἴδιον ἔχον μέλος]), and therefore was not chanted with a verse, but without it. Of the automela [idiomela] the most renowned are those whose melody in the 8 tones was probably found for the first time in Bulgaria, and therefore they called them “Bulgarian” [*Bolgar*] chants.

12. **Prosomoia** [Model-stichera], that is, stichera (*stichera prosomoia* [στιχηρὰ προσόμοια]), are such original stichera after whose pattern other stichera are metrically composed, and according to whose melody others also are sung.

13. **A Troparion** (in Greek: *troparion* [троπάριον]). Some derive this name from the text, and others from the melody. Of those who explain that name from the text, that is, according to its meaning, there are some who derive this name from the Greek word *tropos* [τρόπος] == a manner of living (*modus vivendi*), for in troparia is contained the manner of life of a saint, or a description of the feast. Others again derive it from the Greek word *tropaios* [τρόπαιος] == a trophy of victory (*signum victoriae*), because in troparia the victory of a saint is glorified, or the triumph of a feast. Others again derive it in a metaphorical sense from the word *tropos* [τρόπος] == an image (*imago, figura*), and that because in troparia a saint is represented under various figures, such as: a ray, a star, etc. And finally, some derive it from the word *trepein* [тρέπειν] == to turn (*vertere*), because a troparion hath the aim to incline the faithful unto such a life as is represented to us in troparia. Those who explain it according to melody likewise derive it from *trepein* [тρέπειν] == to turn, because those poems with respect to melody are turned in various manners, that is, unto the tones, unto prosomoia, and unto other models. Moreover, troparia are either short hymns or strophes of a single long poem which they sang in church without accompaniment of music. And according to this, they are divided into two parts, into “independent” and into “subordinate.” 1) A troparion as independent is a short hymn chanted in church, wherein is expressed the essence of the day, of the feast, or of the intention, or the virtue-filled life of a saint is described. One may also in this sense call a troparion a principal or ruling chant (*canticum principale vel regens*). Independent troparia have 8 melodies (tones), and a single troparion hath only one, and not two melodies, unto which it is necessary each time to turn, as many times as the troparion is sung. Independent troparia

<!-- LEAF: p70 -->
are given both on movable feasts and on immovable feasts, both on Sundays and on weekdays; besides this, diverse intentions also have their proper troparia. Of troparia the most renowned are: the 8 Sunday troparia in the 8 tones chanted throughout 8 weeks. Every single troparion is joined with a Theotokion troparion, because according to the custom accepted in the Greek Church we at all prayers magnify the Mother of God and entrust ourselves unto her mighty intercession. Besides this, independent troparia are joined with a kontakion, an ikos, a synaxarion, and with a hypakoe.
2) Subordinate troparia are parts of a single poem; such are:
(α) Strophes of the odes of a canon, which after the pattern of the first strophe (that is, the heirmos) are metrically composed, and according to the melody of the first strophe (that is, the heirmos) it is necessary to chant them;[^52]
(β) Sessional hymns, or kathisma-troparia, subordinate unto the kathismata, because at the beginning of Matins, between the kathismata, they are chanted or read, and therefore are called sessional troparia (*versus sedendarii*), for when they read a kathisma and chanted those troparia, it was permitted to sit;[^53]
(γ) Exaposteilaria [troparia photagogika], subordinate unto the Sunday Matins Gospels (τροπάρια φωтаγώγικα), of which there are 11, just as there are 11 Gospels and 11 Evangelical Stichera;
(δ) Dismissal Theotokia (τροπάρια ἀπολυτίκια), by which a conclusion is set unto a service or unto some part of divine worship.

14. **A Kontakion** (in Greek: *kontakion* [κοντάкιον] == *breviculum*, from *kontos* [κονтὸς] == *brevis*). It is so called either from the text, for it is a short hymn (*hymnus brevis*), or from the melody (*en konto* [ἐν κονтῷ] == *breviter*), for it was necessary to chant it in a short melody (*brevis modulus*). Kontakia are in union with independent troparia and serve for the fulfillment of the thoughts composed in troparia.

15. **An Ikos** (in Greek: *oikos* [οἶкоς] == *domus vel imago*) is a hymn chanted in church which expresseth the glory and significance of a feast or the praise of a saint more extensively than a kontakion and in figures. An ikos is the counterpart of a kontakion, for a kontakion describeth a feast for us in brief, while an ikos describeth it in its fullness (*amplius et diffusius*). An ikos always followeth after a kontakion and is never read without a kontakion.
