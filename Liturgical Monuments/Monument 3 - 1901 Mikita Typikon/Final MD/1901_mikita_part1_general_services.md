# INTRODUCTION

## § 1. Concept, Principles, and Division of the Typikon

Since the worship of God is not only internal, but must also be manifested externally, holy Mother Church hath therefore established and appointed external services for the worship of God. A service is nothing other: *quam veritatis religioso-theoreticae practica professio* [than the practical profession of religious-theoretical truth], that is, the practical confession of theoretical supernatural truth. For example, Baptism is the confession of redemption and justification.

The services that are prescribed by the Church for Sundays and weekdays, as well as for feast days and ordinary days, are compiled in their completeness in the ecclesiastical books; they are divided into private and public; they consist of multiple parts; and these parts consist of diverse liturgical actions, symbols, and chants.

Public services are divided into 3 parts: (α) Into pre-liturgical (*ante-liturgicae*), that is, those preceding the Divine Liturgy, and these are: Vespers, Compline, the Midnight Office, Matins, the Hours, and Typika (*Obidnytsia*)[^3]; (β) Into liturgical (*ceremoniae liturgicae*), concerning the three Liturgies, namely: of St. John Chrysostom, of St. Basil the Great, and of St. Gregory the Dialogist; (γ) And into services following the Divine Liturgy, and these are of the Holy Mysteries, Ecclesiastical Sanctifications, and Blessings (*ceremoniae Sacramentorum et Sacramentalium*).

The pastor must celebrate the holy services: (α) Intelligibly, that is, with distinct pronunciation and with a decent voice; (β) In godly fear, that is, with an interior spirit for the profit of the people; (γ) In unity with other pastors and with ecclesiastical ordinances; therefore the Council of Trent states: “If anyone should say that the received and approved rites of the Church

=== LEAF p26 ===
[Book Page 2]

may be omitted by any pastor at his own pleasure without sin, or changed into other new ones, let him be anathema.”[^4] (δ) Uniformly, that is, without any diminution or addition, and at that very time which hath been appointed by the eparchial authority.

The systematic collection of all church services constitutes a rite (*complexus ordinatus omnium ceremoniarum est ritus*). The Typikon (*in Greek Τυπικὸν, from the word τύπος = forma, praescriptio, regula* [form, prescription, rule]): (α) Objectively is the collection of all those prescriptions, that is, rules, according to which the sacred services must be celebrated (*est complexus regularum, juxta quas ceremoniae administrari possunt ac debent* [is the collection of rules according to which ceremonies can and ought to be administered]); (β) Subjectively is the science which anyone acquires for himself if he learns those prescriptions, that is, the rules. Those prescriptions are taken either: (α) from the church books; or (β) from the decrees of the Congregation of Sacred Rites (*Congregatio SS. Rituum*); or (γ) from customary law (*consuetudo*).

Just as services are divided into 3 parts, so also prescriptions are divided into 3 parts, namely: (α) into prescriptions concerning pre-liturgical services; (β) into prescriptions concerning liturgical services; and (γ) into prescriptions concerning services of the Holy Mysteries and other Ecclesiastical Sanctifications and Blessings.

In accordance with this, we also divide our Typikon first into 2 principal parts: and namely, in Part I we shall speak concerning services in general, and in Part II concerning services in particular. This Part II we furthermore divide into 3 sections: and namely, in Section I we shall speak concerning pre-liturgical services; in Section II concerning liturgical services; and in Section III concerning services of the Holy Mysteries and other sacred actions.

## CHAPTER I. § 2. On the Ecclesiastical Menologion in General

Just as in the Old Testament the people of Israel had a twofold year: (α) the civil year, which began with the month of Tishri in autumn; and (β) the ecclesiastical year, which began with the month of Nisan in spring: so also in the New Testament the Christian people have a twofold year: (α) the civil year, which begins on the first day of January; and (β) the ecclesiastical year, which begins on the 1st day of September.

The ecclesiastical year consists of: (α) Days dedicated to the worship of God, and specifically to the worship of the most important acts of God concerning the redemption of the human race, that is, Feasts of the Lord; (β) Days dedicated to the veneration of the Most Holy Virgin Mary, that is, Marian Feasts; (γ) Days dedicated to the veneration of the Saints, that is, Feasts of the Saints. But since every greater feast is already announced beforehand by holy Church, and holy Mother Church is occupied with it for a considerable time even after its celebration, therefore: (δ) the ecclesiastical year consists also of Forefeasts and Afterfeasts; (ε) Furthermore, it consists of days which holy Mother Church hath established for the repentance and sanctification of the human race, that is, fasting days; (ζ) And finally, it consists of days on which mankind entreats God for those who have “fallen asleep in faith and in the hope of resurrection,” that is, days for the departed.

The focal point of the entire ecclesiastical year is the Resurrection of Christ, even as the Resurrection of Christ is the seal of the redemption of the human race. “And if Christ be not risen,” saith the Apostle, “then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain” (1 Cor. 15:14). And therefore His memorial is celebrated every week on Sunday, and all other feasts receive their brightness and warmth from this feast; in a word: what the sun is among the stars, that the Feast of Pascha is among the other feasts.

=== LEAF p28 ===
[Book Page 4]

The sequential order, that is, the arrangement of the church year according to diverse feasts, is called the “Menologion” (*Misiatsoslov*), in Greek: “*Heortologion*” [Ἑορтоλόγιον].

Furthermore, feasts are variously divided,[^5] namely:

1. In regard to time (*ratione temporis*): (α) into those which do not fall in the Menologion at the same time or on that same day, but their celebration depends upon Pascha, which falls on one of the Sundays between the 22nd day of March and the 25th day of April, and therefore these are called movable feasts; (β) into those which fall in each year in the Menologion on that same day and on that same date of the month, and therefore are called immovable feasts.
2. In regard to exterior sanctification (*ratione exterioris sanctificationis*), they are divided: (α) into holy days of obligation (*festa fori*), on which the pastor is bound to celebrate the Divine Liturgy for the people (*praeceptum missam pro populo applicare*), and the people are bound to hear the Divine Liturgy (*praeceptum missam audire*); (β) into feast days of devotion (*festa chori*), on which the pastor is bound to celebrate the Divine Liturgy for the people, but the people nevertheless are not bound to hear the Divine Liturgy; (γ) into ordinary days, on which neither the pastor is bound to celebrate the Divine Liturgy for the people, nor are the people bound to hear the Divine Liturgy.
3. In regard to the object celebrated (*ratione objecti celebrati*), they are divided: (α) into Feasts of the Lord (*cultus latriae* [worship of adoration]); (β) into Marian Feasts (*cultus hyperdouliae* [veneration of highest honour]); and (γ) into Feasts of the Saints (*cultus douliae* [veneration of honour]).
4. In regard to the liturgical rite (*ratione ritus liturgici*), into: (α) great; (β) middle; and (γ) lesser feasts. — Great feasts have Great Vespers with 10 or 8 stichera with an entrance, and sometimes with a Litiya; Matins with a Polyeleos, Gospel, and the Great Doxology. Middle feasts have Vespers with 8 stichera with an entrance, but without a Litiya; and Matins with a Polyeleos, Gospel, and the Great Doxology. Lesser feasts have Vespers with 6 stichera, but without an entrance; at Matins there is no Polyeleos, the canon is on 6, but they have the Great Doxology, and therefore are called “doxology feasts” (*doxologoumena* [δοξоλογούμενα]).

Besides feast days there are also non-festal days, on which we celebrate the memory of lesser Saints having 3 stichera at Vespers and a canon on 4 at Matins.

## § 3. On the Menologion of Movable Feasts

Pascha is the focal point of the movable feasts. It can fall from March 22 unto April 25, that is, on one of these 35 days. — The “Paschalia”[^6] is the unfailing index of the day of the Pascha of Christ and of all festal days which depend upon Pascha. — In the Paschalia, the greatest importance is held by the boundary key (*kliuch hranychnyi*) or boundary letter. The boundary keys are letters of the alphabet which show on which day and month, in what year, the Pascha of Christ will fall. — Thus the earliest day of Pascha, March 22, hath the key A, March 23 hath B, and so forth in order. The last and latest day of Pascha, April 25, hath the key Ѿ (*Ot*).

Together with the boundary key there is also found the Dominical letter (*vrutsilito*, or Dominical letters, Sunday letter), and they are seven in number. These are alphabetical letters taken as numbers to designate the 7 days of the week: A, V, H, D, E, S, Z [А, В, Г, Д, Е, Ѕ, З].

The boundary key begins with the Nativity of Christ and governs throughout the entire year. And therefore, if we wish to know when in any given year the movable seasons fall, it is necessary to know beforehand what the boundary key is in that year; and knowing the key, everything that we wish to know will be unlocked by that key, if we only look in the tables at what stands opposite the governing key. The Dominical letter (*vrutsilito*), however, doth not begin together with the boundary key; it begins with the 1st day of March and governs unto the last day of February of the following year. — Therefore, the Sunday days in January and February of that second year stand in the Menologia always under the Dominical letter of the preceding year. The Menologion of movable feasts consists of: 1) the Forefeast of Pascha itself; 2) the Feast of Pascha; 3) the Afterfeast of Pascha; and 4) the 32 weeks after the Descent of the Holy Spirit, which are called “ordinary.”

I. To the Forefeast of Pascha belong: (α) The Sunday of the Publican and the Pharisee, with the following week called “fast-free” (*pospolytsia*, *zahalnytsia*, *hyrtsia*),

## § 4. On the Menologion of Immovable Feasts

Of the immovable feasts we shall adduce only the most important. The focal point among these feasts is the Nativity according to the flesh of our Lord Jesus Christ, with which many of the immovable feasts stand in close connection. The Menologion of immovable feasts begins with the 1st day of September and ends with the 31st day of August.

I. In September the most notable of the immovable feasts are: (α) On the 1st: The Beginning of the Indiction, that is, of the New Year. The name “Indiction” is the Latin word *indictio*, *initium et finis* [beginning and end]. It is a period consisting of 15 years, which period was divided into three parts (*lustrum*), after which the Roman Emperors decreed a tax upon their subjects in the first days of each five-year period, and collected the same in the first days of each five-year period. The Church begins the new year on this day either because on this day Jesus came into the Jewish synagogue and there began to preach, and having taken the book of the Prophet Isaiah, read from it that place where it is written: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me” (Isa. 61:1, *Initium indictionis ab initio

=== LEAF p34 ===
[Book Page 10]

...praedicationis Jesu* [The beginning of the Indiction from the beginning of the preaching of Jesus]). Others, however, consider that the Church begins the new year on that day because, according to tradition, time began on that day (*Initium indictionis a principio temporis* [The beginning of the Indiction from the beginning of time]); (β) On the 6th: “Commemoration of the Miracle Wrought at Colossae by the Archistrategos Michael.” This miracle was that the pagans wished to destroy the church of the holy Archistrategos Michael by means of a river which they directed against the church; but the holy Archangel Michael appeared, and striking the rock with his staff, made a passage for the river, which even now flows through this rock; (γ) On the 8th: The Nativity of the Most Holy Theotokos; (δ) On the 14th: The Exaltation of the Precious Cross. On the 16th: St. Josaphat; (ε) On the 26th: The Falling Asleep of the Holy Apostle and Evangelist John the Theologian; (ϛ) On the 28th: Our Venerable Father Chariton.

II. In the month of October the most notable feasts are the following: (α) On the 1st: The “Protection of the Most Holy Theotokos,” instituted in memory of how St. Andrew the Fool-for-Christ and his disciple Epiphanius, during the All-Night Vigil on October 1 in the year 1309, beheld in the air in Constantinople, in the Church of Blachernae, the Mother of God with Angels, Prophets, and Apostles, praying for the world and covering Christians with her veil. ## § 10. Which Are the Great Feasts?

Great feasts are divided into 3 classes: (α) To the first belongs only Pascha, which is the “feast of feasts”; (β) To the second belong the 12 feasts: Nativity of the Theotokos, Entry, Annunciation, Dormition of the Most Holy Virgin, Nativity of Jesus Christ, Meeting, Theophany, Transfiguration, Entry into Jerusalem, Ascension, Descent of the Holy Spirit, and Exaltation of the Precious Cross; (γ) To the third class belong: The Circumcision of the Lord, Nativity and Beheading of John the Baptist, and the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul.

=== LEAF p40 ===
[Book Page 16]

Great feasts are designated in the Menologion by an enclosed cross 🞣, and the titles are usually printed in red letters. They are Feasts of the Lord and Marian Feasts. All these feasts have Great Vespers with a Litiya, and Matins with a Polyeleos, Gospel, and Great Doxology; besides this they have a Forefeast and Afterfeast, that is, the divine service of those feasts is not limited to the very day of the feast, but extends unto preceding and following days. For the most part, great feasts have one day of Forefeast each; only the Feast of the Nativity of Christ hath 5 days, and the Feast of Theophany 4 days. The Circumcision and the other feasts of the 3rd class do not have a Forefeast.

The Afterfeast likewise is not always the same, but is at times longer, at times shorter. — Thus the Afterfeast consists of one day after the Annunciation of the Most Holy Theotokos; of four days after the Nativity of the Theotokos and the Entry into the Temple of the Theotokos; or of 6 days after the Nativity of Christ; or of 7 days after the Exaltation of the Precious Cross and after the Transfiguration of the Lord; or of 8 days after Theophany and after the Dormition of the Most Holy Theotokos. The Feast of the Circumcision and the other feasts of the 3rd class do not have an Afterfeast.

The last day of an Afterfeast is called the “Apodosis of the feast,” because on that day the celebration of the feast itself is for the most part taken. All Apodoses are “doxology” feasts.

## § 13. What Must Be Noted Concerning Sunday, Weekday, Fasting, Fast-Free, and Requiem Days?

Sunday is dedicated to the Resurrection of Christ; of all Sundays there are 52. They are reckoned either after the Resurrection, 1st, 2nd, etc., or after the Descent of the Holy Spirit, 1st, 2nd, etc., or 1st, 2nd, etc., of the Fast.

=== LEAF p48 ===
[Book Page 24]

Monday is dedicated to the Angels; Tuesday to St. John the Baptist; Wednesday and Friday to the Precious Cross; Thursday to the Apostles and St. Nicholas; Saturday to All Saints and the Departed.

On Wednesday and on Friday there is a fast; on Saturday and Sunday there is no fast even in the Great Fast itself, but only abstinence, with the exception of Great Saturday.[^29] For thus saith Apostolic Canon 66: *“Si quis clericorum deprehendatur die Dominico jejunans aut Sabbato, praeter unum solum (nempe Sabbatum Sanctum) deponatur, si vero laicus segregetur”* [“If any cleric be found fasting on the Lord’s day or on the Sabbath, except only one (namely Holy Saturday), let him be deposed, but if a layman, let him be segregated”].[^30] For Saturday is the day of creation, but Sunday is the day of redemption.

Fasts are: 1. Before the Nativity of Jesus Christ, from November 15 unto December 24 inclusive; 2. Before the Resurrection of Christ, from Cheesefare Sunday unto the Resurrection; 3. Of the Holy Apostles Peter and Paul, from the Sunday of All Saints unto the feast of Peter and Paul; 4. # CHAPTER II

## § 14. On Church Books in General

Church books are either public or private, that is, intended either for public or for private use. — Public books are those which are approved for public use by the Church herself; private books are smaller books taken from one or more public books for the benefit of the clergy or the faithful, and they are approved only by one or another bishop.

It is not permitted to use unapproved books or books approved by schismatics in church; so commands Benedict XIV of blessed memory, who in his constitution *“Etsi pastoralis”* speaks thus: *“Quia in ecclesiasticis Graecorum libris nonnulli errores irrepsere, quibus primaevam Ecclesiae Graecae puritatem fraude potissimum ac dolo schismaticorum corruptam esse orthodoxi dolent, Ordinariorum locorum vigilantiam excitamus, ut nonnisi probatorum librorum usum admittant”* [“Because into the ecclesiastical books of the Greeks several errors have crept, whereby the orthodox grieve that the pristine purity of the Greek Church hath been corrupted chiefly by the fraud and deceit of schismatics, We arouse the vigilance of the local Ordinaries, that they admit the use of none but approved books”].[^32]

1. The Horologion (in Greek: *Horologion* [Ὡроλόγιον], from the word: *hora* [ὥра] = *hora* [hour]), which is so called because the services contained therein had to be completed daily according to appointed hours (*per horas*), according to the words of the Psalmist: “Seven times a day have I praised Thee for the judgements of Thy righteousness” (Ps. 118:164 [LXX]).
