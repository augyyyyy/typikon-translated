[^195]: After the “Litany of the Catechumens,” “Wisdom” is pronounced twice: when it is pronounced the first time, all the faithful are thereby aroused to show themselves worthy, for from this time begins the accomplishment of the Mystery; but when “Wisdom” is pronounced the second time, those who desire to receive communion of the Holy Mysteries are thereby aroused unto a pure conscience.
[^196]: The Cherubic Hymn was composed probably by Saint Justin Martyr.
[^197]: *Dorinosiat* [Дориносятъ] = that is, they bear aloft upon spears. This expression is taken from the times of the Roman emperors, and hath this meaning: when among the Romans a new emperor was chosen, they set him upon a shield, and the soldiers raised him aloft on that shield upon spears and bore him about among all the legions, that all might recognize him and render him homage: thus do the Angels bear Christ upon shields, whom here the priest with the deacon represent, carrying the Holy Gifts among the people unto the holy Altar-Table.
[^198]: It is customary that the faithful kneel at the Great Entrance. The Synod of Zamość forbids this, saying: “The Holy Synod forbids kneeling or bowing the head when the bread of oblation is transferred from the Table of Preparation unto the Altar-Table at the time of the Great Entrance, and commands pastors to instruct the people concerning this, that they be not exposed to the danger of idolatry” (Sess. III, tit. III, § 4, p. 90). See Pelesz, *Pastoral Theology*, p. 493.
[^199]: The Great Entrance symbolically signifies the entry of Jesus Christ from Bethany into Jerusalem unto His passion and death; but since Jesus Christ before His death foretold both His passion and His resurrection: therefore also at His entrance His taking down from the cross and His laying in the tomb are figuratively depicted.
[^200]: If there are several concelebrants at the Divine Liturgy, then after manifesting love toward God they manifest also their mutual love, kissing one another upon the shoulder, whereat the superior saith: “Christ is in our midst!” and the concelebrants answer: “He is and shall be!” See Pelesz, *Pastoral Theology*, p. 494.
[^201]: The deacon’s exclamation: “The doors! The doors!” etc. had in ancient times this meaning, that by these words the deacon summoned the ostiarii [door-keepers] standing at the doors unto vigilance, lest any unbelievers should enter into the church at that time when the faithful confess their faith, and when the holy sacrifice is about to be accomplished.
[^202]: Some say that the words: “singing, shouting, crying aloud, and saying” signify the four quarters of the earth, that is, east, west, south, and north, in which the Word of God is proclaimed; but Saint Germanus saith that by these expressions are understood those living creatures that were present unto the four Evangelists, for he saith: “The singing one is the eagle, the shouting one the ox, the crying one the lion, and the saying one the man” (See the Prophecy of Ezekiel 1:10).
[^203]: During the singing of “Holy, Holy, Holy,” as also during the words that consecrate and transubstantiate the bread into the Body and the wine into the Blood of Jesus Christ, and at the exclamation “Thine own of Thine own,” the church bell is rung, and the people fall upon their knees and render homage unto Jesus Christ, Who hath come to give Himself unto us as food under the appearances of bread and wine.
[^204]: *Haec oratio vocatur „ἐπίκλησις“ seu „invocatio Spiritus Sancti“, in qua oratione Sacerdos implorat Spiritum Sanctum, non simpliciter, ut faciat panem corpus, et quod est in calice sanguinem, sed ut fiat nobis ad fructum. Mutata enim substantia panis et vini in Corpus et Sanguinem Christi per verba consecratoria et confecto vero corpore Christi, necesse est, ut etiam nos, quos accidentia panis et vini repraesentant, per Spiritum Sanctum in mysticum corpus Christi coadunemur juxta dicta S. Pauli: „Unum corpus multi sumus, omnes, qui de uno pane participamus“* (I. Corinth. X. 17) [“This prayer is termed the ‘epiklesis’ or ‘invocation of the Holy Spirit,’ in which prayer the priest imploreth the Holy Spirit, not simply that He make the bread the Body and that which is in the chalice the Blood, but that it may be for us unto fruit. For the substance of the bread and wine having been changed into the Body and Blood of Christ through the consecratory words, and the true Body of Christ having been confected, it is necessary that we also, whom the accidents of bread and wine represent, should through the Holy Spirit be united together into the mystical Body of Christ according to the words of St. Paul: ‘For we, being many, are one bread and one body, all of us who partake of that one bread’” (1 Cor. 10:17)].
[^205]: Here the priest first of all commemorates those (living or departed) unto whom he desires to apply the fruits of the offered sacrifice (*fructus sacrificii*), and only thereafter the others whom he wishes. In ancient times the deacon read aloud the commemorations for the living and departed from the book called the “Diptychs” (*liber diptychorum*). Diptychs were two tablets folded together, in which on one side the names of the living were written, and on the other side the names of the departed, which were necessary to be commemorated at the sacred action.
[^206]: The core of the Divine Liturgy is termed the *canon Missae* or *anaphora* (ἀναφορά = *actio, secretum*).
[^207]: Saint Cyril of Jerusalem thus interprets these words: “The holy things are the gifts set forth, having received the descent of the Holy Spirit; and ye are holy, having been accounted worthy of the Holy Spirit. Holy things therefore are proper for the holy.”
[^208]: “The fullness of the Holy Spirit” represents the union of the soul and Body of Christ, or His most glorious Resurrection.
[^209]: If, however, several priests concelebrate, then in the first place one after another they approach the holy Altar-Table in an orderly manner, where the presiding priest distributes unto them particles of the Holy Body, saying: “Christ is in our midst!” and after this all together say the prayer: “I believe, O Lord, and I confess...” etc.; likewise in turn they receive communion also from the chalice. But if a Bishop celebrates, he saith: “Priests, draw near”; and the priests, drawing near, say the prayer: “Lo, I draw near unto the Immortal King; forgive me, Holy Master, and bless, and impart unto me the precious, holy, and most pure Body (and for the Blood: the precious, holy, and life-creating Blood) of our Lord and God and Savior Jesus Christ, for the remission of all my sins and unto life everlasting. Amen.”
[^210]: The Communion Hymn is called in Greek (*koinonikon* [κοινωνικόν]), unto which “Alleluia” is appended.
[^211]: With us the priest takes the chalice and turns with it, while the deacon only exclaims: “With the fear of God.”
[^212]: When the priest turns toward the people, saying: “With the fear of God,” this symbolically signifies the appearance of Jesus Christ unto His disciples, whom He blesses. But when he turns for the last time, blessing the people with the holy chalice, this signifies His Ascension from the Mount of Olives.
[^213]: Table of Preparation (*Mensa propositionis* = Πρόθεσις = *Zhertvennyk*).
