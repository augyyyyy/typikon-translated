[^1359]: The surviving copy of the 1491 Krakow Horologion preserved in the National Library of Ukraine in Kyiv (formerly the Library of the Kyiv Golden-Domed St. Michael's Monastery, shelfmark GA-173 / Inv. I 143) concludes with physical leaf 582. Leaves 581 and 582 constitute the original rear flyleaf sheet (laid paper) bearing historical provenance, archival certification, and multiple layers of early modern reader marginalia and scribal pen trials.

[^1360]: Handwritten certification of collation inscribed on June 18, 1924, by Volodymyr Ivanovych Barvinok (1879–1943), prominent Ukrainian historian, bibliographer, and archivist of the All-Ukrainian Academy of Sciences (VUAN). In 1924 Barvinok published his landmark survey of early printed Cyrillic books in Kyiv collections (*Zahalnyi ohliad starodrukiv kyivskykh bibliotek*, in *Bibliolohichni Visti*, nos. 1–3), documenting this very copy. The recorded count of 296 leaves reflects the complete physical foliation of the codex (296 folios = 592 pages in two-page folio numbering, including front flyleaves, printed gathering signatures, and rear endpapers).

[^1361]: Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century pedagogical and cantoral pen trials (*proby pera*) executed in Ruthenian cursive (*skoropys*) and semi-uncial (*pivustav*). In early modern Ukrainian church and brotherhood schools, pupils and novice scribes frequently tested their newly trimmed goose quills, ink viscosity, and penmanship by writing the initial letters of the Cyrillic alphabet (*Ot, Az, Vedi, Glagol, Dobro*), followed by horizontal ruling and personal names.

[^1362]: Scribal pen trials containing personal nicknames and vernacular idioms (*Deidu Boisko kornu rok* and *Filipyk*). These informal vernacular jottings represent typical East Slavic scribal and cantoral marginalia recorded on blank endpapers of liturgical volumes during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

[^1363]: Pious invocations of the Most Holy Trinity (*Sviataia Troitsa* / *Sviaty Troitsa*) repeated across multiple lines beneath minim strokes (| | | | | | |). Practicing uniform vertical minim strokes followed by sacred trinitarian formulas served as the standard warm-up exercise for monastic scribes and liturgical cantors before copying sacred texts.

[^1364]: Faint, fragmentary semi-ustav and display ustav lettering visible beneath the cursive layers on the lower portion of leaf 581 recto. The reading *[vo]lnast budet* ('freedom will be') reflects a secular or student scribble, flanked by traces of sacred doxological inscriptions (*...slava...*).

[^1365]: Physical leaf 582 represents the final rear flyleaf verso of the codex, consisting of unprinted laid paper preserving sewing perforations and thread impressions from the historical monastic binding along the inner gutter. This leaf marks the completion of the 1491 Krakow Horologion facsimile witness (leaves 1–582).
